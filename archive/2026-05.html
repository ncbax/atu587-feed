The President's Report: The Battle is Never Over, WE ARE ONE!

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 28 · Open in the PDF

Internal Organizing

The combination of our membership growth and management's ramped-up application of discipline and termination requires that we develop solutions to keep up. This Union is committed to investing whatever it takes to meet the increased demands for representation and the increasing workload of its Officers and shop stewards.

Adding another Vice President in the next election cycle will help, but we won't stop there. The Union is working on plans to better utilize our E-board officers and shop stewards through training and meetings for shop stewards and chief shop stewards. Additionally, we are continuing to detail members to help support our Vice President's, as we've done consistently over the last year.

As a Union, we are stronger when we focus on being proactive rather than reactive. Along those lines, we formed a C.A.T., or Contract Action Team, for Metro Contract Negotiations. Our C.A.T Team continues to grow through weekly orientation meetings. Stay tuned for more info.

We plan to ramp up our organizing, educating, unifying, and strengthening this union. We need more shop stewards across all classifications and all properties that we represent. While we've been revamping and increasing our shop steward training, our Vice Presidents are committed to more.

We should plan to establish an orientation class about our union and unionism in general for our members who haven't been in a union before. The Union is us. It both requires and relies on all of us for its strength.

Intimidation & Retaliation

This vicious cycle is so difficult to break. There is little to no chance that bad managers will be held accountable without one or hopefully more of our members stepping forward. Retaliation is the age-old tool used to prevent that from happening. While the Union can't guarantee that you won't be retaliated against for reporting mistreatment from a manager, we 100% will stand beside and fight with you if and when you do come forward to report it.

Hats off to the courageous dozens of members in Bus Facilities who recently stepped forward with enough documentation to finally bring serious scrutiny to behavior that would result in any of our members being terminated.

"True North Values" will remain a truly meaningless slogan until ALL are held equally accountable at both Bus & Rail. ATU 587 leadership is committed to shining light on and stamping out management mistreatment of our members wherever it exists. It will take persistence and unity, as poor managers rely on spreading apathy and division.

Our Voice – Our Power!

Our union needs and appreciates anything that each member can contribute to its governance, unity, strength, and success. Whether it's attending a monthly meeting or an open bargaining session, signing up for COPE or the CAT team, becoming a shop steward, writing a news review article, or educating new members about our union, or unions in general.

Shout out to our COPE (Committee on Political Education) for their diligent interviews of the dozens of politicians who reached out for our Union's endorsement. Regardless of one's low opinion of politics, our Union endorsement is highly valued and sought. Our Union's decades of endorsing pro-transit, pro-union politicians have resulted in greater access, tangible help, and funding. Please consider signing up to contribute to COPE or increasing your donation. Your donations really make a difference, even if you just sign up for $1 per paycheck.

The 1st Vice President's Report: Kitsap Transit, Shop Steward Class, Organizing Bases and Arbitration Updates

By Cory Rigtrup · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

April was a drop-dead busy kind of month. I like to come in early, load up on caffeine and pound out as much work as I can, before the commerce of the day begins. However, I have been failing on many levels. In April we've been able to temporarily set up a new workstation and start staffing it with Board Officers to help with the workload. Optimistically, by mid-May, we'll have the logistics worked out, so we can become more effective. Any help is greatly appreciated.

Kitsap Transit:

In April, we were able to get our fourth operator fired this year, their job back. Anyone who is not from Kitsap, is likely not to know what's going on over there. Kitsap isn't just one of the places where if you can hit something with a bus, you probably will. Kitsap has been changing to enforce all kinds of work rules, both old and new. They also like to relive any moment a member does almost anything, and sharing the video viewing experience with members , lots of videos. Our Local has some incredible and rapidly experienced stewards, that are close to being overwhelmed, but do amazing work holding management accountable.

I took the Saturday night Kitsap meeting off to spend time with my wife. Monday, Greg reported that members are riled up and want to speak with the authority board. As luck would have it, the board was meeting next morning, so I jammed over there. Not my typical Cory kind of speech filled with aggression and absolution innuendoes, rather challenging the board as to what kind of employer do they want to be? The following two days I went to a Kitsap termination and suspension grievance. Stay tuned, we are in the thick of it, one side has to give and we are made of sterner stuff.

Shop Steward Class:

If the plan works out, May is going to be exciting! Our Local should be hosting Shop Steward classes at the Union office, also in Bremerton and Sequim. We are going full tilt on providing various classes that are designed to teach property specific stewards as our annual steward rosters have just changed.

This is a great time to become a shop steward. If you're a self-motivated kind of person who doesn't like to see people struggling, becoming a steward could be rewarding for you. If you're the kind of person who's got fire in the belly and energy to burn, becoming a steward is probably great for you. A steward is a position of service, a person who likes to help others. Often times, the people needing help aren't the most popular and don't look like you, but they really need help. Answer the call.

Organizing the bases:

There is some excitement mounting! The idea that our Local will focus on increasing organization with the bases, is spreading like fire. Now that it's May, you should see the new list of names posted on the Union boards. We are working on getting base meetings set up, where we can get as many stewards as possible on those lists together, so we can strategize best commutation methods and what a base's needs are.

In broad strokes, once we get everyone on the same page and communicating, we can assess a base's strengths and weaknesses. With that we can start to structure an action plan for representations and development of stewards. I am a big proponent of mentoring, getting burned, then learning from those mistakes to get better. Remember scares are cool in the steward world, as we learn more from losing than winning.

Parts of this plan are still conceptual at this point; all the help is well appreciated!

Arbitration updates:

If you look at page three of our News Review, you will see a column labeled "Arbitration Update." Last month, we had ten Arbitrations listed. Only the wrongful termination of Ryerson base operator Timbrook had an arbitration date scheduled. Today we have seven exciting arbitrations scheduled. All seven have hearings ranging from June through October, plus the two new ones were just approved in April.

What happened? Well, four arbitrations got resolved, settlements without holding the arbitration. We also added a one from February, Oda. From 2020–2025, we averaged two arbitrations held a year. Cleary that doesn't work. The system was broken; we just couldn't get enough hearings held to keep up with the workload. About a month ago, there was big meeting with our lawyers and the prosecuting attorney's office. I don't believe they broke bread, but they did get every arbitration on the books scheduled. This is great, some of these grievances are many years old. We need resolution.

The Maintenance Vice President's Report: Bus Facilities Maintenance and Bus Vehicle Maintenance

By Jeff Stambaugh · Page 5 · Open in the PDF

The Saga of Facilities maintenance continues. While the Pick was finally posted and conducted, the final posting was not what we had hoped to see, while the number of "Usually shelter maintenance positions" and "supplementals" were reduced, it appears that games are still being played to erode seniority and limit picked work out of South Facilities. TFD management now has an opportunity to update the route sheets to reflect the work that is being done and post the work for employees to pick in the fall. I would suggest that The Union and management put a joint committee together to work through this process.

The Union has also Filed an Unfair Labor Practice (ULP) against Metro for grievance retaliation. It has appears that TFD management has retaliated against our members for exercising their protected right to file grievance(s). We will let PERC decide.

Speaking of ULP, we are scheduled to meet with PERC in early May over the ULP filed for Bus VM issuing unexcused absences for Holiday work assignments. More to come.

After the VM bus pick, many of our members requested a change of hours to meet the work life balance we all strive to achieve. Most of these 15-to-30-minute requests were for Childcare or Carpool, and VM has been granting many of these types of requests for years. Not anymore apparently. First, I would like to thank the chiefs and superintendents who were able to accommodate their employee's needs, additionally I would like to thank the superintendents who took the time to sit down and listen to their employees needs and provide reasonable feedback on why they felt they could not meet the accommodation, while we may not agree on the ability to accommodate, we have a better understanding of why you denied the request. All that being said, I am a bit confused at the number of denials that were sent to the Union and the various information included (or not), some simply denied by the superintendent "Does Not Meet Business Needs," others with with chief approval, and a denial from the superintendent "Does Not Meet Business Needs".

Many of these denials were from CSC, which only added to my confusion, most of our members at CSC have routine work that they perform daily, and they usually know what that work is, and what is needed from them. While what they do every day greatly contributes to the day to day VM operations, no one in management has been able explain to me how a 15–30 minute schedule change "Does Not Meet the Business Needs," very few employees at CSC receive or provide turnover to the next shift, so I ask of senior leadership, "What business need does this not meet?" I always thought King County was a benevolent employer and could reasonably accommodate these types of requests to help achieve a greater work life balance. Maybe I need to rethink that?

One last thought, FIFA. The Union has been asking leadership for the past few months if OT will be used prior to FIFA to be prepared, in the various responses and through other conversations is appears that management has taken the position that if OT is limited then AC requests will also be limited and more employees will be at work and not requesting time off, I actually had one "manager" tell me "if we limit OT, they wont have AC to take off."

Way to boost morale!

The Recording Secretary's Report: Pride in Toledo!

By Latrelle Gibson · Pages 7–8 · Open in the PDF

I had the pleasure of attending the ATU International 2nd Annual Pride Caucus Conference April 26 – 29, in Toledo, Ohio.

On the date of arrival, we checked in, gathered our credentials and were invited to a tour of the TARTA (Toledo Area Rapid Transportation Authority) system. We were all bused to the main garage where TARTA's Local 697 President, Krissy Bofia, worked with management in putting together a welcome social for us. While at the worksite, we toured the yard, maintenance shop, and operator bull pin. After leaving the worksite, we were bused to TARTA's main transportation hub in the city center. I had the pleasure of being the only one on the bus and was able to have a great conversation with the operator and ask about their working conditions, management support with unruly passengers, and how they deal with unruly passengers. Once we arrived at the transit hub, we were given a tour of their facilities there. This is where the IVP and I grilled a manger on issuing discipline and when the operators are invited to provide input for their workspaces and what this looks like. I did make the comment that I wished we were being guided by an actual boots on the ground transit operators, and not management, but was happy that the Union President was able to help get this organized.

IVP Carly Allen welcomed all of us to Toledo and was happy to host and show us what Toledo has to offer. She also thanked the Pride Caucus Board for selecting Toledo to hold the conference, knowing other offers were made by other locals to host the caucus.

We heard from the ATU Local 113 Attorney, Simon Blackstone, who spoke about LGBTQIA+ rights in the workplace, and writing protections in the CBA for these rights.

Conference Day 1

ATU International Pride Caucus President, Michael Mackay opened the conference with a Land Acknowledgment. After the invocation, we learned the history of the labor movement and queer history from the yester queer group. We heard from the honorary speaker, Tammy Parish, who spoke on Women's history month and her being the first woman in the maintenance role at TARTA.

Mayor Wade Kapszukiewicz spoke about the lowering crime rate in Toledo, private companies not recognizing Unions in the 30's, Toledo's history of sit down strikes, Toledo being ahead of the curve when it came to LGBTQIA+ rights, being the first city in Ohio to offer medical benefits to domestic partners of city workers, and about Toledo not making time for the divisions and un-inclusiveness of their population.

Panelist and facilitator, Lillian Brents on how to be an ally:

Ways to take action as an ally:

  • Sit down and listen to the LGBTQIA+ community
  • Speak out against injustices, don't wait until it happens to you or someone you know
  • Try taking these actions back to your local
  • Listen and learn
  • Stop listening to respond
  • Allies showing up to support and learn, and those allies bringing more potential allies to these events to learn
  • Work on listening

How to be an ally:

  • Acknowledge your privilege
  • Listen actively
  • Educate yourself
  • Speak out on Anti-LGBTQIA+ statements and jokes
  • Negotiating for your ENTIRE unit

Why be an ally:

  • Promote equality and justice
  • Create a safer and more inclusive environment
  • Amplifying marginalized voices
  • Personal growth and learning
  • Building stronger Unions
  • Taking actions
  • Challenging bias and stereotypes
  • Creating a workplace that values diversity and inclusion

We also heard from Karima Howard who spoke on the struggles on the fight the LGBTQIA+ community faces. She spoke on her own experience with her granddaughter struggling with her sexuality and her not thinking anything of the behavior change, discounting this as normal teenage issues. Her granddaughter tried to commit suicide by taking pills because of this struggle, and Karima spoke to why it's important to address these issues early and not put them on the back burner, because you never know what someone is struggling with.

Other panelist spoke on how they stood up for their family members struggling with their sexuality, with the President of Local 697 Krissy Bofia, spoke about her mother losing custody of her and her siblings and the threat of being placed in foster care unless a family member would step up and take them in. The only family member who would step up was her aunt, who is gay, and this was a fight with the state due to her aunt being gay, however, luckily, the courts allowed for her aunt to take them in. Experiencing this firsthand and defending her aunt against homophobic comments made by her teacher has made Krissy an ally since she was a child.

Conference Day 2, last day

John Costa opened the conference with an opening speech and spoke about his brother who was closeted and didn't come out until he was 40yrs old.

TARTA CEO, Laura Koprowski, spoke on transit safety and advocating penalties that match the crimes committed against transit workers. Spoke on the funding short falls of reliance on property tax and legislating sales tax instead to fund transit and close these short falls.

We heard from City Council member, Nick Komires, who spoke on being the 2nd openly gay elected Council Member in Toledo. He spoke on laws passed under the first openly gay Council Member to protect gay rights. We heard from Carlos Mayorga, ATU local 758 Pride Caucus President, who shared slides on how to get a local Pride Caucus started, how to form a local Pride Caucus, the obstacles to getting this started, and recruiting and maintaining members.

And lastly, we heard from Brittany Anderson from Pride At work, who spoke on Union values, fighting the billionaire agenda, trans and non-binary 101, common workplace and Union challenges trans workers face, trans rights are labor rights, Trumps Executive order stripping collective bargaining rights from 1 million federal workers, the repeal or weakening of over 60 workplace safety regulations. We also participated in an interactive crowd participation exercise where we wrote our own 5 words of Union solidarity.

In closing, I want to thank the ATU 587 membership for supporting sending us to this Conference, as it was an honor to be able to attend the 2nd Annual Pride Caucus Conference. Moving forward, I plan on trying to attend all the Caucus Conferences, I do not mind paying my own way to immerse myself in the experience. I truly believe the experience is invaluable, and I'm able to learn the experience of others which will help me be a better advocate.

The Stories We Tell: Clearing the Mush at Metro (Part 1 of 2)

By Balaramdas Stern, Shop Steward, SLC Rep, Wellness Champion, Mentors Moving Metro – Bellevue Base · Page 11 · Open in the PDF

We work in a place where people are always reading…the route, the radio, people's tone of voice, the look on someone's face, and the silence that sometimes follows.

Sometimes this is where problems arise: less in what happened, and more in the stories we create in our minds about "what happened."

When we don't know why someone did something, our brains naturally try to fill in the blanks. This space, between what someone intended and what was received, has been called The Interpersonal Gap.

Usually, when we let our guesses and unverified assumptions run the show, we end up in what organizational psychologist Gervase Bushe calls interpersonal mush: the foggy, sticky, fear-fed mess where trust starts to break down and fester. The interpersonal mush erodes the kind of trust that is vital to an agency grounded in sustained partnership on behalf of all those we serve...including each other.

Here is what struck me when I first learned about this: the mush is not a malfunction, it's the normal default. It forms automatically every time human beings interact, because none of us can see inside another person's mind. The question is never whether mush will form. It always will. The question is whether we have a way to notice when we're doing this, and clear it.

For a long time, this was a blind spot for me. I wonder how many of us are in the same boat.

Here's an example. A new recruit in training finishes a rough pull-out. They see the Instructor lean toward a Supervisor and whisper while looking in their direction. The trainee's heart sinks, and their mind goes immediately to the worst case scenario: "They're talking about me and what I did wrong. I'm sure to get fired."

What the Instructor is actually thinking: "They held it together through a difficult start. We should give them a later report time tomorrow."

Same situation. Two completely different ideas about what's happening. I have been on both sides of this one.

Now picture a vehicle maintenance Lead walking through the bay multiple times in an hour. No greeting. No stop. Just that slow, thinking face we all know so well. The mechanic asks herself: "I'm sure they're watching me because they don't trust my work."

The Lead is actually buried in a parts delay, a schedule problem, and a vendor issue, trying to solve three fires with limited time and resources.

Same walk-by. Different story being fabricated.

Or consider a Superintendent who sends a vague email to a Chief at their base: "See me at 2:00 PM." That is all it takes. The rest of the afternoon becomes a full internal trial. "I'm being called in for bad news. Maybe they've already decided."

The Superintendent wants to talk about a mentoring opportunity. They think you'd be good at it. No one does their best work while imagining the worst case scenario. One added sentence…"It's a positive conversation, wanted to give you the heads up"…and the whole day changes. That is a leader who understands human nature and chooses to get ahead of the story before it starts. What if more of us did that for each other?

Here is the question that can be hard to sit with: how many times this month have you been the person someone else was building a story about, and you never knew it?

What if the worst version of you that lives in someone else's head was created on a day you don't even remember, and the story they made up about you isn't true?

That is what makes the Interpersonal Gap relevant in our work and home lives. It doesn't just happen to us, it happens by us. We are all doing this, every day, without realizing it. Every time we rush past someone without a greeting, make an expression we're unaware of, send a vague message, or sit in silence when interacting with others, someone else's story machine starts spinning.

A skeptic might say: "Sometimes the story IS right. Sometimes they ARE talking about you."

This may very well be true. However, even then, the conversation is how you find out. This possibility of resolution isn't available to anyone when we don't sincerely inquire.

The clearing conversation is simpler than you think. We can name what we saw. Name the story we started telling ourselves. Then, ask a clear question: "I noticed you and the Supervisor talking while looking my way. The story I started telling myself was that I was in trouble. Is this true or is it something else?"

One sentence, and the chaos in our minds lifts.

Our roles in transit are integral to communication with others. We rely on kind, respectful communications that fortify trust and sustain partnership. A missed signal, a rough tone, a vague message…these things spread faster than good news, especially if nobody checks out the stories they're making up about others, and then lives from that story as if it were the truth.

So here is an invitation: try it once this week (or more if you like!). Pick the smallest moment…someone's look, a flippant comment, their tone…and instead of being absolutely sure of your story and keeping it to yourself, or trying to make sense of it with others, ask those involved. See if your assumption is correct. I've found it takes courage to do that. It may even surprise you.

So here is what I find myself wondering:

What would happen if we brought that same character to the stories we tell ourselves about each other? What would it look like if we chose to ask instead of assume? And what might we discover about ourselves and each other that the silence was never going to reveal?

Next month in Part 2: What happens when the story isn't wrong. Why the conversation still matters. And what it takes to build the kind of trust that works for everyone.

One Union, One Voice, Unity Is Strength; Division Is Costly

By Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs, ATU 587/King County Labor Delegate · Pages 12–13 · Open in the PDF

Who Are We? Unity Across the black communities. In recent years, as a minority affairs executive Board ATU587 I have seen something troubling: growing division within communities that have long shared common struggles. These divisions are especially visible within Black communities, where questions of identity, history, and belonging are being debated in ways that can either strengthen us or pull us apart.

As a first-generation immigrant, I have personally experienced moments where my place within Black spaces was questioned. I have been told that because my family was not part of the history of slavery in the United States, I could not fully belong in Black caucuses or conversations. That perspective, while rooted in real historical pain, risks overlooking a broader truth.

Our histories may be different, but they are deeply connected. In the United States, the Black experience has been shaped by slavery, Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and the long fight for civil rights. This struggle produced powerful leaders like Martin Luther King Jr., who called for unity through nonviolence and justice, and Malcolm X, who emphasized dignity, self-determination, and global awareness of Black struggles.

At the same time, across Africa and the Caribbean, nations were fighting their own battles against colonial rule. Countries like Ghana, led by Kwame Nkrumah, gained independence in 1957, while others followed throughout the 1960s. Leaders such as Julius Nyerere guided their people through liberation movements that challenged exploitation and demanded self-governance.

These movements were not isolated. They were connected by a shared desire for freedom, dignity, and self-determination. Martin Luther King Jr. himself attended Ghana's independence celebration, recognizing that the fight for justice in America and the fight for independence in Africa were part of the same global struggle.

This idea is rooted in Pan-Africanism, a philosophy advanced by thinkers like W. E. B. Du Bois, who believed that people of African descent, no matter where they are in the world, share a linked destiny.

So why the division today?

Part of it comes from the need to protect history. For many African Americans, identity is tied to a specific and painful legacy that must never be erased or diluted. That concern is valid.

At the same time, immigrants from Africa and the Caribbean bring their own histories of colonization, displacement, and struggle. Being excluded from Black spaces can feel like a continuation of the very marginalization we are all trying to overcome.

When we focus only on what separates us, we weaken our collective voice. When we recognize what connects us, we strengthen it.

The leaders we often look to for guidance understood this clearly: Martin Luther King Jr. spoke of the "Beloved Community," a vision where justice and equality are achieved through unity across differences. Malcolm X, especially later in his life, expanded his view to see the struggle for Black liberation as part of a global human rights movement. Shirley Chisholm built coalitions across race, gender, and background because she understood that real change requires solidarity.

None of them argued for division. They argued for expanding the circle while still honoring the unique histories within it.

Unity does not mean ignoring differences. It means respecting them while recognizing that our futures are tied together.

In the workplace, in our unions, and in our communities, unity has practical power. It strengthens our ability to advocate for fair treatment, equal opportunity, and protection from discrimination. It ensures that no group is isolated or left behind. It builds the kind of collective strength that institutions cannot ignore.

Division, on the other hand, weakens our influence and allows inequality to persist.

So the question "Who are we?" is not meant to divide us. It is meant to remind us.

We are descendants of resilience. We are products of different histories, but shared struggles.

We are stronger together than we are apart.

The path forward is not about choosing one identity over another. It is about building bridges between them. Because in the end, unity is not just an idea. It is a strategy for justice.

Probationary Discharges and the Impact on Minority Workers

Article 4, Section 7 of our agreement states that employees who are discharged during their probationary period are not subject to the grievance and arbitration procedure. However, the language does provide one safeguard: upon request, an employee has the right to a termination review. This review must be requested within 15 days of notification, and Rail is required to schedule the review and respond to the Union in writing within a reasonable timeframe.

On paper, this may appear to strike a balance between management authority and employee rights. In practice, however, this language has had a disproportionate and concerning impact on minority employees, particularly within the training department.

As a representative in Minority Affairs, I have witnessed firsthand how this provision operates. It places probationary employees in a vulnerable position, where decisions about their future often rest on subjective evaluations rather than clear, consistent standards. Many members are released without being given a meaningful opportunity to improve, demonstrate their abilities, or fully learn the job.

There is a growing concern that this process has been inconsistently applied across training classes. Outcomes often vary depending on trainers, instructional approaches, and expectations. This inconsistency raises serious questions about fairness and equity, especially when patterns emerge showing that minority employees are disproportionately affected.

The situation also creates tension within Amalgamated Transit Union Local 587. The union represents both trainers and trainees, which can make accountability difficult and leave Minority Affairs caught in the middle. Despite this, it is our responsibility to speak up when we see patterns that undermine fairness and equal opportunity.

For many in our membership, particularly those of African descent and other underrepresented groups, the current process does not feel neutral. There is a growing perception that bias, whether intentional or not, is influencing outcomes. When individuals are judged as "not capable" without being given a fair chance to develop, it raises serious concerns about the integrity of the system.

The termination review process, while important, is limited in its effectiveness. It does not carry the same weight as a grievance or arbitration process, and in many cases, decisions are upheld without meaningful reconsideration. This leaves employees without a true path to challenge their discharge.

It is clear that this language needs to be revisited. We must ask whether it still serves the purpose of fairness, or whether it has become a tool that allows too much unchecked discretion. A system that gives management full control without strong accountability measures risks undermining trust across the workforce.

Engaging in the Political Process and The Role of ATU-COPE

I had the privilege of serving on the Committee on Political Education (COPE), the political action arm of the Amalgamated Transit Union. This committee plays a critical role in connecting transit workers to the political decisions that shape our daily lives.

COPE is more than just a committee. It is the collective voice of transit workers, ATU-COPE ensures that working people have a seat at the table at every level of government.

By law, union dues cannot be used to fund political campaigns. Instead, ATU-COPE is powered by voluntary contributions from members who believe in advancing pro-labor and pro-transit leadership. These contributions help support candidates who understand and respect the needs of frontline workers. But the work does not stop after Election Day. ATU-COPE continues to engage with elected officials to make sure our members' interests are represented in policy decisions.

Many of our members hold strong and diverse views about politics. Some are skeptical of politicians altogether, while others fall on different sides of the political spectrum. Regardless of where we stand individually, one thing is clear: political decisions directly affect our jobs, our safety, and our communities. Choosing to disengage does not remove the impact, it only removes our voice.

We have seen firsthand how political races, such as the recent King County government executive election, can create division within our union. Endorsements are not always easy, and they can bring strong opinions. However, the purpose of COPE is not to divide, but to ensure that our collective interests are considered when policies are written and implemented.

During my time on the committee, I had the responsibility, as part of Minority Affairs, to ask critical questions of candidates seeking endorsement. These questions focused on issues that matter deeply to our members and our communities.

These conversations are not symbolic. They are essential. They help us understand how those seeking power intend to use it, and whether their values align with fairness, justice, and the well-being of working people.

ATU-COPE makes endorsements as needed each year, always with the goal of protecting and advancing the interests of our members. Participation in this process is one of the strongest tools we have as union members.

Being part of COPE reinforced something important: whether we engage or not, politics will always shape our workplace and our future. Through COPE, we choose to engage, to ask questions, and to make sure our voices are heard.

Adding a Third Vice President: Questions of Representation and Responsibility

By Andy Price, ATU Local 587 Member · Page 14 · Open in the PDF

In the April edition of the ATU 587 News Review, President Greg Woodfill published a President's Report outlining the reasoning behind the proposal to add a third Vice President position to our Local. The membership voted to adopt that proposal during the March cycle of meetings. While that vote should be acknowledged, it is also appropriate to continue discussing the broader issues raised during the debate.

Union democracy does not end with a vote. Ongoing discussion about governance decisions—especially those involving long-term financial commitments—is a normal and healthy part of any democratic organization. Many of the concerns raised during the debate were not simply matters of opinion or preference, but questions about structure, cost, and long-term planning for the Local. Those questions remain relevant regardless of the outcome of any single vote.

Representation and Structure

One of the main arguments in favor of the new position is that two vice presidents were no longer sufficient to represent a membership of roughly 4,800 members. Yet the structure that was ultimately adopted raises its own questions.

The newly created position is largely justified on the basis of light rail operations along with several smaller properties. At present, light rail operations include roughly 200 operators, and even when including related classifications such as streetcar operations, the broader rail grouping still represents a relatively small portion of a membership that is primarily composed of bus operations. Whether creating a full-time executive officer position to represent that segment of the bargaining unit is the most efficient structure is a question many members raised during the discussion.

Another issue raised during the debate involves how representation and voting rights are structured. If implemented as approved, the position would be funded largely through dues paid by the entire membership—particularly the much larger group of Metro Transit bus operators—while those same members would not be eligible to run for or vote for that position.

Questions about representation, eligibility, and accountability are not unusual when unions adjust their governance structures. These are exactly the kinds of questions that thoughtful discussion can help clarify.

It is also worth noting that other large locals within the Amalgamated Transit Union have addressed representation challenges using different structural approaches. Some combine certain executive offices, while others utilize part-time vice presidents rather than expanding the number of full-time officers. These variations demonstrate that there are multiple ways to approach representation issues without necessarily increasing the number of full-time executive positions.

Financial Responsibility

The President's Report states that under any metric the Local can afford another Vice President. That conclusion deserves careful consideration.

The new position is expected to cost approximately $226,000 per year when salary, benefits, and associated expenses are taken into account. While the Local currently enjoys budget surpluses, the concern raised by many members was not whether the Local can afford the position today, but whether it represents a prudent long-term commitment.

Public transit funding and agency budgets are influenced by economic cycles and political decisions beyond our control. Recent discussions about potential federal transit funding reductions illustrate exactly why uncertainty should be part of any long-term planning conversation.

Financial decisions made during periods of surplus can become more difficult if economic conditions change. Once a full-time officer position is created, eliminating it later becomes politically and organizationally challenging. That is why questions about long-term financial commitments deserve thoughtful consideration before new permanent positions are added.

Process and Alternatives

During the debate, some members suggested alternative approaches to strengthening representation without immediately creating another full-time executive position. These included expanded use of detailed positions, rotating assistant roles, and other structural adjustments that could help support the workload of the Vice Presidents.

It is encouraging that the President's Report indicates the Local is exploring additional support structures such as rotating assistant-to-the-vice-president positions. That suggests there may be multiple ways to improve representation and support the work of union officers.

Exploring these kinds of structural improvements may offer opportunities to strengthen representation while maintaining flexibility in how the Local allocates its resources.

Looking Forward

The bylaw change has not yet taken effect. As noted in the President's Report, it must still be reviewed by the International to determine whether it aligns with the International Constitution and General Laws. That review process exists to ensure that local bylaw changes remain consistent with the broader governance structure of our union.

Regardless of the outcome of that review, the broader questions raised during this discussion remain important: how our Local balances representation, financial stewardship, and long-term planning.

Strong unions benefit from open discussion about these issues. Continued thoughtful dialogue helps ensure that our Local remains accountable to its membership and prepared for whatever challenges the future may bring.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 5

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
