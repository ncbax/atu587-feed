The President's Report: Still No Contract?

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 24 · Open in the PDF

This is the question Union officers get daily. Members are frustrated and can't understand why it always takes 12–18 months to negotiate a new contract.

The answer is we'll have a contract when Metro offers one that is worth the membership voting on.

We could have a tentative agreement tomorrow if we just agreed to a paltry raise, restrictions on overtime, and watering down of just cause. We will keep fighting for what the members have asked for.

We'll present a contract to vote on when it's right, and not one day sooner.

Benefits Bargaining Update

Every two years, we renegotiate our medical benefits, separate from contract bargaining.

The County's projections on medical cost increases for the next two years, on top of the last two years' cost increases, paint a picture of the sky falling, as they always do.

These County projections show that our current $16 million surplus in the medical benefits reserve fund would turn into a $13 million deficit if the County's monthly contribution were to remain at its current level.

Contractually, if at any point the reserve fund falls below $4 million, we are required to meet with the County and negotiate replenishing the fund.

I'll leave it to your imagination who the County proposes to cover the vast majority of this projected $17 million shortfall, and what unacceptable funding mechanism they propose to use to cover it. The union's position is that the employer should fully cover the cost of our medical benefits, PERIOD! Buckle up and stay tuned; this could get ugly.

Hiring Bonus Update

On 8/14/2023 the Union and King County agreed to a recruitment/retention bonus which was added to the last contract on Page 245.

The MOA stated that these bonuses were for those hired before October 31st, 2025. Recently King County OLR contacted the Union, stating that they had mistakenly continued paying these bonuses past 10/31 to around 50 members and would need to recollect those payments due to not having the budgeting authority to give those bonuses. Additionally, they stated that they would not be paying others who weren't paid these bonuses.

The Union objected, and King County won't recollect while they are talking with the Union on this. Not paying a recruitment/retention bonus that was listed on a job recruitment feels like false advertising, breach of contract, and wage theft all rolled into one. These members should be paid these bonuses.

Ultimately, it's The County that will decide whether to pay these bonuses or not. Once they make their decision, we'll take it from there. Do the right thing King County, your word is your bond, keep your promises! True North Values shouldn't just be a slogan.

Non-domicile CDL Update

As most of you know some of our members you reside and work here legally are losing their CDL's. Imagine waking up one day and being told for no fault of your own that the Federal Government has made a rule to revoke your right to your job.

We battle daily with King County Metro Bus and Rail on contract violations, discipline & terminations without just cause. Or not holding managers to the same accountability standards as their front-line workforce.

But we still do find common ground and work together on mutual interests. We won't hesitate to call things out but should and will give credit where credit is due.

The County has gone above and beyond in dealing with the non-domicile CDL issue by offering temporary employment to those who are affected by this issue. But these aren't long term solutions, and they don't make our members whole or wipe away the indignity and injustice.

A Washington State Supreme Court Justice during their COPE endorsement interview with our Union suggested that Washington State could create their own CDL and gave the following similar examples.

Washington State Created L&I because OSHA was not doing enough to protect our citizens in terms of safety standards, wage protections, adequate breaks, etc.

Washington State created WAFSA because FAFSA was not offering aid to non-domicile students and children of non-domicile parents (Dreamers).

Washington State could, in theory, create a division of WDOL that oversees and regulates WA State Intrastate CDL License requirements.

Essentially saying that the Federal Law is not doing enough to provide for the needs of our state residents and we need to write laws that govern our state that are more specific than the Federal Laws.

Our COPE committee has engaged with State House and Senate members who are committed to try and write and pass legislation that could solve this injustice. The Governors office reached out to me and is very interested in this possibility. All of this is happening solely because of the political clout this Union has cultivated over decades of actively endorsing candidates. Please contribute to COPE, your contributions make a real and tangible difference. Our Voice Our Power!

The 1st Vice President's Report: Benefits

By Cory Rigtrup · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

If you think back to what originally drew you to apply for this job, maybe things haven't changed that much? Unless you expected the transit world to be full of glamour and glitz, the core attractions remain mostly intact. I suspect most of us came here for reliable work, good benefits and the steady incomes that go hand in hand with a strong unionized job. This article is about our benefits and the fight to keep them attractive.

We have five different bargaining units in our Local. Four properties are in the public sector, while the other is private. All five offer different variations of benefit packages. Four out of the five properties offer a variation of a Kaizer plan, along with a PPO plan. Three out of the five buy benefits from Washington state's exchange. The remaining two, contract benefit independently through a competitive bid process. Yes, it's a bit confusing, so bear with me as I try and explain how this all works.

When our Local negotiates the next contract, our future benefits are part of that. Often, benefits don't get the attention that wages and working conditions get when members are deciding to accept, or reject an offer, but we should. We are in a time where medical benefits cost are increasing nationwide at levels not seen in 15 years.

During open enrollment, members are allowed to select the next year's benefits for their families. I suspect these decisions are made on what works best for them and what they can afford. For my family, I chose the PPO plan. My wife is a Swedish SEIU hospital worker and my daughter is an out of state student. In my case only the PPO offers coverage for both, and I pay additional money for my choice. By choosing the PPO, it not only comes at a cost to me, but also to everyone in the bargaining unit.

Unless you're working at SGT, the taxpayers of Washington state are paying the vast majority of your benefits. If you've paid attention to what's been happening with tax monies in the last year, less federal dollars are going to states for medical costs the before the Affordable Care Act was passed into law. Washington state, like most states, are trying to find ways to compensate for the loss, while medical providers are increasing their cost, trying to remain solvent. For folks like us with medical insurance, the cost has increased.

Currently, we are bargaining the benefit packages with King County for years 2027 and 2028. The benefit packages are not purchased through the state's exchange, but rather independently offered through a provider. This allows for custom modification to plans, and the group rate charges are based on the expenses of our bargaining unit, not the larger group like the state. Simply put, whatever the cost of benefits we use, is what is charged to King County.

Last year for 2025, we had 4,511 members enrolled in benefits for all of Metro, Rail and Streetcar. The total benefits King County paid for 2025, was $93,516,849. If you look at our agreement, or a paycheck advice from last year, you will see the funding rate was $1,694 per month. If you're good at basic math, take monthly investment, $1,694, multiply it by 12 months, multiply it by 4,511 members, we get $91,699,608. If we take the $91,699,608 and subtract it by the $93,516,849 we used in benefits, we come up deficit of $1,817,241.

For 2026, our funding rate increased by 4% to $1,762. Keep in mind our local bargained for this increase in the summer of 2024. Well before knowing what would happen with the federal funding changes in 2025. It is likely that our 2026 benefit cost will increase more than 4% causing an even bigger deficit.

So here we are today, bargaining for the next couple of years of benefits. Medical costs are up, we either need more funding or to lower our average cost per member to stabilize our benefits. King County's proposals are for us to pay for the increases, ours is for them to pay the increase. Every fiber in my body revolts against us paying, but we need benefits and have to come to a deal before open enrollment. So, we need to get cheaper and drive the average cost per member down.

In 2018, when I first started attending medical bargaining, our Local was the most expensive group in King County for medical. We had the highest average cost per member and incurred the highest deductible for using our benefits. Fast forward to today, we are the cheapest cost per member and don't pay any premium share like our bosses. This did not happen by accident, rather a partnership between members getting healthier and a successful bargaining strategy. Folks who do not use medical don't pay, and incentives members to choose the lower cost medical plan, Kaizer. Year after year, Kaizer is between $6,000 and $3,000 a year cheaper per member, than the PPO. Over the years, we have seen enrollment increase in the Kaizer plan, and a slow reduction in members in the PPO plan. As a result, our medical costs grew slower than all the other groups in King County.

Getting a deal without us paying is one hell of a challenge, the struggle is what makes us strong! This is where I stop, bargaining continues and I won't put in writing the union's strategy.

In Solidarity, Cory Rigtrup

The Maintenance Vice President's Report: Change of Hours Request, Heavy Handed Discipline, and Holiday ULP

By Jeff Stambaugh · Page 5 · Open in the PDF

Change of hours request

As I reported last month Metro Bus VM has made the decision to honor very few change of hour's request, while most of the denials seemed unjustified without any clear reason, we did have an in-depth discussion at LMRC about the issue. We spoke of a few specific denials, management had a different perspective on what prompted the request, and why it was denied vs my conversation with the requesting employees. In short management claims it's too difficult to manage a crew that comes in at different times.

As a Lead Mechanic myself I recognize those challenges, however, I was always able to get the crew to work despite the varying start times. It seems Metro management wants to continue to drive down morale and continue to exert authority rather than uphold the True North Values they claim to have. I was promised a more in-depth review of the denials, but I have yet to get it.

Heavy handed discipline

Both Bus VM and Bus Facilities continue to issue heavy handed discipline and fail to follow provision in the contract that says, "discipline is subject to just cause and that the intent of discipline is corrective, rather than punitive, in nature."

Part of that discipline is based off of the use of AVL data that The Union believes Metro is using in violation of the MOA, more to come.

Holiday ULP

Metro requested summary judgment to dismiss the ULP filed with PERC. PERC has denied the motion, we now move closer to PERC hearing, and the membership has approved an arbitration request over the issuance of an unexcused absence where the member called in and reported his unavailability 63 minutes prior to his shift. The Union has had discussions at the bargaining table with Metro in an attempt to resolve this, at this point Metro and The Union have been unable to come up with an agreeable solution, we make look to PERC and an arbitrator to help out if we can't get there.

On a positive note, The Union was able to work a deal with Rail and was successful in returning a Rail Maintenance employee back to work after being terminated. The employee and The Union would like to thank Rail management for being able to work this out.

Getting Prepared for the BIG DAY, Pick:

It's time to start thinking about your Fall plans and what you will pick for the Fall/Winter Shakeup.

This will be the LONG shakeup, so this pick will be the pick to really make sure you're picking the right schedule, route, or base that you really want.

Come prepared with the work you desire on paper so you can easily scratch off what's been picked and move down your list. If you plan on picking O.T trippers, PLEASE, make sure the work you want to pick will not create an illegal pick due to not having the required 8 hours off or time in between assignments. Prior to your pick date, it's recommended to research all the work you're interested in picking and lining this up with the O.T trippers you would want to ensure it will be a legal pick, this would include making sure you have at least a 5 minute buffer time between the off at the base time and sign in time of the O.T tripper of vice versa. Be sure to write these combinations down so you can have an easy reference in the pick room that way, you won't burn up your 20min of preview time trying to piece this all together.

If you pick via Absentee, please follow the same process to help us ensure that you're getting what you want and that it won't create an illegal block.

Good Investigations Identify Causes—Shortcuts Identify Culprits

By Andy Price, Union Member · Page 9 · Open in the PDF

Missed stops are not new. What appears to be new is the growing tendency to treat every missed-stop complaint as though the only question that matters is whether somebody can later be identified on a video.

That should concern every operator.

Many operators have noticed an increasing reliance on surveillance footage and a growing tendency to reduce investigations to a single question:

"Was someone standing there?"

If the answer is yes, the conclusion increasingly seems to be that the operator must have done something wrong.

But transit is not that simple.

A person visible on a paused video image reviewed later in an office is not necessarily visible to an operator approaching in real time while monitoring traffic, pedestrians, cyclists, mirrors, signals, and roadway hazards.

You cannot stop for what you cannot reasonably see.

Many Eastside operators know exactly what we're talking about. Some bus zones are hidden behind hedges. Some stop poles are placed far back on private-property setbacks. Some are poorly illuminated. Some are so rarely used that the only reason operators know they exist is because years of experience have taught them where they are.

If a stop can disappear behind a hedge, then the problem is bigger than the operator.

Operators are increasingly finding themselves disciplined for passing up passengers who made little or no effort to indicate they wanted service. Standing motionless in dark clothing, several feet back from the curb, looking at a phone, and giving no visible indication of intent may be enough for a surveillance camera to identify someone afterward. That does not necessarily mean the person was reasonably visible to an operator approaching in darkness.

Some passengers quite literally stand like statues in the dark and then, after the bus has gone by, become clearly visible on a paused video image viewed later in an office.

Management points to the video and says, "See, they were there."

But that is exactly the problem.

Article 3.8 was negotiated because surveillance footage was never intended to become the basis for routine minor discipline. Policy 15 exists because customer complaints are supposed to be investigated, not reduced to a freeze frame and a predetermined conclusion.

The increasing dependence on video may actually be narrowing management's vision rather than improving it.

Technology should assist investigations, not replace them.

Metro already has a customer complaint policy. Policy 15 exists for a reason. Complaints are supposed to be investigated. Customers are supposed to be contacted. Circumstances are supposed to be considered. The goal should be to understand why something happened and how to prevent it from happening again.

Instead, many operators are concerned that investigations increasingly begin and end with video review.

Ironically, this growing dependence on surveillance can undermine protections intended for both customers and operators. In the process of showing videos to operators, the identities of complaining customers can effectively become known, even though the complaint process was designed to protect those individuals.

Nobody benefits when policies designed to protect everyone are gradually ignored.

Across the transit industry, agencies recognize that visibility is a shared responsibility.

Island Transit instructs riders to stand where they can be seen, signal approaching buses, and even use flashlights during darkness or fog. Kitsap Transit encourages riders to wave, so operators know they intend to board.

Around the world, passengers are expected to make their intentions known. Anyone who has used transit systems in places like London understands this. Simply standing near a bus stop without making your intentions obvious can easily result in being passed by. That isn't poor service. It's common sense.

King County Metro already recognizes this principle in some locations. Passenger-activated flashing lights already exist at certain stops to alert approaching operators that someone is waiting. If these systems improve safety and reliability at some locations, why not expand them?

Why not trim vegetation more aggressively?

Why not relocate hidden stops?

Why not move stop poles to locations visible from the operator's seat?

Why not review locations generating repeated complaints?

Why not focus on prevention instead of discipline?

Assigning blame is easy.

Solving problems is harder.

Operators accept responsibility for doing our jobs professionally. We always have.

Management should accept responsibility for the system it creates.

Missed stops are not simply operator problems.

They are system problems.

And system problems require system solutions.

Operators are not asking for special treatment.

We are asking management to follow the contract, follow its own policies, and recognize that missed stops are rarely caused by one factor alone.

Good investigations identify causes.

Shortcuts identify culprits.

Those are not the same thing.

Stranger Than Fiction: When Safety Policy Starts Writing Its Own Story

By Michael Mackay, Transit Operator Rep #1 · Pages 12–13 · Open in the PDF

I have spent enough time inside this system to know what it is supposed to be: precise, accountable, and fair. That is the foundation accident preventability was built on, grounded in National Safety Council standards and reinforced through decades of policy meant to correct behavior, not punish it. But what I have been seeing lately does not always match that promise. At times, it feels like the narrative is no longer guided by facts, but by something else entirely, something closer to fiction than reality.

Nearly twenty years ago, when I was operating out of North Base, I was accused of striking a truck while merging onto I 5 in the early morning hours. By midday, the decision had already been made preventable. Case closed.

But it was not closed.

With guidance from a seasoned shop steward, Neal Safron, I returned to the scene with a Polaroid camera and a measuring tape. I was not there to argue. I was there to document. What I found told a different story. The damage on the bus sat nearly three feet higher than where the truck's mirror could have made contact. Even after factoring in tire height variations for that vehicle model, the conclusion became unavoidable: the narrative did not hold up.

That ruling was overturned.

That experience stayed with me because it proved something essential. The system works when facts are allowed to lead. It also showed how quickly a conclusion can be written before the facts are fully understood.

In my current role overseeing accident rereads and arbitrations for the Union, I am seeing a troubling pattern. Since stepping into this position, I was informed that we have experienced an 84 percent turnover rate. That level of turnover does not happen in isolation. It raises a necessary question: are we applying policy in a way that aligns with its intent? Too often, the answer appears to be no.

I see small errors turning into major consequences. Preventable designations are issued without full context. Point totals escalate in ways that feel mechanical rather than deliberate. With the matrix, fifteen points become twenty four, and suddenly a career is on the line.

I have seen tires at the end of their lifecycle unzip at worn seams and be labeled preventable. I have seen coaches, physically unable to move, still deemed preventable when another vehicle struck them. I have seen inconsistencies in accident probation, where lower seniority employees are granted second chances while higher seniority operators with decades of service are not.

And then there is the math.

You are told this system is objective, built on clear thresholds and consistent application. But when a Safety Quality report explicitly states, "no visible injury," and two points for visible injury are still added, the numbers begin to tell a different story. When a 15,000 dollar vehicle damage claim is treated as if it crosses a 28,000 dollar threshold, triggering additional points, the calculation no longer reflects reality.

Somehow, two plus two becomes eight.

At that point, it is no longer just a miscalculation. It is a narrative being imposed on the facts rather than drawn from them.

In Stranger Than Fiction, the main character begins to hear a voice narrating his life, dictating outcomes before they happen. He initially assumes the system around him is fixed and inevitable. As the story unfolds, it becomes clear that the narrative itself can be challenged, and that it is not beyond correction.

That is where you are now.

You are told the system is straightforward: resolve issues at the lowest level, keep safety and discipline separate, keep safety and base chiefs separate, and focus on correction rather than punishment. On paper, that looks simple. In practice, that simplicity often breaks down. Outcomes drift. Decisions lose connection to context. The line between what happened and how it is recorded starts to blur.

There are moments when the system corrects itself. I served as the partisan arbitrator in the Ben DeMonte arbitration last year, where termination was pursued despite the absence of a fatality or external claim. The arbitrator rejected that approach and reinforced a key principle: the most severe discipline must be reserved for the most severe circumstances. The result was reinstatement with back pay.

That decision made something clear. The system is not beyond repair, but it does not correct itself automatically.

We now have to decide what kind of system we are operating in. We can continue to rely on a framework that is sound on paper but inconsistently applied, or we can insist that its principles are followed in practice.

We know what Metro's policy says. It calls for fairness, accuracy, and proportionality. It is designed to correct behavior, not to produce outcomes that feel disconnected from reality.

But when similar cases produce different results, when calculations become flexible, and when context is overlooked, confidence in the system erodes. People stop believing that decisions are anchored in facts. They start feeling like characters in someone else's story, waiting for a conclusion that has already been written.

Like any story, this one is still being written. If we are not paying attention, it can drift into something unrecognizable, structured, documented, and official, yet just a little too strange to be true.

But it can also be corrected.

That requires attention to detail, a willingness to question outcomes, and a commitment to ensuring that decisions reflect facts, not assumptions. It requires us to insist that accident preventability remain what it was meant to be: precise, accountable, and fair.

Because in transit, as in any system built on trust, accuracy is not optional. Fairness is not negotiable. And the line between reality and interpretation must remain clear.

Take the Wheel: Our Stories Are Our Power

By Chuck Lare, a Shop Steward & Safety Committee Chair · Page 14 · Open in the PDF

You already know what it feels like when the road is against you — a brutal schedule, a behavioral health crisis at your stop, and backup that shows up late or not at all. What you might not fully feel yet is just how much power you actually hold.

It starts with a story.

On May 29th, Metro's Refugees & Immigrants Support & Empowerment (RISE) affinity group and Equity, Inclusion and Belonging (EIB) team gathered operators for "The Stories That Shape Us." An operator described her father's Vietnam service and gave us a name that fits: Bus Pilots. A Samoan brother got the whole room clapping with two simple hand motions. A Hawaiian operator couple explained what it truly means to be a chief. Different routes, different backgrounds — one coach.

That is not sentiment. That is strategy.

Cohesion Is a Safety Tool

When operators know each other's stories, they move differently. They cover each other. They communicate. The bus ahead of you is not just your leader, the bus behind you is not just your follower, we are partners. When you work in unison with your leader and your follower, you stop being commanded by a bad schedule and start controlling your own trip.

That is not a small thing. That is your livelihood.

The more cohesion we build through shared stories and mutual trust, the more authority we earn over our own working conditions — not handed down from management but generated organically from the floor up.

What We're Demanding Right Now

Your Base Safety and Security Committee reps are not venting. They have come together to advocate for specific, concrete changes:

  • Visible security at high-incident stops — site-specific deployment, not vague reassurances. Metro Transit Police need to operate as a full-service model. Coverage gaps need to be mapped and closed.
  • Chronic offender accountability — real removal mechanisms and real-time exclusion information shared with you, the operator, not delivered after an incident.
  • Real crisis response — expanded CARE teams and KC Outreach Group behavioral health coverage during evening and overnight hours. You should not be alone at 2 a.m. with zero backup.
  • Regional coordination — unified data infrastructure and cross-agency partnerships so a crisis on your route does not vanish into a gap between three agencies that do not talk to each other.

These are not policy wishes. These are the distance between what you face every shift and the support that should be there.

The KC Transit Safety Regional Task Force and the Contract Action Team are the vehicles we use to push these demands beyond the individual trip level. Get connected. Show up.

The Story We Want to End With

There is an operator operating for the longest time who picked off that route. On his new assignment, he received five rider accommodations almost immediately. He will tell you why: his attitude changed when his environment changed.

When operators are supported, riders feel it. Service improves. The job becomes something worth doing again.

The story we are working toward is the day that operator — and every operator running a hard route — can bring that same renewed spirit back with them, because the environment finally earned it.

That is the finish line. Tell your story. Know your leader. Cover your follower. Show up for each other.

The narrative belongs to us.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 5

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
