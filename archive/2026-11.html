The President's Report: Broken Promises Have Lasting Consequences

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 28 · Open in the PDF

For True North Values to be taken seriously as anything other than a slogan or lip service, they must include honoring your written and verbal agreements, keeping your promises, and following the law. Examples of the opposite of True North Values would include wage theft or false advertising in job recruitment, both of which would destroy trust and render your words and commitments meaningless going forward.

On October 22, 2026, I issued a Union Bulletin explaining that the County had publicly advertised a $3,000 hiring bonus during multiple operator hiring recruitments after this contractual bonus provision expired on November 1, 2025.

On April 6th 2026, the County informed The Union that it had offered the bonuses in error. Management maintains that those who made the offers were not authorized to do so.

The bonus was payable in two installments: $1,000 upon graduation from training and $2,000 upon completion of probation. We understand that, among those hired or placed in training after November 1, 2025, only 53 members received the $1,000 payment. To our knowledge, none received the 2nd installment of $2,000 for completing probation.

Our Union lawyers firmly believe the County has a binding obligation to pay these hiring bonuses and that the law requires it to honor that obligation. Our Lawyers advised us that our Union does not have the authority to bargain an agreement that would result in any of these bonuses not being paid, since the County owes these new hires a binding obligation.

From day one, we have told the County that they must honor these commitments, and the Union could not and would not agree to the County not paying any of these promised bonuses.

The Union strongly disagrees with characterizing these bonuses as a "gift of public funds." These bonuses are part of the pay owed to our new colleagues.

We have waited since April 6th for the County to state unequivocally whether it would pay these promised bonuses. Yesterday we finally got that answer. The County notified the Union that on 10/23 it would send out 53 $1,000 collection letters. Shame on the County! King County should do the right thing and live up to its often-stated County Values, not treat them like disingenuous slogans. The Union is exploring all options going forward; stay tuned for more info.

Benefits Bargaining Update/Review

As of 10/26, benefits bargaining is still ongoing, but we could possibly reach a TA on our health benefits before you read this article. Depending on what's in the TA, our ATU International Constitution and General Laws may require a membership vote, which we haven't done for benefits up to now.

If we reach a benefits TA with the County that requires a membership vote, we will hold in-person and Zoom informational meetings with Q&A sessions, along with worksite tabling sessions. We will announce dates and times for all sessions that give members enough time, information, and opportunities to ask questions to allow an informed decision. As a democratic union, we will respect and carry out the membership's decision by ballot.

Contract Bargaining Update

We continue to meet regularly, making slow, incremental progress on less contentious issues before turning to bargaining, mediation, or arbitration on the major unresolved issues. Bargaining is open, so members can attend to observe and learn about our progress. I will also give verbal updates and answer questions at all Union meetings and whenever I find opportunities to gather a few members for updates. We will not publish contract updates that reveal our strategies or bottom lines to management.

Please don't believe or spread wild rumors that the Union has agreed to, or will agree to, major concessions. That's not true and could divide the Union at a time when unity matters.

If or when the County stalls, attempts to impasse negotiations, or bargains in bad faith, we will respond accordingly. Regardless of how it appears, we are not there yet for either, and we will not risk our union's credibility or integrity by falsely representing how bargaining is going for political expediency.

Happy Thanksgiving! November 26th

The 1st Vice President's Report: Pay and Discipline Issues on the Rise!

By Cory Rigtrup · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

For all those that don't know, I am dyslexic. Prolonged periods of reading and writing exhaust me. That was part of the reason I did not submit a report last month. I really have no valid excuse.

September was a rocking month, followed by October which did not disappoint. The days were long and action packed with intimate member issues. I've watched through the years, the Vice-President duties focus increasingly on being the champion of individual members' issues, then from a leadership position to the masses. Right or wrong, that's where we are today.

Kitsap Transit

Kitsap has consumed large volumes of time. Pay and discipline issues have been off the charts.

Pay: In the beginning of year, Kitsap switched payroll providers to UGK. Goodbye to the days when members were able to view their payroll advice. The new system required an app that didn't work for the vast majority of members. At the same time, some internal processes changed that created issues when pretty much everyone couldn't tell if their pay was correct or not? Recently, we were able to get Kitsap to build a process so members can get a printed copy of their bi-weekly payroll. If you are a Kitsap member and would like a printed copy of your payroll, contact Kitsap payroll and make the request.

Discipline: Wow, Kitsap is burning. Year to date, we've had 11 vested Operators get terminated. This number includes our current Union's Kitsap Executive Board Officer. For perspective, that is 1000% more this year, than the comparable sized Rail Operation has terminated. Kitsap has been exceptionally ruthless to our Access operators. The struggles are real. It's hard not to notice a link from last year's retired Operation manager to the punitive tyranny of today's leadership. I want to give props to all our union reps who are fighting these injustices. We will fight all injustices and we will not rest!

Timbrook Arb

In September we rocked and rolled the Phil Timbrook arbitration. For two days, the full display of how isolated and deplorable the conditions can be for Operators working after midnight, was righteously presented to an independent party. I was watching the arbitrator's face as she viewed the video for the first time. Clearly, the obscene behaviors of the intoxicated rider left a mark.

A Pair of sixes: Timbrook's incident happened in the University district six weeks prior to Shawn Yim's murder. His employment was terminated 6 weeks after the murder.

During the arbitration, we present 19 comparables of other Operators, who at some point in their career, felt the need to touch an unruly passenger. These comparables all happened in the last five years. Only two resulted in termination of employment, that was until Shawn was murdered.

In Addition to Timbrook, the memberships have approved similar terminations for Kassie and Snyder. All three of these don't come close to the behaviors deemed irredeemable just three years ago.

Closing briefs for Timbrook were submitted just before Halloween. We expect a decision/award by year's end.

A friendly reminder to all, please, don't touch the passengers.

Medical

I wrote this article in mid-October. I suspect we've just reached an agreement on medical benefits for the next two years. If we haven't, I'm lost. I spend a lot of time asking questions and running angels through my head before speaking. Even then, sometimes the words don't come out right.

The medical benefit world has changed and so has the game of benefits bargaining. Next year's Washington state open medical market premiums are increasing by 22%. Our plans forecast are not that dyer, but the projected increase are far more than we have ever gotten, and our reserves won't cover the gap.

In 2013, medical benefits we also on the rise, not quite as bad as today, but it still sucked. At the time, we were part of bigger country-wide group, and the union's leadership decided to chart our own course. We choose arbitration. Long story short, we lost.

The arbitrator ruled that members had to pick up some of the rising medical costs. For those that recall a time when we did "Gold, Silver, Bronze" tiered benefits, that was part of the outcome. A lesser-known part decision was to establish a minimum dollar reserve. That minimum was set at 4- Million dollars, and if the number drops below that, we are required to negotiate concessions to keep reserve stable. Fast forward today. We started this year with over 16 million, updated in July to 10-million, and shrinking fast like a keg of beer at a frat party.

If an agreement is reached, it will require a vote. Be educated and draw your own conclusions. The federal government is projecting 15 million Americans will be losing their medical coverage next year due rising cost. Keep developing your critical thinking skills.

Metro's New Zonar Inspection System Raises Safety and Accountability Questions

By Tzur Wilfand, #20149 East Base · Page 8 · Open in the PDF

On Sunday, September 20, 2026, I attended King County Metro's Zonar training. The electronic inspection system may improve documentation, but the training left me with serious questions about employee safety, inspection time, road relief and operator responsibility.

Under the new procedure, operators use a tablet to perform inspections, scan inspection points, report defects and photograph damage. Metro's own Zonar training material warns operators to "BE AWARE OF MOVEMENT IN THE YARD."

That warning deserves attention because operators may have to walk around a coach while using the tablet. To photograph damage high on a bus, an operator could need to move 30 to 50 feet away to obtain a clear picture potentially placing the employee in or near an active traffic lane while buses are moving through the yard.

Does Zonar Conflict with Metro's Existing Safety Message?

Metro has long recognized the danger of electronic-device distraction in a bus yard. Metro's The Manual, Section 10.06, identifies PED use while walking in the base yard as a hazard and prohibits employees from walking while using a personal electronic device.

Zonar is an agency-issued device, not necessarily a "personal" device covered by that prohibition. But the safety hazard is similar: an employee looking at a screen may have less attention available for a moving 40- or 60-foot bus.

That raises a basic question: If looking at an electronic device while walking through the yard is considered dangerous, how will Metro safely require operators to walk around buses while using Zonar?

Metro should consider a designated inspection area separated from normal bus traffic, where operators can safely inspect and photograph their coaches without standing in an active traffic lane.

Where Is the Additional Inspection Time?

Zonar also adds steps. Operators must access the tablet, log in, complete the electronic inspection, document defects, take photographs when necessary and submit the report.

Will Metro provide sufficient paid time to complete the entire process safely? What happens when Wi-Fi is unavailable, the tablet freezes or an operator cannot log in? Metro's Zonar instructions tell operators to contact the Transit Control Center if the tablet fails. But the policy should also explain whether operators receive additional time and whether a technology-related late departure can result in discipline.

What About Road-Relief Operators?

An even bigger question involves operators who take over buses already in service. Under current practice, road-relief operators are not required to complete the same full Zonar inspection.

Federal regulations make the underlying safety responsibility important. 49 CFR §396.13 says that before driving, a driver must be satisfied that the vehicle is in safe operating condition and, when applicable, review the previous vehicle inspection report. The regulation expressly allows required reports to be maintained electronically.

49 CFR §392.7 also says a commercial motor vehicle cannot be driven unless the driver is satisfied that specified safety equipment, including brakes, steering, lights, tires, mirrors, wheels and emergency equipment is in good working order. FMCSA guidance likewise describes a pre-trip inspection as occurring before operation so the driver can be satisfied the vehicle is safe.

These regulations do not automatically mean that every road-relief operator must perform a complete new Zonar inspection. But they raise an important question:

If Metro expects every driver to meet the federal pre-driving safety requirements, what inspection procedure and scheduled time are provided when a new operator takes over a bus during road relief?

Operators Need Clear Answers Before Implementation

There is also an accountability issue. If an operator completes Zonar and later damage is discovered, will that operator be held responsible for a defect or damage that was not reasonably visible during the inspection?

Before full implementation, Metro should issue a clear written policy answering these questions: Where can Zonar inspections be performed safely? How much paid time is provided? What happens when technology fails? What is required during road relief? And exactly where does an operator's responsibility begin and end?

Technology should make transit safer. It should not require operators to choose between watching a tablet, watching a moving bus, and watching the clock.

ATU Latino Caucus Brings Solidarity to the Big Easy

By Michael Mackay, Executive Board Officer #1 · Page 9 · Open in the PDF

Transit workers from across the Amalgamated Transit Union came to New Orleans in September 2026 for the 35th Annual ATU International Latino Caucus Conference. Over several days at the Loews New Orleans Hotel, caucus members learned from one another, built power, and took on the challenges their families and coworkers face today.

International President John Costa opened the conference with a clear message: "We won't stand by while our members live in fear." He spoke about immigration raids near transit hubs and about members who are afraid to go to work because of where they were born, the color of their skin, or the language they speak at home. He tied that fear to money pressures many members already know too well: rising rent, grocery bills, health care costs, and automation advancing faster than paychecks.

"For our members, immigration fear and economic pressure are not two separate fights," Costa said. "They show up in the same household, sometimes on the same day."

Costa also highlighted the caucus's history of organizing and honored Executive Vice President Yvette Trujillo, the first woman and first Latina elected to that position. "Our answer to fear is organizing," he said. "Our answer to fear is each other."

Attendees heard from New Orleans Mayor Helena Moreno, the city's first Latina mayor, and ATU International staff led trainings on grievances and arbitrations that gave members practical skills to bring home to their locals.

Photo caption: From Left to Right, Luis Alzate, President, ATU Latino Caucus; Michael Mackay, President, ATU PRIDE Caucus; and Karima Howard, President, ATU Black Caucus

Latino Caucus President Luis Alzate provided a strong panel on non-domiciled CDLs and what the new restrictions mean for immigrant transit workers. He told me the idea for the session came from the ATU PRIDE Caucus conference earlier this year.

President Costa asked me to speak about Seattle's transit levy, which is on the ballot. I thanked ATU Local 587 President Greg Woodfill for securing the $75,000 our local contributed to the campaign. President Costa and the International Executive Board then doubled that amount. If voters pass the levy, it could add 100,000 service hours to Metro and create many more union jobs.

I thanked President Woodfill for making it possible for me to attend, along with the many people who have supported the ATU PRIDE Caucus. I also walked the room through what we've built together over the past four years.

For me, some of the most valuable moments came in conversations between sessions. I spoke with Raymond Jackson, President and Business Agent of ATU Local 689 in Washington, D.C., and the first-ever recipient of the Yvette Trujillo Award. Local 689 represents 16,888 members under 15 contracts. Raymond told me the local has spent millions of dollars fighting for its members' right to strike, and that it was a fight worth having.

I had a great conversation with Jim Bradley, Financial Secretary-Treasurer of the Boston Carmen's Union, ATU Local 589. He told me Boston wants us to beat $48.55 an hour, because if we go above it, they can use our contract as a comparable in their next round of bargaining. That's solidarity across locals: every raise we win helps lift transit workers across the country.

I also valued building a relationship with Karima Howard, President of the ATU International Black Caucus. She is a powerful voice for our members and a mentor to me.

The city carried its own union history. Members visited Parkway Bakery and Tavern, a New Orleans po'boy institution. The po'boy dates back to the 1929 streetcar strike, when local restaurants fed striking transit workers, members of the union that became today's ATU, and called them "poor boys."

The conference closed with a message for every Latino and Latina transit worker: you are not alone, and the ATU stands with you. ¡Sí se puede!

35th Annual ATU International Latino Caucus Conference

By Stephanie Binschus, ATU 587 Kitsap Transit Executive Board Representative and ATU International Latino Caucus Northwest Regional Representative · Page 10 · Open in the PDF

My interactions with the ATU International Latino Caucus (ILC) began in 2025, when I was asked to help with their registration system for the 34th annual ATU ILC conference, held the weekend before the ATU tri-annual Convention. This task involved creating a website for the ATU ILC and managing their online system; they also allowed paper registrations, which I did not handle. As the conference concluded and the NW rep position was vacant, the current board asked me to step into it; I agreed, and they appointed me about a month after the convention. This has meant more work for me this past year, working with some of our fellow members of 587 to resurrect the SCLC (Seattle Chapter Latino Caucus) (as of writing this [October 14], we still have not received the written response to hold officer elections for the SCLC).

Enough background. Let's talk about the 35th Annual ATU ILC Conference.

This year's Conference was held in New Orleans, Louisiana, from September 16 to September 20, 2026. Each year brings its own challenges, and this year the biggest challenge for the Latino Caucus Officers was the enormous amount of support we had, which forced us to shut down registration early because we hit the capacity of the meeting space where we were holding the conference. We had over 300 participants, including attendees, guests, and vendors/sponsors. The ATU ILC executive board met most of the morning and afternoon of Thursday, September 16, to prepare for the mass check-in expected that evening.

We opened the conference with a local group of Black Masking-Indians (also known as Mardi Gras Indians) in traditional dress and many playing instruments.

During the conference, the NOLA Mayor joined us and gave a nice talk about the need for unions to hold our politicians accountable for keeping their promises by encouraging members to vote.

We had a panel discussion about immigration law and the effects of recent political changes, such as the revocation of non-domicile CDLs. The Q&A session was informative; several attendees shared additional important information beyond what the panel presented.

My favorite presentation at the conference was the one ATU International Staff gave on Grievances and Arbitrations. John Lyons and Rich Otzel, along with attendee participation, made the presentation entertaining when John or Rich took on the personas of the different types of people a Shop Steward (or Union Representative) might interact with and interview to help write a grievance.

The board renamed an award previously named after someone once thought to be a good union leader in the Latino community, but whose reputation was tarnished by actions that recently came to light, to the "Yvette Truillo Labor Leader Award," which was awarded to Raymond Jackson from Local 689.

Letter to the Editor: A "Break-Language Win" — But What Did We Give Up?

By Andy Price, Bellevue Base Shop Steward · Pages 12–13 · Open in the PDF

Dear Editor,

In the October News Review, President Greg Woodfill announced what he described as "A break-language win for ASM service on the G-Line."

He reported that operators would receive guaranteed breaks after every revenue trip, with a minimum of 12 minutes or 18% of scheduled trip time, whichever is greater.

That sounds encouraging. But after examining the actual Memorandum of Agreement (MOA), I have some serious questions about what we've gained, what we've given up, and why Metro wanted these changes.

And there's another question: Why couldn't I ask about this agreement during questions on the President's Report when the agreement was expressly discussed in his published report?

What the Agreement Actually Says

Our existing collective bargaining agreement, Section 15.3(I), requires guaranteed breaks and layovers to be printed on operators' run cards.

That's an important protection. It gives operators a written record against which their guaranteed rest periods can be checked.

The new G-Line MOA changes that arrangement for ASM operations. Instead of documenting guaranteed breaks on run cards in the existing manner, the agreement provides that they will be referenced in the Operations Manual.

Why remove an existing contractual documentation requirement without clearly explaining what equally enforceable protection replaces it?

At the October 3 membership meeting, Woodfill acknowledged that Metro approached the Union seeking changes to accommodate ASM operations. He explained that departure and break times under ASM are not fixed.

That explains Metro's desire for flexibility. It doesn't fully answer what operators receive in exchange for giving up an established documentation requirement.

And the agreement contains provisions deserving even closer scrutiny.

Operators arriving at an ASM terminal are required to immediately notify the on-duty supervisor when beginning a guaranteed 15-minute break.

Does that mean locating the supervisor? What happens if the supervisor is unavailable? Does the break begin upon arrival or only after notification?

The language doesn't clearly answer those questions.

But perhaps the most concerning provision states:

"In an extreme emergency, Metro may use discretion to ensure functional service for customers while maintaining at least one hour of break time per eight hours."

Read that carefully.

The agreement establishes guaranteed breaks after every revenue trip, yet also provides management with discretion during an undefined "extreme emergency."

Who determines what constitutes such an emergency? Can ordinary service disruptions qualify? Can individual guaranteed breaks be postponed or shortened?

And what exactly counts toward that one hour of break time? Does it include recovery time or only actual guaranteed rest periods during which operators are relieved of duty?

An aggregate hour of break time over eight hours is not necessarily equivalent to guaranteed rest following each revenue trip.

The agreement doesn't expressly grant unlimited management discretion. But it does establish an exception whose limits and application deserve explanation.

A guaranteed break should not become discretionary simply because management encounters operational difficulties.

Negotiating Changes During an Expired Contract

Our principal collective bargaining agreement expired October 31, 2025.

Yet this MOA expressly states that the parties intend to incorporate the new Section 15.3(W) into the successor agreement.

We're therefore negotiating changes to established rest-break provisions while our principal contract remains unresolved.

If this agreement represents a significant improvement, it should withstand careful questioning.

Why Couldn't I Ask During the President's Report?

At the October 3 membership meeting, Woodfill completed his oral report and opened the floor for questions.

I attempted to ask about the G-Line MOA, which he had expressly discussed in his officially published President's Report.

Recording Secretary Latrel Gibson intervened, asserting that the written President's Report and Woodfill's oral presentation were separate. Because Woodfill hadn't mentioned the MOA orally, I was told to wait until New Business.

I had the News Review in my hand and pointed to the heading "The President's Report."

I asked where our bylaws established that restriction.

No governing provision was identified.

My initial motion to proceed to New Business died for lack of a second. Eventually, I was permitted to ask both questions.

Woodfill offered explanations, but important questions concerning documentation and the relationship between existing and new contractual protections remained unanswered.

This Concerns Every Member

The President's Report has appeared prominently in the News Review for as long as I can remember. Frequently, the written report contains considerably more information than the President repeats orally.

Our membership also conducts business through several meetings during each monthly cycle. Oral presentations can vary, and another officer may preside when the President is absent.

Should members' opportunities to question the President's published report depend on what happens to be repeated aloud at the particular meeting they attend?

Article XI, Section 1 of Local 587's bylaws establishes Reports of Officers as part of the regular order of business and directs members to raise questions under the proper heading. Section 17 adopts Robert's Rules of Order for matters not otherwise covered.

I've found no provision stating that matters officially published in the President's Report are excluded from questions merely because the President didn't repeat them orally.

After the meeting, I asked Woodfill to identify the governing rule. I received no response identifying it.

I subsequently sought clarification through a formal appeal to the International. President Costa declined to consider the appeal on procedural grounds, noting that I eventually asked my questions under New Business.

But that doesn't resolve the central question.

May members question the President about matters expressly contained in his published President's Report when his report is opened for questions, even if those matters weren't repeated orally?

And returning to the G-Line agreement:

If this is a win for operators, why are existing documentation requirements being changed, why is supervisor notification being introduced, and why does the agreement give Metro emergency discretion over guaranteed breaks?

An agreement described as a victory should withstand questions about its actual language.

If Metro requested changes to established contractual protections, members deserve to know what changed, why it changed, and how their rights will be enforced.

And when the President publishes a report announcing those changes, members deserve a clear, consistent opportunity to ask questions about them.

Bad Schedules Are the Greatest Safety Hazard. Help Us Prove It.

By Chuck Lare #12497, ATU 587 Shop Steward · Pages 26–27 · Open in the PDF

Every operator knows the feeling. Traffic backs up, the bus loads slowly, a wheelchair boards, and by the time you reach the outer terminal your recovery time is gone. Your break exists on paper, but the clock says go, "turn-and-burn." So you pull out and keep driving.

That is not a personal failing. It is a hazard built into the schedule.

The Hazard Chain

  1. Schedule design: recovery time is too short.
  2. Late running: congestion, passenger loads, incidents, and construction eat the buffer.
  3. Missed breaks: you arrive at the terminal with no time left, under pressure to leave.
  4. Fatigue builds: sustained attention and workload without recovery wear you down.
  5. Impaired judgment: reaction time, attention and decision-making suffer.
  6. Crash risk: collisions, pedestrian strikes and passenger injuries become more likely.

Metro Knows

Metro cannot claim surprise.

  • CBA Article 15.3.L requires Metro to review routes showing "a pattern of insufficient break time" after operators file OSFRs (Green Sheets).
  • The Transit Operator Manual tells operators to send DDU messages when taking guaranteed breaks, which acknowledges that breaks get missed.
  • The labor agreement created a joint Committee to Address Employee Fatigue. Fatigue is a system-level safety issue, not an individual comfort complaint.
  • FTA Safety Bulletin 26-01 put every transit agency on notice. It counted 133 fatigue-related safety events since 2014, 73 of them from 2022 to 2025.

The science is just as clear. NIOSH says fatigue slows reaction time, reduces concentration, and impairs judgment, and recommends brief rest breaks at least every two hours. FTA research describes transit driving as "active fatigue": dense traffic, a timed schedule, fares, and dispatch communication all at once. L&I policy recognizes that hurried restroom or food stops are not genuine rest, especially when the work demands constant vigilance.

The toll is also personal. A NIOSH-supported study of medical claims found depression in 16.2% of local passenger transit workers, the highest of 55 industries studied and about 55% above the 10.5% overall rate. Chronic time pressure, little recovery, and restricted restroom access wear on people. The fix is changing the work environment, not telling operators to cope better.

Why Your Evidence is Needed

Metro may say this is only a contract issue. It isn't. Washington's safety law (WISHA, RCW 49.17.060) requires every employer, Metro included, to provide a workplace free from recognized hazards likely to cause serious injury or death. That duty exists regardless of the CBA. L&I/DOSH has authority to investigate whether Metro's scheduling and dispatch practices create a recognized fatigue hazard, and to require correction.

But investigators and Metro's own schedulers respond to documentation, not stories. We need a record showing where, when, and how often the schedule fails.

What You Can Do Starting Your Next Shift

  • File an OSFR (Green Sheet) every time a run does not give you the guaranteed break. The CBA says Metro will review them. Make them.
  • Record the facts every time you arrive late at a terminal: date, route, block/run, terminal, scheduled and actual arrival, scheduled and actual departure, and minutes of break or layover lost.
  • Note the cause: congestion, event traffic, construction, passenger incident, dispatch instruction.
  • Note any pressure to leave early, and DDU messages tied to breaks.
  • Know your entitlement: under Article 15.3.J, less than 7 minutes of layover in the previous two hours entitles you to a 13-minute break. Take it, and document what happens.
  • Send copies to Chuck@lareassoc.com / text 206-465-1435 so patterns by route, run dates and time of day patterns of insufficient break time can be identified.

Please don't exaggerate or guess. Accurate, consistent records are the most powerful tool we have.

Where This Goes

With the evidence by route, run, date, and time(s): Metro per CBA Article 15.3.L is required to address routes that have a pattern of insufficient break time. If routes are not fixed, and there is sufficient material evidence that an L&I Worker Rights Complaint and a WISHA safety hazard complaint may be in order. These would request an onsite inspection of Metro's scheduling, dispatch and break-management practices, including run cards, AVI data, OSFRs and dispatch logs. If investigators confirm a recognized hazard, L&I can require abatement/corrective action.

Operators are asked to drive safely, on time, through the worst traffic in the region, without the recovery the schedule promises. A bad schedule is a safety hazard for you, your passengers and everyone on the road.

Document it. Report it. Send it: chuck@lareassoc.com / 206-465-1435.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 5

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
