The President's Report: Contract Bargaining Update

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 24 · Open in the PDF

Our Current Metro Bus/Rail contract has 56 Articles, 188,000 words, 542 pages, plus 100 pages of past MOAs to negotiate on whether to extend, incorporate into the contract, or let expire. To start contract negotiations, each side must present its protected position on all 56 articles, either proposing changes or maintaining the current contract language.

Historically, bargaining for a new contract with Metro has averaged 18 months and 60 bargaining sessions. The Union and Metro began negotiations for a new contract last July. As of 5/22/26, we have met for 30 contract bargaining sessions, with 12 more bargaining dates scheduled through August. Progress/movement has been slow but is starting to pick up.

In the spirit of the Union's decision to engage in "Open Bargaining," the Union committed for the first time ever to post our initial protected positions on our Union website for our members to see. It was a lot of work to process and post, but you can find all our union's initial proposals on the Union's website under Contract & Policies > KCM Negotiation Proposals.

Here are some of the things that you'll see that the Union is fighting for:

  • A 6% raise for each year of the new 3-year contract.
  • Improved break language for Bus, Rail, and Streetcar Operators.
  • Longevity Pay for all of our members.
  • A 7.5% safety premium for members working graveyard shifts in the field who aren't currently receiving a shift differential. And increases in some shift differentials and premiums for the classifications that currently receive them.
  • A market adjustment for the trades in maintenance who didn't receive one in the last contract.

I strongly encouraged the county to allow us to post its protected positions and told them we would inform the members if they refused. The County declined to authorize us to post their proposals. In short, the county's initial proposals include:

Mediocre wage increases, restricting overtime if you work less than 40 hours a week, and disciplinary language that the Union believes would water down just cause.

At the encouragement of an ATU International recruiter, we decided to form a Contract Action Team (CAT). I appointed Tamieko Cook to lead this team. We have brought in a total of 150 members to the Union Hall for a 2 ½-hour training on being a Contract Action Team member. Some AB/CB CAT members held a couple of internal organizing meetings at their base, and some CAT members from SB are scheduling Zoom meetings.

The CAT team's purpose is to help inform, educate, unite, and activate our membership as we transition from our past closed bargaining model to our local's first attempt at open bargaining.

The CAT team's short-term goals are to increase membership knowledge and participation in contract negotiations. Its long-term goals are to build an internal organizing model that increases membership participation in and ownership of the Union.

Benefits Bargaining Update

Up until around 15 years ago, we bargained for our benefits as part of the coalition of King County Unions. At that time, our Union decided we could do better by withdrawing from the coalition and started negotiating our medical benefits separately. While that strategy initially seemed questionable, it has proven to be beneficial in the long run.

When our Union withdrew from the coalition, a Protected Fund Reserve was set up for ATU 587's Benefits. Per the agreement that established this fund, if the fund falls below $4 million, Metro and the Union are required to negotiate a solution to increase the fund's balance. If an agreement can't be reached, it will go to mediation.

Every two years, we meet with the County to renegotiate our medical benefits. We negotiate the County's monthly contribution per employee, and any other necessary changes to keep the fund above $4 million. This bargaining is separate from Contract bargaining.

This year it happens to line up with our contract bargaining, and we recently held our 2nd benefits bargaining session. The County always comes with its dire projections on anticipated cost increases; this round is no different. The County will spend $102 million on ATU 587 medical benefits in 2026. They project that cost will increase to $111 Million in 2027 and $121 Million in 2028, leaving our protected reserve fund with a negative balance, or shortfall, of $13 million should their projections prove correct.

The County's initial protected position proposal is for ATU 587 members to absorb the entire shortfall by adding a monthly employee premium share, along with other out-of-pocket and deductible increases.

The Union's initial protected-position proposal is for the County to absorb the entire projected reserve fund shortfall by increasing the County's per-employee monthly contribution. Our stated on-the-record goal in these negotiations is not to add a monthly premium share; this has been our goal for every medical benefit negotiation.

Last year, the King County Coalition of Unions agreed to a $75-a-month medical premium share. I believe that leaves ATU 587 as the last King County Union holding the line against a monthly premium.

While technically medical benefits bargaining is separate from contract bargaining, the County has made its position clear that it will consider the total package cost of wages plus benefits during these two separate negotiations.

The Union's intent is to keep the membership updated as much as possible throughout both Contract and Medical Benefits Bargaining. Our Voice, Our Power!

The 1st Vice President's Report: Paid Time Off

By Cory Rigtrup · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

May was a roaring month. Filled with the optimism that Spring brings and an insatiable workload formed from the reality of a sustained hiring spree of folks learning how to fit into the transit world. This month's article is specifically about paid time away from work, and why employers have prioritized increasing worker attendance at bargaining tables.

When I started my transit career over two decades ago, my youngest child was 6 months old. The first few years, I worked the graveyard shift while my wife worked days as we raised our kids. As anyone who comes from a working family knows, the struggle to be at work every day with young children is real.

With the introduction of state sick leave in 2018, the flexibility of being able to take time off to care for ourselves or families became protected. This protected leave was doubled, then tripled down in subsequential years, as paid parental and Washington paid family medical leave were introduced. About the same time covid hit, along with the sickness came even more paid leave. Suddenly, folks had all this paid time off work in addition to their normal leaves. It was so nice and natural for folks to get accustomed too!

Today, working families have more paid time off than there was even a decade ago. Not just more paid time off, but protected time to take care of their families. While I am a bit jealous these benefits weren't available in my child rearing years, these additional leaves have created a number of issues.

Last month, our Local sent out dues arrears letters to 820 members who are behind on their dues. That is roughly 1/6 of our membership. Because dues are automatically deducted from payroll earnings, a member would have to receive no payroll income for some time to be behind on dues. Think about that, 1/6 of our membership at some point earned no income in the last 9 months.

A few years ago, our Local started seeing proposals from employers on ways to limit or penalize members from taking time off work on non-protected leaves. These kinds of proposals started coming from each property where our Local was negotiating the next contract. Regardless of the property the theme was the same, employer proposed penalizing ways of getting employees back to work. None of these proposals would suit your fancy. We've seen proposals: reducing the number of protected sick leave days; Increased restrictions on holiday pay eligibility; Reductions in premiums for how overtime is earned; Additional compensation earnings converting to just wages, less leave. The list goes on, but the theme is the same, how to penalize employees so others will return to work.

I decided to write this article to educate you on our collective interest. When our Local proposes things we all are interested in, employers start talking about ways to get you to work. That is the real trick, how to get them to say yes, when we are saying no. I've always enjoyed bargaining; it is like a real long confidence game with consequences. Come to a bargaining session, see it for yourself.

Hopefully this article gets you thinking.

The Maintenance Vice President's Report: The Never-ending Saga with Bus TFD

By Jeff Stambaugh · Page 5 · Open in the PDF

Why can't we have open honest up-front communication?

You know the adage it's better to beg forgiveness than ask permission? Maybe TFD should make this their slogan. As most of you know, every month we have LMRC meetings. In fact, we have several of them, all throughout Metro, Bus, Rail, Operations, Maintenance, TFD you name the section we probably have an LMRC meeting.

What is an LMRC you ask? Well the Labor Management Relations Committee language in Bus Facilities reads in part, "The purposes of this committee shall be implementation, discussion and resolution of working conditions, updates to the notebook entitled Policies, Procedures, and Guidelines, issues /problems of METRO policy/procedures which affect Facilities Maintenance, contract clarification issues, issues or problems of contract administration other than formal grievances which are being processed, and other matters of mutual concern."

I think one of the most important pieces of that language is the last six words," and other matters of mutual concern."

Well, rather than bring issues forward for discussion TFD routinely makes announcements at crew meetings on their next big plan or project and how it's going to be done before any discussion is had with The Union. The latest being clean-up of a homeless encampment at a Metro Park-n-Ride. After it was announced to the crew, I had several concerned members call me wanting to know what I knew. The answer, "NOTHING," you know why? TFD management failed to communicate any of this with The Union. Once I learned of this, I reached out to TFD supervision and asked a bunch of questions about Safety, Training and how the work would be assigned. TFD replied and did not fully answer the concerns of the members and The Union. The Union then respectfully requested them to postpone the work until the concerns could be worked through. Rather that delay or have more conversation TFD proceeded to have our 587 Members report to and dismantle several homeless encampments without Police or First Line Supervision presence. It is very frustrating that rather than having an upfront conversation with The Union and working together to make things better TFD continues to do what they want, how they want, when they want, without any mutual collaboration.

Bus Maintenance

VM leadership has still failed to explain why they have denied most of all the requests for change of hours. Im hoping that at some point someone can explain it to me. And for clarity "Does not meet business needs" is not an acceptable answer. It is like saying "I'm daddy and I say so" to a child!

Holiday unexcused absences update. We met with Metro and a PERC mediator and attempted to resolve the Holiday ULP issue. We could not come to an agreement so we will continue to move through the PERC process in hopes of resolution.

As we get closer to FIFA, I still hold out hope that VM management will start to ramp up OT opportunities and get more coaches on the road and not lose service during FIFA.

Rail Maintenance

Rail for the most part has been quiet other than a dispute about OT that arose around the word "BASE" in the CBA. I had a manager try to tell me that the platform at the end of the line was a "base." My response was "Are they building a base in Federal Way?" he replied "Yes." Seems like we will have a "base" in Federal Way but, currently we do not.

The Recording Secretary's Report: Black Caucus in Jersey City, NJ!

By Latrelle Gibson · Pages 7–8 · Open in the PDF

ATU International Black Caucus Conference, May 1:

I was able to attend the 59th Annual ATU International Black Caucus Conference that was held in Jersey City, NJ, April 30 – May 3, 2026. On day one, the conference was called to order by ATU International Black Caucus President, Karima Howard. Following was the National and Canadian Anthems. We received a welcome from Karima which included an introduction of the ATU International Black Caucus Executive Board members. We were welcomed to NJ by James Solomon, Mayor of Jersey City, NJ.

There were some EXTREMELY influential speakers at this year's conference. We were able to hear from Lawrence Hamm, founder of People's Organization for progress, based out of New Jersey. He spoke about the importance of Unions and how the current administration is working around the clock to get rid of Unions. Unions are how many black people were able to enter the work force and make a fair living wage. He spoke about the importance of all Union workers standing in solidarity with one another to keep our Unions strong!

Next, we heard from guest speaker and preacher, Terry Melvin, Coalition of Black Trade Unionists. He spoke about liberation and how much we all value our freedom. He spoke to the Bill of Rights that all men are created equal, that those words weren't talking about us. Spoke to there being a time where we as black people couldn't come together as group, that if more than three of us met in a group, there would be a riot. Spoke to the fact that no working person can be free as long as another person is oppressed, we cannot demand our fair treatment while others are being oppressed. Spoke that this current administration is focused on immigration, but this isn't about undocumented workers and immigrants, it's about those sitting on the conference floor, it's about the working rights of Union workers and doing anything to get rid of these rights, we have a target on our backs for being part of the labor movement. He spoke to American Descendants of the Enslaved still not getting any credit for a nation built on slave labor. He spoke to the rouge Supreme Court who allowed the decision of gutting the voting rights act, that the Supreme Court stated that they allowed redrawing of lines based on the party one is in, but not on race, even though this was the whole point of the voting rights act, so those in predominately black communities could have representation. Spoke about the unlimited power this administration has and hold all accountable for continuing to allow this to happen.

Next, we saw a presentation from ATU International on AI technology. This presentation highlighted Tech in Transit and how this is squarely aimed and getting rid of Transit jobs. Customer Service could see AI phone operators and chatbots, scheduling and routing could see automated decision making systems (run cuts, microtransit routing), we could see surveillance turn to AI and aimed at operators to watch us real time, see our faces and emotions with tools to recognize these movements and expressions, and this all could lead to their ultimate goal, Automated Driving System, which would take our place by enabling a computer to operate the equipment.

Included in this presentation was information on how Waymo, the driverless rideshare vehicles are growing. They now have driverless vehicles in Atlanta, GA, Dallas, Houston, and San Antonio TX, Los Angeles and San Francisco Bay Area, CA, Miami, and Orlando FL, Nashville, TN, Phoenix, AZ. They are coming to the following cities next: Baltimore, Boston, Charlotte, Chicago, Denver, Detroit, DC, Las Vegas, London (UK), Minneapolis, New Orleans, New York, Philadelphia, Pittsburg, Portland, Sacramento, San Diego, SEATTLE, St. Louis, Tampa, and Tokyo. Waymo has logged 500,000 paid trips per week, which equals to over 170 million driverless miles traveled. They have a fleet of 3,000+ vehicles and have raised at least $27 billion in known funding, and this couldn't be from all the lobbying, could it!? The tune of $3.7 million was spent lobbying in the U.S Congress in 2025, the company spent $357,000 in New York State in just 6 months in 2025, they spend $28,000 a month on retainers in Washington, D.C for 2026, and have 678 disclosed contracts with San Francisco officials in 2026!

Where do Waymo employees work, you ask? They have four remote assistance sites: Arizona, Michigan, and the Philippines, with only 70 remote assistants on the clock at one time! That's only 70 assistants overseeing 3,000 vehicles in 11 cities!!! We were shown a video on what this looks like, we saw Waymo's running yellow lights, getting confused on roundabouts, not knowing how to properly yield to emergency vehicles, getting stuck, etc., and when these vehicles are having issues, if someone is trying to remotely navigate the vehicle in unfamiliar territory, they just stop the vehicle and put the flashers on. How safe is that!!

We heard from money manager quest speakers who announced their ATU IBC Scholarship winners! It was very exciting to be a part of this; I love that the Caucus has a scholarship like this! Next, we heard from ATU International Workforce Development Training, Jermaine Gibson, the director of training (I know, folks always mix us up, ha-ha) who spoke on the development of the growing mentorship program.

May 2, Last Day:

We opened the conference with more guest speakers, such as Dr. Loretta Johnson of the American Federation of Teachers, AFL-CIO, and Janet Gerhard of FHE Health. Following was the Roll Call of Locals and the reading of the minutes by ATUIBC Recording Secretary, Raynell Redd from Local 689. Next, the financial report was presented, followed by a message from our ATU International President John A. Costa. We also received a presentation from ATU International Secretary-Treasurer, Kenneth Kirk on Locals being financially responsible, and if this doesn't happen, how trusteeship of a Local works. He highlighted the fact that the International doesn't enjoy taking over a Local and forcing them to hand over the keys. When the International steps in, the Local is in bad shape, and it's the job of the International to get the Local running according to our General Constitution.

After lunch, we enjoyed the cultural dance of the Fearless Dancers Empire, who all danced so well. Following this, we heard words of partnership and accountability from IVP Amanda Turner-Sawyer, then swearing in of New Locals to join the ATUIBC. The financial report presented created some questions to the board, including John Costa, since we were given one report then it was revised, however, not listing everything that was revised in line-item format. Those with questions made sure to let it be known that nobody was accusing them of mismanagement, however, there needs to be a better system for how these reports are generated and presented. John Costa and Kenneth Kirk helped with answering some of the questions from those at mics on the floor, with Kenneth saying that he would help the IBC devise a better system for presenting these reports. This is exactly what solidarity looks like, all coming together to help uplift others in need. I absolutely love this!

In closing, I want to say the last two ATUIBC conferences I attended were jam packed of knowledge, recognition, solidarity, and self-care and most importantly, loving oneself. I can't wait to see what's in store for us next year, and speaking of next year, the conference will be hosted in Chicago! Exciting!

When the Bus Says Metro, Workers Deserve Union Protections

By Andy Price, ATU Local 587 Member · Page 9 · Open in the PDF

Over the past several months, while working my regular assignment and laying over at Eastgate, I have had occasions to spend some time talking with operators working the route 249. That route is historically Bellevue Base work. ATU 587 work. Today it has been subcontracted out.

I believe this deserves serious discussions among the membership, because our Collective Bargaining Agreement is not vague on this subject.

Section 3.10(A) states plainly: "Metro shall not contract out work historically performed by Employees if the contracting of such work eliminates or reduces the normal workload of the Union." The same section further affirms that the Union has "exclusive work jurisdiction over all non-deviated fixed-route service." That language did not find its way into the contract by accident. Route 249 was part of Bellevue Base operations. Now Bellevue Base is being shut down on weekends. Folks can draw their own conclusions, but I do not believe it is reasonable to pretend there is no connection between bargaining unit work disappearing and the gradual reduction of work supporting the base itself.

What makes this issue even more concerning is that most of the public likely have no idea this transition has even taken place. The buses still say, "King County Metro" and "DART" on the side. Riders naturally assume they are boarding ordinary Metro service operated under the same labor standards and protections Metro operators spent decades fighting to establish. But behind the Metro branding sits an entirely different labor structure. One of the subcontracted 249 operators I spoke with told me he had previously worked as a Teamster truck driver before the company he worked for went bankrupt. According to him, he lost his pension in the process and eventually ended up operating this Metro-branded route for a nonunion contractor.

He also told me the company is struggling to retain operators and is offering substantial signing bonuses to attract drivers. According to the operators I spoke with, this contractor is also receiving increasing amounts of Metro-related work throughout King County. That ought to get the attention of union members. What struck me most, however, was not simply the wages or benefits. It was the lack of protection.

One subcontracted 249 operator became visibly upset while discussing a customer complaint investigation involving management review of onboard video footage. He felt management immediately took the passenger's side and that he had no union representation available to him during the process. To be perfectly honest, that conversation sounded awfully familiar.

Metro operators have been raising concerns for years now regarding the growing use of surveillance and retrospective video review. Article 3.8 of our agreement was negotiated specifically to place limits on indiscriminate surveillance practices. Many operators already believe Metro has been stretching, and in some cases outright disregarding those protections on a regular basis.

The difference is that Metro operators still have contractual protections, grievance procedures, shop stewards, arbitration rights, and representation standing behind them. These subcontracted workers often do not. That should concern every Union member paying close attention to where this road leads.

Because subcontracting does not make these problems disappear. It simply transfers them onto a more vulnerable group of workers with fewer protections, lower wages, reduced benefits, and very little ability to push back. That is precisely why subcontracting bargaining unit work is such a serious issue under both our contract and Washington State labor law. RCW 41.56 exists to protect collective bargaining rights in the public sector, and PERC rulings over the years have repeatedly recognized that subcontracting bargaining unit work without proper bargaining or contractual justification can raise serious legal concerns.

This situation deserves careful scrutiny. I also believe it is fair to say the Union should have pushed back harder when this work was first moved out of the bargaining unit. I do not say that for the purpose of assigning blame. These situations are often complicated, and management rarely presents subcontracting decisions as plainly as they appear later in hindsight. Still, when historically union-operated fixed-route service leaves the bargaining unit without meaningful resistance, it becomes easier for those decisions to expand over time.

The harm this change has done to Local 587's numbers is clear and evident and in our face every day. The best way we can push back now, in my opinion, is to facilitate the unionization of DART. At the same time, I believe we ought to approach this issue intelligently. The workers performing this service are not the enemy. In many ways they are dealing with the same surveillance pressures, disciplinary systems, and management practices, many Metro operators already feel subjected to themselves, except without union protection standing behind them.

Historically, Unions did not grow by abandoning workers laboring under weaker conditions. They grew by organizing them and raising standards across entire industries. If Metro and its contractors intend to continue moving work in this direction, then we should seriously explore bringing these operators into union representation rather than standing by while a divided system of labor standards quietly expands throughout King County transit. And the truth is, these operators are not difficult to reach. Many work side-by-side with us every single day at the same transit centers and park-and-rides.

The Stories We Tell: Clearing the Mush at Metro (Part 2 of 2)

By Balaramdas Stern, Shop Steward, SLC Rep, Wellness Champion, Mentors Moving Metro – Bellevue Base · Pages 10–11 · Open in the PDF

What happens when the conversation isn't wrong, when the silence, the vague message, or the story we are spinning about someone else still carries real weight? What does it take to build trust that holds up through the daily grind of the road?

In Part 1 we explored the Interpersonal Gap, that foggy space between what we intend and the impact our behavior has on others. We saw how the mush forms so easily: the new recruit's rough pull-out, a superintendent's quick email, or a lead walking past without a word. I have been wondering how often I do the same thing without realizing it. This month I keep sitting with the question. What might change if we practiced clearing the mush as an ongoing way of working and living together?

Learning Conversations: Finding Our Way Through

Gervase Bushe, in his book Clear Leadership, describes learning conversations as a practical way to cut through assumptions and get back to what is really happening for each person. These are not about deciding who is right. They are curious check-ins. What did you see? What were you thinking or feeling? What did you intend? How did it land for me?

I am curious how many of our workplace tensions might ease if we got more open about the stories, we tell ourselves. Bushe suggests many conflicts dissolve once we surface those assumptions. The real issue is often not the schedule, the tone, or the decision. It is the inaccurate and unverified stories layered on top. John Wallen's Interpersonal Gap model adds depth. We encode our thoughts through our own ideas, viewpoints, and filters, and others decode them through theirs. The gap is normal. Simple skills like describing behavior, checking perceptions, and owning feelings can help close it. These feel-like grassroots tools worth trying on the bus, in the shop, in dispatch, or at home. Here is one example I have been reflecting on. After a service disruption, an operator notices their supervisor seems short during the debrief. Instead of assuming they are upset with how it was handled, the operator later says, "I noticed you seemed a little frustrated after that delay today. I am wondering what was going through your mind. From my side I was trying to keep things safe while dealing with a tough passenger situation." The supervisor replies, "I was mostly worried about the headway and how it was affecting the following trips. I wasn't upset with you. I just did not have the full picture yet." A short exchange like that can quietly turn defensiveness into understanding and teamwork.

Another check-in I have been thinking about might happen between coworkers at the base. One person says, "I noticed you have been quiet in our last few meetings. I am wondering if something is on your mind or if I said something that landed sideways for you."

Why This Matters in a Transit Organization

Transit work is uniquely interpersonal and high stakes. Operators juggle passengers, traffic, and radio calls. Dispatch and service quality make fast calls with limited information. Maintenance works under pressure. Supervisors, base chiefs, admin staff, and union folks try to hold it all together. The riding public feels the results. I wonder what happens when small misunderstandings accumulate. Do they quietly affect safety focus, morale, and our ability to solve real problems around scheduling, training, or equipment? Do we sometimes avoid certain people at work because we have created preset ideas about who they are and what we can expect from them, based on assumptions we have made about their behavior, without ever checking the story directly with them? I have been thinking about how clearing the mush might support the kind of sustained partnerships that help us navigate command and control limits and the bigger challenges of recruitment, retention, and reliability.

Blind Spots and the Gift of Others

We all have blind spots about how we come across. I might think I am being straightforward while someone else hears it as abrupt. I am learning that conversations like these can let others gently reveal those spots as helpful information rather than criticism.

At home I once built a whole story that my partner was pulling away because they seemed distracted in the evenings. A quiet check-in revealed she was stressed about work deadlines and did not want to burden me. The relief was instant, and we laughed about how fast our minds write dramatic novels. With our teenager it is easy to misread eye rolls or short answers as attitude. One evening I tried, "I noticed you went straight to your room and seemed pretty quiet. I am wondering if something happened or if I did something that landed wrong." Turns out it was a tough day with friends. We ended up taking longer than we had in weeks.

Everyday Examples Across Roles

Two operators on relief keep missing clean handoffs. One thinks the other is careless. The other feels watched. A curious conversation reveals one was dealing with a family issue and appreciates a little grace on timing. Judgment turns into mutual support.

I have also been reflecting on a harder situation many operators know well. An operator starts carrying the story that a particular base chief is "out to get them." Every note, every question, every look begins to feel like proof. The weight of that story grows heavier with each shift.

One operator I know finally tried a careful check-in: "I have been feeling like I am under extra scrutiny lately and I started wondering if I have done something that caused you to lose trust in me. I would like to understand what is really happening from your side."

The conversation did not magically fix everything, yet it opened the door. The chief shared the actual pressures they were under and some specific concerns. The operator was able to share how certain interactions had landed. Things did not become perfect overnight, but the heavy story lost much of its power. That shift made the workday feel lighter and safer for everyone involved. Two Service Quality supervisors might find themselves at odds after a major disruption. One assumes the other dropped the ball on rerouting during construction. The other feels unfairly blamed and starts pulling back from collaboration. A learning conversation might sound like: "I noticed we seemed out of sync on that reroute call the other day. I have been wondering if I missed something or if my approach landed wrong for you." Hearing the other person's pressures and constraints often softens the story and strengthens coordination next time.

A supervisor assumes an admin staff member is dragging their feet on paperwork. The admin is actually buried because of upstream delays. Exploring the gap leads to better processes and less frustration.

Union stewards and base chiefs can get caught in stories about priorities or not understanding the reality on the ground. A learning conversation sometimes uncovers shared goals and practical next steps.

Even with the public we serve, a rider's sharp comment about a delay can meet a tired operator's short reply. Stepping back to consider the human day both are having can turn tension into something kinder.

Clearing the Mush as an Ongoing Practice

This is not a one-time fix. I wonder what it would look like if we created more space in our organization for these kinds of inquiry conversations at every level. Bushe emphasizes that sustaining partnership requires periodic clearing of the inevitable mush. Think of it like vehicle maintenance. Ignore it and stories worsen. Do it regularly and the whole system runs smoother.

There is even a light side. Sometimes after a good clearing talk you catch yourself thinking, "Wow, I built that whole dramatic story for nothing. My mind should write fiction."

An Open Invitation

Next time a vague email lands, a coworker seems off, or silence follows a decision, I am trying to pause and ask myself. What story am I telling here? What might their side actually be? What small question could I try?

I do not have perfect words for any of this, and I certainly do not fix everything in one conversation. I am hoping that starting to be more curious and respectful might shift something at work and at home.

Our work at Metro is about moving people safely, reliably, and with dignity. When we clear the stories that separate us, perhaps we move with each other a little more easily. The road ahead feels a bit brighter, and a lot more human.

Sources and influences: Gervase Bushe's Clear Leadership and John Wallen's work on the Interpersonal Gap, along with everyday experiences on the job and at home.

As Founding President and Chair of the ATU International PRIDE Caucus, I had the honor of welcoming members, allies, and guests to Toledo, Ohio for our second annual conference. Throughout the conference, we held meaningful discussions on LGBTQIA+ rights in collective bargaining, allyship, and organizing. We heard from union leaders, community voices, and members committed to pushing our movement forward. One powerful message I shared is that an injury to one is an injury to all, and that solidarity must be intersectional if it is to be real.

We also emphasized that coming out is not a single moment, but a lifelong act of courage. Our responsibility as a union is to create workplaces where authenticity and safety can coexist.

By the end of the conference, we had helped grow the caucus to 16 committed locals, including a new one in Canada. And with a successful vote, we're bringing the 2027 ATU International PRIDE Caucus Conference to Seattle.

I also had the opportunity to attend the 59th ATU International Black Caucus Conference, which brought together leaders, members, and community voices for two days of powerful discussion and action.

The conference also highlighted innovation, including discussions on artificial intelligence and the future of work, as well as leadership development and education opportunities. It was a space that combined leadership, education, and celebration, reinforcing the importance of unity and forward movement.

Why Caucuses Matter

Both conferences reinforced something I strongly believe union caucuses are essential to building power.

Caucuses create space for members to organize around shared goals, advocate for issues that matter to them, and hold leadership accountable. They strengthen our union by making it more democratic, more inclusive, and more responsive.

They also play a strategic role, whether it's shaping bargaining priorities, mobilizing members, or building coalitions in our communities. Historically, some of the most significant gains in the labor movement have come from strong, organized caucuses pushing for change from within. At their core, caucuses are about connection, empowerment, and action.

Moving Forward

This past month has been demanding, but it's also been meaningful. Whether it's handling grievances, building new systems for fairness, or strengthening our union through caucus work, it all comes back to one thing: showing up for our members.

The work is constant, and the pace doesn't slow down, but neither does our commitment. We're building something stronger every day.

When "Education" Replaces Enforcement, Fare Policy Becomes Fiction

By James E. Reese · Page 21 · Open in the PDF

This article is going to make some people uncomfortable and honestly, that's the point. There's this old saying, if what I wrote is upsetting, if you can't stand the heat, get out of the kitchen. King County Metro Transit has spent the past year insisting that its fare enforcement strategy is working, that it's humane, equitable, and rooted in "education." But the numbers tell a different story. Not a story of compassion, a story of avoidance.

Since Metro resumed fare enforcement last May, officers have conducted nearly 79,000 fare checks. Out of all those interactions, they issued 8 citations. Not eighty. Not eight hundred. Eight. And not even one has been paid. Meanwhile, more than 2,000 warnings were handed out which sounds meaningful until you remember that a warning without consequences is just a conversation. Metro might call this a success. I call it a system that has quietly stopped believing in its own rules.

The County's official line is that the goal is "education, not punishment." But let's be honest: education is not the issue. Everyone riding a bus knows what a fare is. Everyone knows what the farebox is for. Pretending that fare evasion is a misunderstanding that people simply need more information is a convenient fiction that lets the County avoid enforcing a policy it claims to uphold. And here's where the logic collapses. If someone robs a bank and the police catch them, we don't expect a teachable moment. We don't expect a pamphlet on financial ethics. We expect accountability. Because without accountability, the law becomes optional.

So why is fare evasion treated as a philosophical disagreement rather than a policy violation? Why is Metro bending over backward to avoid the very enforcement it says it has reinstated? If the County believes fares are essential, then enforce them. If it believes that fares are unjust, then eliminate them. But this halfway approach, this symbolic enforcement, satisfies no one and solves nothing.

Transit systems run on trust: trust that the agency will provide reliable service, and trust that riders will contribute their share. When one side abandons its responsibility, the entire system strains. Operators feel it. Paying riders feel it. And the public, watching from the outside, sees a government unwilling to stand behind its own rules. At some point, we must ask the uncomfortable question: If Metro has no intention of collecting fares, why have a farebox at all? Why maintain the illusion of a policy that the County has no appetite to enforce?

This isn't about criminalizing poverty. It's about acknowledging that a rule without consequences is not a rule, it's a suggestion. And a transit system built on suggestions is not a system built to last. King County Metro owes the public clarity. Either enforce the fare policy you claim to believe in or be honest enough to admit the farebox is nothing more than a relic from a time when expectations mattered.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 5

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
