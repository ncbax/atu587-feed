The President's Report: Metro True North Values — Terminations

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 24 · Open in the PDF

In the printed headline, the words "True North" are struck through.

Terminations at King County Metro are currently at an unprecedented level. In the first 11 months of 2025, we had 25 terminations. In the last 11 weeks, we've had 22 terminations. Houston, we have a problem. Make this make sense, Metro management.

For many of these terminations, it makes no sense to the Union why management would go straight to proposing termination, then just dig in their heels. We've had twenty-plus-year county public servants with good work records fired for things the Union cannot understand why termination would even be proposed, let alone carried out.

This is not the Metro that many of us signed up for decades ago. We were not expected to be robots incapable of error. The Chiefs, Superintendents, and upper management had actually worked in our front-line shoes. They had the authority to apply progressive discipline along with the experience, wisdom, and courage to do so. They recognized the value of second chances for County employees who had given years of service, and that the County had so much invested in.

I'm not lumping in or attempting to stereotype all Chiefs, Superintendents, or Labor Relations personnel. Or insinuate that all terminations have no merit or don't meet just cause. The Union does observe and appreciate those who are doing their job correctly, as they should, and are being paid well to do so.

Upper management won't take our concerns seriously and seems to want to circle the wagons around their management personnel, refusing to investigate their out-of-line disciplinary actions and discharges, or to believe they could do no wrong.

From more than one person in upper management, I've heard, "We don't want to come across as not supporting our managers." What about supporting your long-term front-line employees? The ones who actually do the work, and who, without them, there would be no need for management jobs.

With the benefit of hindsight and frame-by-frame video review, it's easy to sit in warm rooms and pass judgment. These are people's lives; they deserve the legal and contractual protections of the just cause standard for discipline or discharge. They deserve to be disciplined with dignity and respect, taking their work record and difficult working conditions into account. Every person and every situation is different, and those differences need to be seriously considered before deciding on termination.

What is a "just cause" standard? In 1964, labor arbitrator Carroll Daugherty introduced "The seven tests of just cause" in the form of seven questions.

  1. Fair Notice - An employer may not discipline an employee for violating a rule or standard whose nature and penalties have not been made known.
  2. Prior Enforcement - An employee may not be penalized for violating a rule or standard that the employer has failed to enforce for a prolonged period.
  3. Due Process - An employer must conduct an interview or a hearing before issuing discipline, and must take action promptly. Once assessed, discipline may not be increased.
  4. Substantial Evidence - Charges must be proven by substantial and credible evidence.
  5. Equal Treatment - Unless a valid distinction justifies a higher penalty, an employer may not assess a considerably stronger punishment against one employee than it assessed against another known to have committed the same or a substantially similar offense.
  6. Progressive Discipline - When responding to misconduct that is short of egregious (very serious), an employer must issue at least one level of discipline that allows the employee an opportunity to improve.
  7. Mitigating and Extenuating Circumstances - Discipline must be proportional to the gravity of the offense, taking into account any mitigating, extenuating, or aggravating circumstances.

In the last year, we've observed Metro violating all seven items on the list above. Some of Metro's proposals on discipline in our current contract negotiations would weaken our just cause protections.

This Union will spare no expense or resources to defend our members, who we believe were subjected to discipline or termination without just cause.

The termination numbers listed in the first paragraph above don't include the greatly stepped-up termination of probationary employees at Metro, both at bus and rail. Management chose to unilaterally apply the part-time operator probationary points standard to full-time probationary employees.

When upper management was pressed to justify this inequity, they admitted they intentionally wanted to tighten the standards, saying that too many were "getting through probation." And that they viewed this as "a liability."

While probationary employees' employment is ultimately at their employer's will until they pass probation. Metro has long-established policies to guide its decisions on terminating probationary employees. Applying policy guidelines for terminating probationary employees rather than picking and choosing whom managers want to fire demonstrates the True North values that Metro claims to be guided by.

Recently, when upper management was presented with facts that they hadn't followed their probationary termination standards, they said the quiet part out loud, "We just don't want them back," and asserted their ability to fire at will regardless of policy.

Sadly, I must report that terminations that don't meet the just cause standard aren't limited to King County Metro. Kitsap Transit management is also jumping right to proposing terminations for things that, per their policies, call for applying progressive discipline, not termination.

Both Metro and Kitsap Senior management should stop blowing off our concerns and direct their managers to follow their contracts and policies, which include meeting the Just Cause standards before issuing discipline or terminations. Our union will react accordingly if need be.

The 1st Vice President's Report: Good Stuff

By Cory Rigtrup · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

It is an honor being appointed as your Vice-President. The shoes I must fill are mighty big. I'll do my best to wear them honorably.

I have been helping out during Ken's absences. Before that, I was helping out with bargaining and representation for more than a decade. I've been around fighting pretty much every fight I could, now it's time to pass on what I've learned to all.

When I first took office in 2018, on average our Local was representing two terminations a month. The last few months, we've seen that number increase to two a week. That is a lot of people losing their jobs. In that same time, we were able to see five members return, three from Kitsap Transit, two from King County. As invigorating as it is to see folks return, the ratio isn't healthy.

It is an exciting time where I am meeting some pretty smart and helpful people. The workload is overwhelming, but there are good people that are willing to help. I've run into some of these folks at the bases, and I appreciate the engagement into conversation. There are also good people that are willing to you, we call them stewards. We put their name on a list and post that list on your worksite's union board. Our Local provides these stewards with training and you give them the opportunity to learn and grow. Feel free to look them up and reach out.

Shop Stewards:

In February we held two shop steward classes, one for Rail, one for Bus. We kind of broke Rail by holding the class on a Saturday. Half of the members we sent union details were denied. I guess we live and learn. The next Rail class with be held in the middle of the week later this month for all those who were denied.

3rd Vice-President:

This month we are voting on the creation of another assistant business agent. The proposal caveats out representation for all the smaller properties and almost half of Rail. It also has a delayed implementation to coincide with our regular cycle of elections. I support the proposal.

The Vice President duty is to represent all members. The addition of the 3rd Vice President doesn't change that. What changes is the workload. The current workload is suffocating if our Local is to stay committed to providing stellar representation to members. With the addition of the 3rd Vice President, I speculate the workload of the Vice President reduces by 30-40%, at the same time increasing resources to the smaller properties.

Out Local has been utilizing Board Officers to help out and create exposure to membership issues. It does help. However, the weight and responsibility that members demand are best served by a full-time officer who has the day-to-day information and resources that will enable us to build our Local into something even stronger.

Come to anyone of the six union meetings in March. Be loud, be quiet, but vote.

Update, Ben-Damnati Arb:

Arbitrator awarded Ben-Damnati his job back in June, with a make whole remedy. Metro did not calculator overtime time earnings into lost wages. Currently, our Local is suing King County in Superior court for wage theft for refusing to pay the full award.

Update, Grant Arb:

Arbitrator awarded Grant his job back in July, with a make whole remedy. Metro did not calculator overtime time earnings into lost wages. Our Local has retained jurisdiction of the arbitrator and demanded a full award to include lost overtime earnings.

Thomas Arb:

March second and third is the arbitration for operator Melissa Thomas. Like most folks with sick kids, she ran into attendance issues. This will be a hard-fought arbitration, where we believe we can show that one, if not more, or the attendance marks should not have occurred. Settlement talks failed, so we gear up to through down. Stay tuned, arbitrations are like a crapshoot, you just never know.

If you see me around say hi. I like to help.

It's Nothing Personal…

By Jim Dimpsey · Page 8 · Open in the PDF

Most of you have by now noticed the unexplained plastic discs stuck on parts of our coaches. They're about three inch rounds and come in black or yellow, depending on where they're stuck. Look closely, and you'll see a word indented into the inner circle.

When I first saw them, I knew what they were immediately. But I was in a unique position. Twenty-two years ago I worked with someone who helped start the company behind those discs.

I worked a few other jobs as well as Metro back then, including one driving some pretty weird vehicles around Seattle. One of the mechanics at that other job, John, told us about a local freight hauling company that had a truck involved in an accident. No biggie, but when the State Patrol asked the driver for his inspection records, the driver (who was new) had no idea what they were.

Oops. The company was slapped with a fine.

You see, after every twelve hours of operation, someone must physically inspect commercial vehicles on the road and make sure they are safe and sound. This inspection must be documented, and that documentation kept onboard the vehicle and presented on demand. (I assume that since our vehicles are run close to home, the primary inspections are performed by Vehicle Maintenance, and inspection records kept locally, Metro has been given an exemption to the onboard record-keeping requirement.)

Someone at Oak Harbor Freight mulled over that fine. That someone was technically minded, and got an idea. He was also a friend of my coworker John. He and his partners (who hired John) developed an electronic inspection recording system for commercial vehicles. They called the new company Zonar.

That's the word indented on those little discs. Though it hasn't been officially introduced to drivers, I've heard enough corroborated rumors to know that Metro has signed a contract with Zonar, and that soon operators will be asked to use Zonar equipment to conduct their daily coach inspections.

Which raises a very, very serious problem.

You see, Zonar has technologically moved on from their humble beginnings two decades ago. The original electronic inspection reader was a custom-made yellow device. Today, Zonar uses a simple tablet computer.

A tablet. A large touch screen with a camera and some speakers. EXACTLY the kind of device Metro SPECIFICALLY PROHIBITS its drivers to use in the yard. Don't believe me? From The Manual, read section 10.06 on Personal Electronic Devices (PEDs). After listing what electronic devices can do, go to the second sentence, where you'll read: "These include, but are not limited to… tablets…."

Ah, but keep reading! The final sentences in the section reads: "Yard Policy: PEDs are also considered a hazard in base yards. Employees may not walk while using any PED."

So, I hope you are asking: how am I supposed to do a "walk"-around inspection of my coach "while using" the Zonar tablet, which definitely falls into the category of "any PED"?

I sadly expect management to hand-wave away the clear and present danger policy 10.06 was written to prevent. They might try to specifically train us to never "walk" while walking around with the device, but to comically stop at each inspection point and only then bring the device toward the inspection disc.

Or they might get even more convoluted. Since the rule was introduced, forbidden electronic devices were prefaced with the word "Personal." Maybe Metro will label Zonar tablets something other than "personal." Which is complete nonsense!

First of all, the section does not specify that prohibited PEDs are "personally owned" by the operator; rather, the section quite pointedly avoids the ownership issue, focusing only on what PEDs are. After all, what if you were caught using a PED and defended yourself by noting it was a device you borrowed from a friend? Shout all you want about the borrowed device not being "personal," I strongly suspect that you'll see that five days off without pay (or worse, if it's not your first PED rodeo).

Rather, even if they didn't mean to, it seems Metro quite wisely used the term "personal" to define the device as "on the operator's person" as well as one being used. So I see no exemptions in The Manual for Zonar tablets… well, nothing that passes the smell test.

Meaning when the Zonar rollout arrives, we drivers must insistently refuse to use these tablets in the yard simply because doing so is specifically noted in The Manual as forbidden. And the rule is there for a very good reason: as multiple psychological studies have confirmed, doing so creates a distraction and is therefore simply dangerous!

Yes! That prohibition is a very good idea! I personally agree with both the spirit and letter of rule 10.06 against the use of all electronic devices in the yard, personal or otherwise, simply because I regard my own safety has sacrosanct. I hope you agree.

Furthermore, I do hope our Union Officers and Executive Board members will back me and my safety-minded colleagues when it comes to insisting that Metro avoid imposing on its drivers any unsafe inspection regimens, especially when they violate Metro's own procedures.

Please, let's not let Metro tell us something incredibly stupid, like using a Zonar tablet in the yard "is nothing personal." Any compromise to our safety absolutely is.

Resolution No. 2026-01: Affirming Solidarity, Equality, and Mutual Respect and Condemning Racism, Islamophobia, Xenophobia, and Attacks on the LGBTQIA+ Community

Page 9 · Open in the PDF

A RESOLUTION AFFIRMING SOLIDARITY, EQUALITY, AND MUTUAL RESPECT AND CONDEMNING RACISM, ISLAMOPHOBIA, XENOPHOBIA, AND ATTACKS ON THE LGBTQIA+ COMMUNITY AGAINST MEMBERS AND COMMUNITIES REPRESENTED BY ATU LOCAL 587

WHEREAS, the Amalgamated Transit Union was established on the principles of unity, solidarity, and mutual aid among working people, and the ATU International Constitution affirms the equal dignity, rights, and protections of all members without distinction as to race, color, creed, religion, national origin, gender, gender expression, LGBTQIA+ community, or background; and

WHEREAS, ATU Local 587 represents a diverse membership that includes immigrants, refugees, asylum seekers, naturalized citizens, LGBTQIA+ people, and U.S.-born workers who collectively operate and maintain public transportation systems relied upon by the communities we serve; and

WHEREAS, a substantial portion of ATU Local 587 members work in public-facing and safety-sensitive positions, including bus and rail operations, supervision, and frontline service, where they are highly visible and frequently exposed to public interaction; and

WHEREAS, racism, Islamophobia, and xenophobia, including attacks on immigrant and refugee communities based on national origin, culture, language, religion, or perceived immigration status, LGBTQIA+ status undermine social cohesion and directly threaten the safety, dignity, and well-being of transit workers; and

WHEREAS, xenophobia and homophobia often manifests through collective blame and dehumanization of entire communities and does not distinguish between citizenship, immigration status, or legal standing, thereby placing all members associated with targeted communities at risk; and

WHEREAS, rhetoric originating from public officials or positions of authority carries significant influence and can normalize discriminatory behavior, embolden harassment, and create hostile conditions for transit workers who cannot disengage from the public while performing their duties; and

WHEREAS, Muslim members, particularly women who wear hijab or other religious attire, as well as immigrant members from historically targeted or war-torn regions, and the LGBTQIA+ communities experience heightened vulnerability during periods of increased fear-mongering and xenophobic and homophobic and transphobic rhetoric; and

They affect focus, mental health, and overall well being.

It mattered that Metro management acknowledged these realities and clearly communicated county policies that protect employees. The reminder that certain immigration related information cannot be shared without proper legal authority gave reassurance. It also mattered that ATU 587 leadership stood firm in defense of our members. When labor and management can sit at the same table and commit to dignity and fairness, it strengthens trust across the system.

February also carries deep meaning as Black History Month. That is why these conversations require honesty and awareness. Black History Month challenges all of us to look at how far we have come and how far we still need to go.

Open Bargaining — Strength Through Engagement

By Chuck Lare, Atlantic Base Shop Steward · Page 12 · Open in the PDF

For the first time in ATU 587 history, your union is negotiating our Metro contract with the doors wide open. This isn't happening in some back room where deals get cut behind closed doors. Open bargaining means you—every single member—can walk in, pull up a chair, and watch your contract being forged in real time.

This is your invitation. Your bargaining team needs you there.

Why This Matters

When management sits across the table from our negotiators, they need to see more than a handful of union reps. They need to see the workforce they're actually negotiating with—operators, mechanics, maintenance workers, the people who keep Metro running every single day. Your presence sends an unmistakable message: we're watching, we're engaged, and we're not accepting a raw deal.

Open bargaining strips away the mystery of contract negotiations. You'll witness firsthand what our team is fighting for and what obstacles they face in winning fair terms. You'll see management's tactics, their priorities, their pushback. You'll understand why certain battles take time and why solidarity matters.

Knowledge is power. When members understand how their contract gets made, they become stronger advocates for themselves and their coworkers. They see what's possible and what's at stake. They stop relying on rumors and start trusting what they've witnessed with their own eyes.

How We Got Here

I've been advocating for open bargaining since 2014, writing about its virtues in these pages. Credit where it's due: our ATU 587 President fought for and won Metro's agreement to open these negotiations to membership observation. That's a victory before we've even signed a contract.

This transparency isn't common. Many locals still negotiate behind closed doors, leaving members guessing about what happened until after the ink dries. We're doing something different because we believe our members deserve better.

The Contract Action Team

To maximize member engagement, ATU 587 has formed the Contract Action Team (CAT). This team exists for one purpose: getting members to these bargaining sessions. CAT members are reaching out, organizing attendance, and building momentum. They understand that open bargaining only works when members actually show up.

From CAT's efforts, we're seeing increased attendance at sessions. More importantly, we're building trust. When members see the process unfold transparently, they trust the outcome—whether it's a hard-won victory or a tough compromise. They know their negotiators gave everything because they watched it happen.

What You Need to Know

Before attending, review the Code of Conduct for Bargaining Observers at: https://atu587live.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/04153952/Code-of-Conduct.pdf

These guidelines ensure sessions remain productive while protecting everyone's ability to observe freely. They're straightforward: be respectful, stay quiet during negotiations, and understand you're there to witness, not participate directly.

To learn more about the power and mechanics of open bargaining, watch "Open Up Bargaining! News Guild Workshop" at: https://youtu.be/BcDht-BRPgQ?si=gzQ_fWFPkD0rDdD8

Your Move

Open bargaining only delivers its full strength when members engage. An empty observers' section tells management we don't care enough to show up. A full room tells them we're unified, informed, and serious.

Your bargaining team is fighting for your wages, your benefits, your working conditions, and your future. They're doing it in full view because they believe in transparency and member power. Now it's your turn.

Show up. Watch your contract being built. Put your eyes on management. Stand with your union.

This is our contract. These are our negotiations. This is our moment.

Structure Before Expansion: A Different Way to Think About VP #3

By Andy Price, Shop Steward, Local 587 · Pages 14–15 · Open in the PDF

There has been significant discussion about the proposal to add a 3rd Vice President. The workload concerns being raised are real. Contract enforcement is harder. Management behavior has deteriorated. Bargaining is more complex. No one disputes that.

At the same time, we should acknowledge something broader: our Local still operates largely on a governance model built in the 1800s — where structural decisions are made only by those physically present at a meeting. For routine matters that may be workable. For major, long-term financial commitments, it deserves reconsideration.

The question before us is not whether the work exists.

The question is how we structure ourselves to do that work effectively, proportionally, and sustainably.

What Our Structure Was Designed to Do

Under our bylaws and the ATU Constitution and General Laws, full-time officers are generally elected by the membership at large. That principle exists for a reason. It ensures that those who hold the highest authority in the Local are accountable to the entire membership — not just one craft or property.

This does not mean craft-specific positions can never exist. It does mean they should be created carefully and only when truly necessary.

That is also why we have an Executive Board of roughly twenty elected representatives and an established Shop Steward system. Those positions were designed to distribute work, engage membership talent, and prevent concentration of authority in too few hands.

The President of this Local is not only a presiding officer. The title reflects it clearly: President/Business Agent. The Vice Presidents serve as Assistant Business Agents. Their responsibility includes organizing, delegating, training, bargaining, and mobilizing the membership — not personally carrying every grievance.

If workload is overwhelming, the first structural question should be: Are we fully utilizing the officers and stewards we already have?

Capacity Already in Place

It is important to acknowledge the professional office staff we already have. Our office manager and staff handle substantial behind-the-scenes work — tracking grievances, managing documentation, coordinating scheduling, and maintaining operational continuity. Much of that work is not visible, but it is essential.

These are not political positions. They provide institutional knowledge and stability regardless of election cycles.

If enforcement capacity truly needs expansion, another option would be strengthening professional staff support — for example, adding a dedicated enforcement or grievance support position to assist with investigations, document preparation, research, and brief development across all classifications.

Unlike an elected Vice President, a professional staff position could have its duties adjusted as needs evolve and could support the entire membership without being tied to a specific electorate.

A Capacity Question — Not a Feelings Question

Other ATU locals of comparable or larger size, including those with rail divisions, operate with one Vice President. They rely heavily on trained stewards and Executive Board members to handle day-to-day enforcement and preparation.

That model is not about copying other locals. It is about using capacity intelligently.

We already have the ability to detail Executive Board members when workload spikes. That flexibility is built into our structure.

Instead of permanently expanding the officer structure, we could:

  • Detail Executive Board members during high-demand periods.
  • Invest more consistently in steward training and coordination.
  • Strengthen professional support staff.
  • Modernize participation tools to engage more members in real time.

These approaches increase capacity without locking the Local into long-term structural expansion.

The Financial Reality

A full-time officer is not just a salary. It includes benefits, pension contributions, and a vehicle allowance. At present, five full-time officers receive both salary and car allowance. Adding a sixth represents a substantial and ongoing financial commitment.

This is not a temporary adjustment. It is a structural expansion.

At a time when arbitration costs and budget considerations are often weighed carefully, we should be cautious about adding permanent fixed expenses that reduce flexibility elsewhere.

Financial stewardship is part of representation.

Proportionality, Accountability, and Participation

Our current structure allows the membership at large to choose its leadership. Full-time officers accountable to the entire Local must weigh priorities proportionally across all crafts and properties.

While our current structure already includes one craft-specific Vice President, expanding that model further represents a significant structural shift that deserves careful consideration..

Major structural and financial decisions should also involve the broadest possible participation. Our bylaws already require publication and notice. Allowing members to cast votes electronically on major bylaw proposals would modernize participation without eliminating in-person meetings. When long-term commitments affecting thousands are decided by a relatively small number present at a meeting, it is reasonable to ask whether we can do better.

Before expanding the number of elected offices, we should fully utilize the capacity already built into our system.

A strong union is not defined by how many titles it has. It is defined by how effectively it organizes the talent, experience, and commitment of its members.

For these structural, financial, and democratic reasons, I cannot support this proposal as written. I believe there are more effective and sustainable ways to strengthen enforcement and representation. For that reason, I will be voting no.
