The President's Report: The 3rd VP

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 16 · Open in the PDF

The membership voted 90% in favor of adding another Vice President at our March cycle of meetings. It's a historic day for our Union! The membership had a spirited debate on this proposal and spoke with their vote. Since some consistent concerns were raised during this process, I would like to take one last opportunity to revisit how we got here and the ramifications going forward.

Given that the membership at Rail was eventually going to bring forward a motion to add a VP to better represent Rail, the Union's Full-Time Officers have spent the last 20 months trying to reach a consensus on adding a 3rd VP. Whether we should add one, when to do so, whether we could afford it, and how to structure and distribute duties among the three VPs.

The Officers could all agree that two VPs were not adequately representing our rapidly growing membership of 4,800, and that we could afford another VP if the membership chose to add one. We could not all agree on the structure for adding another VP and on how to distribute duties among 3 VPs.

After spending a year and a half searching for the perfect structure, it became obvious that there was none. That any structure would have drawbacks and be opposed by some members. A majority of the Officers eventually settled on the structure that the membership just approved. While not perfect, a majority of the officers believed this was the best option for the membership as a whole.

Once we have the next elections our Vice President will be able to focus on and represent just one contract: Metro Bus operations. And the new Vice President position will focus on representing Rail operations and the other four properties. For those concerned that whoever is voted into that position will need to understand and represent 5 contracts and properties, including bus and rail, in King County and on the Peninsula.

That is the exact argument in favor of this change, because that's what the current Vice President is responsible for knowing and representing, and, on top of that, for overseeing 3,800 Metro Bus Operations members.

The ATUs International Constitution and General Laws require that at least one Vice President be elected by the membership at large. This is why everyone can run and vote for the Vice President.

Additionally, the ATUs International Constitution and General Laws allow for the creation of additional Vice President positions to represent designated subsections of the membership.

In 2009, when our local created the Vice President for Maintenance, the membership approved the bylaw change establishing the position, which only members from maintenance can run for and vote for.

Fairness is in the eyes of the beholder, and no matter which way you choose on this issue of who can vote and run for these added VP positions, some of the membership will feel that it's unfair.

If everyone can run and vote for the additional VP position, Rail operations, and the other properties members may feel like the odds are that a member from Metro Bus Operations will always be chosen to represent them. And if you limit running and voting for this position to Rail operations and the other properties, Metro Bus Operations members may feel excluded from the process.

There is no right or wrong answer on this. It's a matter of everyone's individual opinion and preference.

A choice needed to be made, though, and it was decided that these subsections of the membership would elect their VP from within their ranks to represent them.

Just like in 2015, when we created an additional VP for maintenance, now that the membership has voted to adopt this new bylaw change, it will be sent to the International to decide whether it is in alignment with its Constitution and General Bylaws, or if not they will send it back to our local to bring it into alignment, which is what happened in 2015. Our Local may find out that answer before or shortly after this article is printed, stay tuned.

If or when this bylaw change is approved, we'll need to start thinking about and planning for next year's officer election cycle, which isn't far off. Our Union leadership is committed to helping train and provide exposure to members who step forward and express interest in running for union office in the future.

The more members who run for full-time and E-board positions, the more choices the membership will have in selecting their next leaders.

Whether you agreed with this change or not, having worked side by side daily with our Vice President, first Ken, and now Cory, I can assure you that Metro Bus operations members will be much better represented as a result of this bylaw change. Both would tell you that and were both highly supportive of this change. The same goes for Rail and the other four properties represented by ATU 587.

Under any metric that you choose, there is no argument that can factually be made that our Local can't afford this additional VP. The questions and concerns expressed concern about what happens if we go from budget surpluses to deficits. The answer to that is we've been there before and made the necessary budget cuts when our reserve was around $100k.

Our Union is currently a $6 million-a-year operation. We'll find the cuts if disaster hits. But worrying about a potential disaster shouldn't keep us from investing in better representation for an expanding membership.

Lastly, on the suggestions that we should spend this money on restructuring to a model that details more members to assist the VPs. We need to, can, and will do both. We currently detail members practically daily to help out. And Vice President Cory Rigtrup is currently working on the structure of a rotating assistant-to-the-vice-president-type detailed position; stay tuned.

The 1st Vice President's Report: Wave of Restructuring

By Cory Rigtrup · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

Since being appointed, I've been in the trenches. Fighting the daily battle, one after another. It's relentless, consuming and at moments suffocating. But there are also times when I get to look up and see daylight. This daylight comes in the form of helping hands, that always seem to be offered. Let's focus on the light.

I was inspired last month to see such strong turnout at the membership meetings. Dozens of first-time union meeting attendees turned out to support increasing our Local's ability to provide representation. Way to see the small groups organize, message received!

March also consisted of getting more of our folks trained on accident re-reads and ARB. There are different groups of workers lacking trained people familiar with their work rules and equipment to step in when needed. Additionally, there is an overall shortage of trained folks available, and the process for selecting the union appointees seems off. Working on building a planned rotational system, hang tight, more to come on that.

Restructuring base representation:

If you've seen me at a worksite, it's usually because I was attending a grievance hearing or a termination and I loitered around trying to engage in positive relations with whoever wants to talk. One theme I've heard a few times, "Can we get a base meeting and get everyone on the same page?" A similar message was brought up during the membership meetings last month. The answer to both is YES!

Currently our Union's bylaw appoints each bus operating base their own executive board representative, plus a spare. The spare may change by the time the next executive board meeting takes place, as new base is about to go live. Think about that, each operating base has its own representative in union leadership to talk about your worksite issues, so let's utilize it!

About the time this article goes to print, a new shake has begun. Along with folks starting new work, a host of old and new shop stewards eager and willing to help will be made available. Shop stewards will elect their base chief steward, and the Union will take those names, plus your base's executive board officer and post for all to see.

Here is where things hopefully change. I believe it's good for all of us to empower your bases union representation team in getting better organized, and our Local provide more support at the base level. Shop stewards are responsible for defending members rights, bear witness, provide assistance and communicate. The base Chief steward is responsible for communicating their base members' needs with other stewards and request/provide assistance as needed. The base executive board officer is responsible for addressing their base needs and providing union leadership on location. Depending on which base you are at, the currently set up may be working, or be coming up short.

Maybe that is how it's supposed to work, maybe not, it makes sense in my head. As this work in progress rolls out, be proactive, be part of the build. The Local can say we empower your base's union leadership with what we think you need, but stewards taking part at the base level pounding the issue, helping you, is how we really grow. More to come, stay tuned.

Kitsap Transit:

My goodness, our Local is spending a lot of time lately dealing with Kitsap issues. Policy changes, memo's, lots and lots of discipline. Just an overall abundance of work, for a non-contract bargaining year. It's clear something has changed.

There is profound rubble in my soul; to see so many good Kitsap members rise to these challenges. Last month I saw at least 20% of all our Kitsap members attend our Saturday meeting. Make their voices heard, tell us what is going on, and break bread together! This participation gives credence to our Local's solidarity for each property within. We are currently working on setting up a steward training class for April, stay tuned.

Mellissa Thomas Arb:

The Thomas arbitration was scheduled for March 2 and 3, a last second reinstatement agreement was reached the day before arbitration. This Agreement reinstates Sister Thomas back as a Metro Operator, under terms that will allow her to succeed! Let us all welcome Sister Thomas back to work!

Brother Searcy:

Searcy was a north base operator terminated for refusing a post-accident drug test by being transported to Harborview by ambulance. Metro did the right thing by admitting their mistakes and making brother Searcy whole. Welcome back Brother Searcy!

The Maintenance Vice President's Report: Bus Facilities Maintenance and Bus Vehicle Maintenance

By Jeff Stambaugh · Page 5 · Open in the PDF

Bus Facilities Maintenance

Will the drama ever end? Apparently not. TFD management continues to erode seniority and destroy employee morale. As of this writing management has not posted or conducted the pick, this is now affecting lives. Our members and their employees have things to plan, like vacations and childcare, only they can't because TFD management has failed to do their job. As I reported last month, they have proposed positions outside the collective bargaining agreement. The more I learn the more upsetting it gets. One of the largest classifications in TFD is called Utility Laborer, their main job is to clean the Bus Zones and make them presentable to our customers. In 2019 there were 43 pick positions for this job classification, 6 of these were marked "Usually shelter maintenance as assigned--see supplemental". Basically, what that meant was you came into work and were assigned that day's work based on need for the day, similar to a report operator. For the most part the other 37 picked positions had known work to pick, basically you knew what you were going to do nearly every day, part of knowing your picked work was a route sheet. The route sheet (like a run card) spelled out the location of bus stops you would clean and how often you would clean them. Now, the proposed pick sheets have 46 pick positions with 27, yes you read that correct, 27 "Usually shelter maintenance as assigned--see supplemental". Why would you need 27 of these you ask? Well, I did and TFD management gave me a song and dance about the ability to get the work done and lack of equipment to do it. The reality? The route sheets haven't been updated in over a decade, and management doesn't seem to be able to figure out how get the work done in a timely, consistent, cost-effective manner. Instead, they want to be able to direct the workforce to run around and service the stops that are not being serviced frequently enough because the route sheets haven't been updated. Would operations expect over 50% of the workforce to come into work and be assigned work daily?

The Union has made management a proposal that in our opinion will both get the work done and restore seniority. Management's response? That doesn't work for us.

Hopefully by the time you read this we have reached an agreement that works for everyone.

Bus Vehicle Maintenance

As most of you are aware we just concluded the Spring VM pick, it was going smooth until the final day when management announced Superintendent promotions and location changes, right behind that announcement the chiefs had a move-up with considerable movement causing frustration that employees could now be working with a chief or superintendent they intentionally avoided during the pick. The Union immediately asked for a repick and was denied. Hopefully in the future we can find a way to get management movement announced prior to the pick or find a workable solution that doesn't leave employees upset.

Rail Maintenance was fairly quiet last month, Hopefully that is good sign.

The Recording Secretary's Report: Workers Comp, Short Term Disability, and Long-Term Disability

By Latrelle Gibson · Page 7 · Open in the PDF

An injury that is severe enough that one will need to take time off work to heal can most certainly be an extremely stressful and scary world to navigate. I truly understand the stress related to Workers Comp not approving physical therapies, necessary injections, or surgery. I am here to help you navigate this world and try to take some of the stress off your shoulders. I answer on average 8 member calls a day for this very issue alone. In this article, I will walk you through the basics of both on the job and off the job injury.

For those who have an OJI (on the job injury), FMLA time runs concurrently with your L&I leave to offer leave protection while out on an OJI. What does this mean and how does this work? For FMLA, one has the ability to take protected leave for up to 12 weeks per year, granted that each in preceding 12 months a full-time employee works at least 1,040 hours, and for part-time, this will be 510 hours. KCFMLA will kick in once one is out beyond the 12 weeks, this leave allows a total of 18 weeks of protected leave within a twelve-month period. Now, keep in mind that if you've used FMLA within the same twelve months of you being injured, this will be part of the 12 weeks. For example, in March 2025, I used four weeks of FMLA for family reasons. In July 2025, I was injured on the job. I will now have 8 weeks of FMLA for the rest of 2025.

When you're out on Workers Comp, for the first 260 workdays, we have bargained in our contract for metro to pay a supplemental. This supplemental keeps you on the payroll, therefore not affecting medical benefits for the first 260 days. The supplemental payment reduces every 60 workdays missed, so the first 60 workdays missed, one is paid 100% supplemental, the following 60 workdays missed, 90% supplemental, and the next 140 workdays missed, 80% supplemental. It's important to note that you will still receive time loss payments from Workers Comp at 60% of your wages. 60% will come from Workers Comp and this is not taxed, however, the supplemental payments are taxed. Please make sure your address is up to date as the Workers Comp check will most likely be mailed to your address on file.

Once your supplemental payments are exhausted, if you're still out on OJI for more than 30 days, your benefits may be at risk of turning off. The Benefits department will contact the individual and ask if the individual would like to utilize their accruals, e.g., vacation, AC time, to keep them benefit eligible. This is completely up to the individual. If one elects not to utilize their accruals, you'll be without benefits. Transit Disability Services can steer you to the open market to get benefits through COBRA or Apple health. I don't suggest COBRA as those are the benefits we currently have, and they're very expensive when paying for individually.

Now the stressful part (although all of it can be stressful for some), if you're going through recovery, and Workers Comp denies the number of physical therapies your doctor has asked for, denied the injections needed, or even denied surgery, your doctor will need to appeal this decision. If your doctor is siding with Workers Comp, this will be an uphill battle for you. I would strongly suggest finding another doctor to get a second opinion and if the you're able to find another doctor who agrees with you that your injury was in fact work related, the doctor will need to communicate this with the initial doctor who is siding with the Workers Comp decision, as Workers Comp needs the initial doctor to make this appeal on your behalf. If the doctor is filing appeals, and treatment is still being denied, contact me and I can help with this.

Medical Separation. If you've run out of protected leaves, FMLA/KCFMLA, Transit disability services will ask the base Superintendent/Chief to extend leave as "an accommodation". Bases will extend these leaves so long as you have a projected return to work date in the near future. If your doctor states you're not able to return to the job of injury for an indefinite period, Metro will seek medical separation sooner. The ultimate decision lies with your doctor and what they report to Transit Disability Services. If you've been out on Workers Comp for a year or more, give me a call and I can walk you through the process. There's a lot to explain with this process, so a phone conversation is best.

I hope this helps some understand the Workers Comp world, and if you want to look this information up, all the Workers Comp components are located in the CBA under Article 12.8.A.

Congested Tracks and Compressed Training: The Struggle for New Rail Operators

By Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs, ATU 587/King County Labor Delegate · Pages 8–9 · Open in the PDF

Rail has been growing quickly, and the demand for manpower continues to increase. As service expands and more trains are added to the system, the need for qualified Light Rail Operators grows with it. In many cases, the shortage has reached a point where supervisors are stepping in to operate trains themselves just to maintain service levels. That alone shows how much pressure the system is under.

To address the shortage, rail has accelerated hiring and is currently running two training classes per day to bring in new operators as quickly as possible. On paper, that sounds like progress. But the reality on the ground is more complicated. The pace of hiring has increased, yet the training environment has become far more difficult for students who are trying to learn the job.

The mainline is already heavily congested. With trains running on roughly a four-minute headway, there is very little room for error and very little opportunity for trainees to practice in a controlled environment. New LRO students are trying to learn a complex job while operating in one of the busiest conditions the system has ever seen. Every movement on the line must stay on schedule, and controllers are focused on keeping service moving. That pressure naturally carries over to the trainers and the students.

For trainees, this can create a stressful learning environment. Instead of having the space and time to develop confidence behind the controls, many students feel rushed. Trainers are balancing two responsibilities at the same time: teaching a new operator while also making sure the train stays on schedule and does not disrupt the flow of service. When the system is running at maximum capacity, training can easily take a back seat to keeping the line moving.

The impact of these conditions can be seen in the graduation numbers. Success rates vary widely from class to class. In some classes of 11 to 15 students, only four or five make it through to graduation. In other classes with similar numbers, 11 or even 12 students complete the program successfully. When outcomes swing that dramatically, it raises legitimate questions about consistency in training, evaluation standards, and the environment students are learning in. It also raises the question of whether students are being given a fair and equal opportunity to succeed.

Another concern is the reduction in on-the-job training hours. In the past, trainees received about 80 hours of hands-on operating experience before completing the program. Today that number has been reduced to 64 hours. That means new operators are getting less real-world practice at a time when the system itself has become more demanding and more congested. Those lost hours matter. Operating a train safely and confidently takes repetition, experience, and time behind the controls.

Learning the system involves much more than just moving a train from one station to another. Students must learn signal systems, station procedures, communication protocols, passenger safety, incident response, and how to operate in unusual or emergency situations. These are skills that develop through practice, not just classroom instruction. When hands-on training time is reduced, students may graduate with less confidence and less experience than operators in the past.

At the same time, trainers are under pressure to move classes through the system quickly in order to fill staffing gaps. That pressure can create uneven expectations and evaluations. Some trainers may allow students more time to develop their skills, while others may apply stricter standards under tighter timelines. The result is inconsistency from class to class.

If rail service continues to grow, training must grow with it. Expanding service without strengthening the training structure will only create more pressure on new operators, trainers, and the system as a whole. Adequate hands-on training time, consistent evaluation standards, and a training environment that allows students to learn without excessive operational pressure are essential.

Rail operators carry enormous responsibility. They move thousands of passengers every day and operate within a system where safety and precision are critical. Preparing them properly is not optional. It is an investment in safety, reliability, and the long-term success of the rail system. When new operators are given the time, support, and environment they need to succeed, the entire system benefits.

Representation Must Keep Pace with a Changing Transit System

Congratulations to Rail and the smaller properties for having the option to elect a Vice President Three dedicated to your needs, while Vice President One focuses fully on Transit Operators. That structure creates balance and gives different parts of our membership the representation they need. In many ways, this is a win for everyone.

Over the last few weeks there has been a lot of discussion about the proposal to establish a Vice President position focused on operations, particularly rail. Members have raised questions about cost, voting eligibility, and whether the position is necessary. These are fair questions, and every member deserves clear information before casting their vote.

But before we get lost in the details, it helps to step back and look at the bigger picture. Our union has grown and changed, and our leadership structure must evolve with it.

ATU Local 587 represents workers across many different properties and systems. Our membership includes King County Metro bus operators, Link Light Rail operators, Streetcar workers, and members from Kitsap Transit, Jefferson Transit, Clallam Transit, and other agencies across the region. Each of these systems operates under different contracts, rules, and workplace conditions.

One area in particular has grown rapidly over the past decade: rail. Link Light Rail continues to expand across the region. Lines from Lynnwood, to Eastside, and Federal Way. With each expansion comes more operators, controllers, maintenance staff, and supervisors. Rail is no longer a small part of our union. It is a major and growing division within our membership.

Rail operations also come with technical challenges that are very different from bus operations. Rail members work under a separate collective bargaining agreement and operate in an environment with its own safety systems and procedures.

These are not abstract concerns. They are real situations that affect real members. As rail continues to grow, so does the number of grievances, investigations, and disciplinary cases involving rail operators and other rail employees. Expecting the same leadership structure that existed years ago to keep up with a rapidly expanding rail system is becoming increasingly difficult.

This is why many members believed the time has come to establish a Vice President position dedicated to rail operations. The goal is not to divide the union or create unnecessary positions. The goal is to ensure that members working in a complex and growing system have representation from leadership that understands their work environment, while allowing other union officers to focus on the needs of the rest of the membership. Strong unions adjust when the industry changes. Our transit system is changing, and our union must be able to keep up.

Some members have raised concerns about the cost of adding another position. Those concerns are understandable. But representation always requires resources, and the cost of inadequate representation can be far greater. When a member faces discipline, contract violations, or unfair treatment, having leadership that understands the job can make the difference between protecting a career and losing it.

Others have pointed out that the change would not take effect until 2027 and therefore does not solve immediate problems. But structural decisions are always made with the future in mind. The rail system will continue expanding for years to come. Preparing now ensures that the union is ready to represent the workforce that is still growing.

At the same time, this conversation should not turn into a conflict between different parts of our membership. Bus operators, rail operators, streetcar employees, and members at other properties all face different challenges. Our strength as a union comes from recognizing those differences while standing together.

Operators' Empathy for All: Because We Deserve Better Too

By Chuck Lare, Atlantic Base Shop Steward · Page 10 · Open in the PDF

Let us be honest with each other for a minute.

If you have driven a bus in King County for any length of time, you have been there. End of the line. A passenger who clearly is not okay — maybe they are in crisis, maybe they have not slept in days, maybe they just need help that none of us were trained to give. And what happens? You call it in. A supervisor shows up, or fare security, or a deputy. The passenger gets walked off your bus. And two hours later, they are back on somebody else's.

Nothing gets resolved. Nobody gets helped. And you are left carrying the weight of it for the rest of your shift.

That is not good enough. Not for our riders, and not for us.

That is why I want to talk to you about Operators' Empathy for All — an initiative that could genuinely change how we handle these situations and make our working conditions safer and less stressful in the process.

The idea is straightforward: partner Metro operators with King County Behavioral Health Specialists who are actually trained to de-escalate, treat, and connect people in crisis to real services. Not shuffle them off to the next bus. Programs like CAHOOTS in Oregon and STAR in Denver have already proven this model works — fewer 911 calls, less reliance on emergency rooms, and fewer repeat situations clogging the system.

For us, that means fewer of those tense, unresolved end-of-line moments that eat at you. It means having the right backup — people trained for exactly this — instead of hoping a deputy arrives in time or a supervisor knows what to do. It means focusing on what we actually signed up for: moving people safely through this county.

There could also be an opportunity here for operators to take on additional hours outside of driving — engaging directly in expanding Behavioral Health Specialists' reach into our communities. This initiative is not just about making our shifts more manageable. It is about recognizing that we are on the front lines every single day, and we deserve the tools and support that match the reality of this job.

King County's own executive priorities "Breaking the Cycle," "Boots on the Ground," putting the right people in the right places — line up directly with what this initiative is pushing for. We have a real opening to press leadership to turn that rhetoric into something we actually feel on the bus.

We have always shown up for our community. It is time the system showed up for us.

If Operators' Empathy for All sounds like something you want to be part of, reach out: chuck@lareasssoc.com

Membership Meetings, Transparency, and Representation

By Andy Price, Shop Steward, Local 587 · Page 12 · Open in the PDF

Membership meetings are where the democratic life of our union is supposed to take place. They are where members raise concerns about working conditions, discuss contract issues, debate proposals, and vote on the direction of the Local. When members take the time to attend a meeting, they should be able to expect that the meeting allows meaningful participation and that the procedures being followed are clear and transparent.

Recently, a number of members have raised concerns about how some meetings are being conducted and how certain votes are handled. These concerns are not about personalities or about taking sides on any particular proposal. They are about making sure the process works the way members expect it to.

One concern raised by members who attended the most recent meeting was how little of the meeting was devoted to regular union business. From what was described by those present, the meeting moved very quickly to the bylaw proposal under consideration, the vote was taken, and shortly afterward the meeting was adjourned. Members who attempted to raise other issues reported that they were not given the opportunity to do so.

Membership meetings exist so that members can bring forward concerns affecting their jobs, their contracts, safety issues, and other union matters. When a meeting becomes focused almost entirely on a single item of business and then ends immediately after that vote, members understandably question whether the meeting served its broader purpose.

Parliamentary procedure exists to help ensure fairness in situations like this. Our bylaws recognize Robert's Rules of Order as the authority for questions of procedure when they are not otherwise addressed. One of the basic principles in Robert's Rules is transparency in voting. Once ballots are counted, the chair normally announces the result of the vote to the assembly, including the number of votes for and against the motion and whether the motion is adopted or lost.

That practice exists for a simple reason. When members attend a meeting and participate in a vote, they reasonably expect to hear the outcome once the tally has been completed.

Another question that has come up concerns voting eligibility during meetings. At one meeting I personally observed individuals arriving and handing in paperwork at the door before participating in the vote. I did not examine the documents, but they appeared to be membership-related forms. I also observed a full-time officer coming over to collect those forms.

To be clear, I am not suggesting any improper motive. However, situations like this can raise understandable questions among members about how eligibility is determined. Normally members sign in when attending a meeting and verify their eligibility before voting. When someone submits paperwork during the meeting and then participates in the vote in the same voting cycle, members may reasonably wonder how those procedures are being applied.

Clarifying these procedures would help ensure that members have confidence in the fairness and transparency of the process.

Representation within the union is another subject that often comes up in discussions about governance. Many members may not realize that while our Executive Board includes a number of representatives, many of those positions are not full-time. At the same time, the traditional full-time officer positions recognized in the ATU Constitution and General Laws—such as President and Vice President—are typically elected at large by the membership and represent the entire Local.

There is a reason for that structure. Officers elected at large are accountable to the entire membership rather than to a single department or classification. That approach encourages broader representation and helps ensure that decisions are made with the interests of the entire Local in mind.

Different locals structure their leadership positions in different ways depending on their size and needs. Some locals have additional vice presidents elected at large, while others create positions tied to specific departments or groups of members. These are reasonable topics for members to discuss, and thoughtful discussion about representation can help determine what structure best serves the membership as a whole.

Open discussion about procedure, transparency, and representation should not be viewed as criticism. It is simply part of a healthy union. When members understand how meetings are conducted, how votes are counted, and how decisions are made, confidence in the union's democratic process grows.

Our membership meetings should always reflect those principles.

Looking ahead, I would also encourage the Local to consider modernizing how bylaw proposals are voted on. Rather than relying only on a small number of meeting votes during a cycle, bylaws could be submitted to the membership for ratification by vote, still requiring the same two-thirds threshold to adopt a change. Allowing members to vote at or near the bases where they work would likely increase participation and awareness of union governance. In addition, we should seriously consider providing informational meetings online where members can hear explanations, participate in discussion, and debate proposals before voting. In 2026, secure online voting technology exists and is used by many organizations. It is probably inevitable that unions will move in that direction eventually. If the goal is to strengthen participation and engagement among our members, exploring those options sooner rather than later may be worth considering.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 5

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
