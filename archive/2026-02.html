The President's Report: My Response to Minneapolis

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 24 · Open in the PDF

Both personally and in my capacity as President of ATU Local 587 I stand in solidarity with ATU Local 1005 and their membership in Minneapolis as they deal with the effects of the large deployment of ICE and its current tactics in their community.

Just like our Local they have a large segment of members who are legally living and working in this Country but must now live in fear of possibly being stopped, detained, deported solely based on their appearance or accent.

Union members do and should expect their Union to support them when their employment is put in doubt, and their safety on the job is placed at risk. Union officers work for the members and should always represent their interests and wishes to the best of our ability and judgement. Many of our members are asking where the support, preparation, and direction from both their employer and their Union is in case Seattle becomes the next Minneapolis?

The Union has set up a meeting with senior Metro management and the members calling for support to hear their concerns and experiences. And to develop plans and guidance going forward.

Up until now, the Union, Metro, and the County have shared the same concerns about not taking actions that could draw attention from ICE to our diverse workforce and the potential negative effects on our members. We're now well past the point of remaining silent without direction or plans.

This is solely about Unionism and collectively standing up for, protecting, and fighting for fellow Union members and NOT about politics, or which side of our nation's bitter political divide that you stand on or identify with. A harm to one is harm to all!

Our Local, through our members' voluntary donations to COPE, has a long history of supporting politicians and initiatives that support public transit, our local, and Unions in general. We do NOT use members' dues for this or support or represent a political party or a party's agenda.

Our COPE committee sends its recommendations on elections to the E-board and, subsequently, to the membership, based on interviews with politicians. The membership has the final say on these recommendations, and it's just a recommendation for members to know who the COPE committee believes will be more supportive of Transit and Unions. It's NOT the Union telling you who to vote for, or which party to support. It's NOT the Union attempting to represent your political point of view for you.

Please consider donating to or increasing your contribution to COPE. This Union has significant political clout on local transit funding and laws that directly affect our jobs and working conditions, thanks to our long history of endorsements. A portion of your donation goes to the International COPE committee, which lobbies our interests with national and state politicians on transit funding, laws, bills, and initiatives.

You could not like, agree with, or participate in our system of politics/lobbying and still recognize the need for your Union to advocate for and protect your interests within the system we are forced to deal with.

We need to be players in this game to have our voices heard, our power felt, and our interests properly represented. It has historically worked out very well for this Union to do so.

New and future generations of ATU 587 members must learn about and choose to participate in COPE if we want to maintain the political clout that we've spent decades building through voluntary member donations to COPE. It's everyone's responsibility who understands this to talk with their peers about COPE and its importance. It's your Union; it requires the membership's active participation to remain viable and strong.

The 3rd VP

You'll see in this News Review a bylaw proposal to add a third Vice President. The Full Time Officers have spent the last year and a half trying to find the perfect formula for adding an additional VP for this local and there just isn't a perfect solution for everyone. We are pushing forward with what we believe is the best solution that is a win-win for all members. With now 4,800 active members two Vice Presidents are not sufficient to properly deal with the amount of work thrown at them on a daily basis.

If we had a VP for all of rail including VM it would take too much work away from VP two, go back to a VP representing both operations and VM, and not lighten the load of VP 1 enough.

With a new VP representing Rail Operations and our other four properties it is a perfect balance of total members for VP 3, and lightens the load of VP 1 which allows VP 1 to Just focus on Metro Bus Operations which is a net positive for those members.

The new VP can now focus on Rail Operations and our four other properties that ATU 587 represents. This VP will be voted on and come from these groups. For those who are concerned about a VP needing to have knowledge of Rail, the Peninsula properties and Solid Ground my counter is that is exactly what VP 1 has had to do all of these years, along with handling the thousands of members in King County Metro operations. Should the membership approve this bylaw proposal All non Vehicle Maintenance members of this Union will be represented by a VP with a much lower workload that can devote more timely attention to each members issues.

Farewell and good luck to Ken

I wanted to take this last opportunity to again thank Brother Ken Price for his decades of service to the public, the union, and our members. I always knew that Ken was passionate about defending our membership, but watching it firsthand for the last year and a half showed me how deeply personally he took each heavy-handed discipline or termination that our members faced. Congratulations, and enjoy your well-earned and deserved retirement, brother Price!

"It is not length of life but depth of life" ~ Ralph Waldo Emerson

We are saddened to announce the passing of retired transit operator Vicente "Vince" Caceres #544, on Monday January 5, 2026. Vince was born in 1941 in the Philippine Islands and immigrated with his family to Seattle in 1953 at the age of 12. He graduated from Ballard High School in 1959, served for 6 years in the National Guard, and worked at Boeing for 5 years before starting his transit career with Seattle Transit in 1971. He operated the monorail and drove bus routes out of Mercer Base, Jefferson Base, Ryerson Base, and then finished his career working out of North Base until his retirement in 2003. Vince is survived by his wife of almost 59 years, Leslie. His two children, daughter and North Base operator Kim Stark, son Mike Caceres, Operator/First Line Supervisor/Senior Scheduler. He enjoyed 22 years of retirement, spending his time gardening, golfing, long walks, trips to the casino as well as Disney trips with his family. Vince will be greatly missed by his Family and friends. A Memorial service will be held on February 4th, at 1:00 pm at Floral Hills Funeral home.

It is with great sadness that we announce the passing of transit operator Larry McGhee. Larry started his transit career in July 2001 and spent most of his time working part time out of South base. He will be greatly missed by his loving family, coworkers, and the passengers on his regular work who were happy to have him as their driver. There are no services planned at this time.

We are deeply saddened to announce the sudden passing of transit operator Abdullahi Mohamoud. Abdullahi started his transit career as a part-time operator in November 2022, then promoted to full-time in August 2023. He enjoyed working out of the downtown bases and serving his community. Abdullahi will be greatly missed by his loving father, First Line Supervisor, Osman Said, his loving family and supportive community, and all who had the pleasure of interacting with him. Services for Abdullahi have taken place at the All-Muslim Cemetery on Friday, January 9.

The Maintenance Vice President's Report: Discipline, Outsourcing, and Grievance Update

By Jeff Stambaugh · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

Discipline

This month we continued to be busy with more final determination letters with attached discipline, we have now moved into the grievance process for several of the members who were issued discipline.

Outsourcing

It seems The Union might need to put on a class to all of Bus and Rail Maintenance what outsourcing means and when to call The Union. As of this writing I have several more reports of Bus Facilities management taking it upon themselves to call vendors to do Local 587 Union work even after several previous grievances were filed and resolved in the Unions favor, yet somehow it continues to be a thing. Rail Maintenance management has apparently decided they want to test the limits of outsourcing without Union involvement by outsourcing seat replacement that has previously been done by our 587 members and not to be left out, Bus Maintenance decided to call a vendor to "recover" a coach in a ditch after our members had safety concerns over the recovery, don't get me wrong I'm all in for doing things safely and correctly, I'm not about sending 587 Union work out the door without an understanding from The Union! In all of these situations Metro Failed to notify or inform The Union until after the fact, if at all.

Grievance Update

Currently there are 58 open grievances in all of Maintenance, these generally fall under one of two main categories, discipline or contract violations. Discipline is usually pretty easy to define, it includes things like, written reminders, suspensions, and terminations. While contract violations are an element in discipline, contract violations are generally defined around issues such as, OT was not assigned per the CBA, pick wasn't posted properly, promotions didn't follow the CBA, or ATU work was outsourced. There has been some positive movement from management to resolve some issues in a timely manner that is in the best interest for all involved, there is lots of room for more improvement and better ways to find resolution that works for everyone.

As your elected VP I would rather spend my time finding better ways to improve your working conditions (think contract negotiations), than spending enormous amounts of time and resources fighting over things like outsourcing and heavy-handed discipline. I promised to fight for you and I will, however, Management, You can do better!

If you're not going to be at pick, please indicate your preferred vacation periods on the absentee form. Please note that the Union does not pick vacations, this is done via the vacation pick room. You will want to contact Transit Disability Services and/or your Chief to let them know you're cleared to return to work. Thank you all for taking the time to read all of this information. I'm here to help and make your pick easier, so please feel to reach out to me if you need any assistance. Happy pick and see you all at pick!

Alternate Rail Schedules and Their Impact on Frontline Staff

By Barbara Pastores and Muhammad Ehsan (ATU 587 Schedulers) · Page 7 · Open in the PDF

For Operators, a schedule is not an abstract idea. It is the foundation of the workday. It determines when they work, when they rest, how they are paid, and how they plan life beyond the cab. To understand why recent disruptions have been so difficult, it is important to understand how Link Light Rail schedules are created, changed, and put into practice; and what can be done to improve the process with fairness, clarity, and compassion.

At its core, train run times are developed through engineering standards, field testing, safety requirements, and historical performance data. Run times define how long it should take a train to travel from one point to another under normal conditions. Sound Transit develops running times and shares them with King County Metro Scheduling staff as part of each service change package.

Using the service change package as a guide, Schedulers build vehicle schedules taking into consideration headways, terminal recovery time, and systemwide operating limits. Together, these elements form the underlying schedule, which becomes the backbone for Operator duties. Duties are then created to align with the current Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA), which sets rules for maximum spread, break frequences and durations, and pay protections. For planned service changes (Shakeups), this system provides predictability and fairness. However, things become far more complicated when the system is constantly changing during a service change.

Planned maintenance, infrastructure upgrades, and system expansion are necessary investments in a reliable future. However, each of these projects can require changes to service patterns. When any segment of rail network is affected, the underlying schedule shifts, and Operator duties shift with it.

These changes can escalate quickly. Single track operations extend run times. Track closures introduce bus bridges. A station closure may force a new fallback location with limited facilities. What may appear on paper as a two-hour closure late in the day can completely reshape an Operator's entire shift.

For Operators, these disruptions are not just operational. They are personal. Sudden duty changes can interfere with childcare plans, second jobs, and rest cycles. Repeated, short-notice adjustments create uncertainty and fatigue, especially when they occur back-to-back. Operators are not resisting change. They are absorbing it, often quietly and often at personal cost.

The real question is not whether disruptions will happen. They will. The question is how they are managed. There are clear steps that can lead to a better path forward.

First, project planning timelines that require schedule changes need clearer structure and expectations. The current Intergovernmental Agreement (IGA) and CBA do not include sufficient language addressing schedule changes during a service change. If scheduling receives change requests well in advance, modifications can be completed earlier, and impacts can be communicated sooner. Clear and timely communication allows Operators to plan, adjust, and make informed decisions.

Clear and timely communication also helps First Line staff prepare for the day of disruption. This can create opportunities for voluntary sign-ups, swaps, or other flexible solutions that reduce stress and confusion.

Second, there must be clearer definitions of what truly qualifies as an emergency. Not every disruption qualifies as an emergency. Clearly defining emergency work builds transparency and trust.

Third, emergency work must be fairly compensated. When genuine emergencies require short notice changes, Operators should be paid accordingly. Recognition in pay matters just as much as recognition in words.

Finally, the strength of the workforce lies in its union. When expectations, protections, and processes are clearly written into agreements, they are implemented. When they are not, work still gets done, often at the expense of frontline Operators and First-Line Supervisors. Putting solutions in writing is not about rigidity. It is about respect. It ensures consistency, accountability, and fairness, even during disruptions.

None of this should overshadow one essential truth. Operators and First Line Supervisors have performed extraordinarily well under extraordinarily difficult conditions. They have adapted to constant change, navigated unfamiliar service patterns, and delivered reliable service despite ongoing disruptions. Their professionalism has kept the system moving. They deserve not only gratitude, but systems and agreements that truly honor their effort.

A Man Named Ken

By Cory Rigtrup, VM Board Rep · Page 8 · Open in the PDF

Like fire, this guy with slicked back hair walked into my life. Now he is gone like wind over the sheets. Love you Ken Price, keep living till the air leaves your lungs.

I first met Ken over a decade ago. He presented as a cleaned up, blue collar heavy hitting union steward. Quick with the lip, and sly enough to get you to agree with him. At that time, Ken had a reputation for being likable, hardworking, emotionally charged and popular. Destiny collapsed our circles, where I learned the strategic and manic side of Ken, which of course is nature's building blocks for genius.

Ken possesses several priorities that I value, family, love, fighting and money. He also displays a few behaviors that masquerade as unpredictable, erratic or surprise. That is the beauty of Ken Price, loyal as hell, but don't know what he's going to do.

Ken ran his political campaigns the same way I did, by reputation. He would later tell me that he ran for president because he wanted to help people, and why not he had the hair. In 2018 when we started sharing ridiculous hours, I would grow to learn he still supported people who disagree with him and refused to talk bad about them, even in private.

I remember the summer of 2019, killing time with him in Port Townsend. Discussing lessons learned from childhood and how "mom's" teachings should filter into a contract. Acceptance, repentance and redemption. Then strategize about the next few plays on how to make it happen. Years later, those childhood lessons of how people should be treated when mistakes are made, have filtered into many contacts and many jobs have been saved.

I also remember a time when our Local built coffins and marched in Seattle, only to be marched by a Me-2-protest. It was cool, we had a marching band, Ken spoke like a bad-ass, it was so much fun!

Shortly after came a couple hundred layoff, followed by a couple hundred vaccination termination. Ken stated this Local is going to fight, every member gets representation. He knew the vaccinations would bear a heavy polarizing cost, Ken kept silent on his personal views and fought through the ugliness as a president should. That part of Ken I truly admired, when the chips are down, just keep swinging.

I sat in countless meetings with Ken, in every property. He would often reserve speaking and let others pound the position. His method of operation was to listen, reserve judgment, and make decisions later, often after his walks. However, in those meetings, when things got tough, he would revert back to how this affects the operator in the seat. Ken would become emotional, language aggressive, voice sharpen, and he would start overtalking everyone until he had enough, then blast out of there like a dynamo. This mania was Ken's true power, it was awesome! Because inevitably the phone would ring, the answer was always the same, "I don't know where Ken is at, but he's pissed, so you better change your position."

What I will miss most about Ken is our early morning talks. Both of us would come in before the office opened to focus on certain things before the drudgery of work pulled us away. Always positive, we would talk about our families and run through life goals. When the conversations switched to work, we would talk strategy. He would float ideas of what he wanted to do, and I would layout out the pieces that needed to be on the table. It was jovial, like two kids planning to rob a candy store. These scheming conversations went on for years, and when it switched to money or benefits, the electricity was incredible. Ken wanted our Local to be number one, both in wages and benefits. Ken fought above our Local's weight and delivered!

Ken's exit strategy is true to his nature, unpredictable. But then again, is it? 65 years old, house in Neveda, hide your cards and play your game. Love you Ken Price, thank you for all the memories!

Letter to the Editor: Stand Up for Our Immigrant Members

By Marcelo Albernaz · Page 8 · Open in the PDF

Dear Editor,

On December 3rd, 2025, our nation's president referred to Somalians as "garbage."

I understand that our Union tries to stay politically neutral, but I believe we have the obligation to protect our members in every unjust situation. Safety Reporting Is a Right — Not "Unauthorized Overtime"

By Andy Price, Shop Steward, Local 587 · Page 9 · Open in the PDF

Safety in our workplace does not happen by accident. It happens because workers speak up, document hazards, and take the time to report conditions that could injure someone or worse. That process is not only encouraged — it is protected.

Under Washington State workplace safety law and our collective bargaining agreements, employees have the right to identify and report unsafe conditions without fear of retaliation. Joint health and safety committees exist for this very reason: to ensure hazards are found, documented, and corrected before they result in injuries. That protection matters, because safety reporting is real work.

Safety reports take time — and that matters

Anyone who has completed a safety report knows it is not a casual exercise. It requires logging into a system, documenting conditions accurately, explaining why an issue matters, and sometimes identifying patterns across equipment or vehicles. This takes focus and time, and policy recognizes that reality.

For that reason, members are permitted to be compensated for the time it takes to complete legitimate safety documentation. This is not an abuse of the system — it is the system. A reporting structure that demands accuracy but refuses to acknowledge the time required to achieve it is fundamentally broken. Reframing good-faith safety reporting as "unauthorized overtime" is not only incorrect — it is counterproductive.

Why labels don't change the facts

Calling safety-reporting time "unauthorized overtime" does not change what actually occurred: a worker identified safety deficiencies and documented them through an approved process. The law does not look favorably on attempts to discourage safety reporting through wordplay or after-the-fact recharacterizations.

The real question is not how the time is labeled, but what the effect is.

If workers begin to believe that taking safety reporting seriously might expose them to discipline, fewer reports will be written. Hazards will go undocumented. Problems will spread quietly instead of being fixed early. That is not speculation — it is exactly why safety laws exist.

The chilling effect is real

A workplace where people hesitate before reporting safety issues is a workplace heading in the wrong direction. Fear is the enemy of safety.

When even one worker is challenged, scrutinized, or disciplined for submitting a safety report in good faith, the message travels quickly. Others notice. Conversations happen. People begin to ask themselves whether it's "worth it" to speak up.

That chilling effect doesn't just harm individual workers — it undermines the entire safety culture.

Your rights extend beyond the report

Members should know that their rights do not end once a report is submitted.

You have the right to:

  • Report unsafe conditions without retaliation
  • Be compensated for authorized safety-reporting time
  • Ask questions about safety policies
  • Seek union representation if management challenges your actions
  • File a grievance if safety-related rights are violated

Shop stewards play a critical role in this process. If management raises concerns about a safety report, that is precisely when members should involve their steward — not after the fact, and not alone.

Safety reporting benefits everyone

Safety reporting is not about pointing fingers. It is about prevention. Every documented hazard is an opportunity to correct a problem before it causes harm. Every report strengthens the record and improves accountability.

Management often emphasizes the importance of collecting safety data. That data does not appear magically. It comes from workers who take the time to document what they see. Undermining those efforts — intentionally or not — defeats the very purpose of the system.

Getting safety culture right

A healthy safety culture requires consistency. Workers must be confident that reporting hazards will be met with corrective action, not suspicion. Management must recognize that encouraging safety reporting means accepting the time and effort it requires.

Safety reporting is not insubordination.

It is not misconduct.

And it is not "unauthorized overtime."

It is a protected activity — and a responsibility we all share.

Black History Month, the Ongoing Struggle, and the Moment We Are Living In

By Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs Officer, ATU 587/King County Labor Delegate · Pages 12–13 · Open in the PDF

February is Black History Month, a time set aside to reflect on the history, struggles, and contributions of Black Americans. Each year, communities prepare events to honor this history, but Black History Month is not meant to be symbolic or ceremonial. It exists because the full story of this country has too often been ignored, distorted, or minimized. This month is a reminder that Black history is not a side chapter. It is foundational to the United States.

From slavery to segregation, from civil rights to labor movements, Black Americans have consistently pushed this nation toward its stated ideals, often at great personal cost. Progress has never come easily, and it has never come without resistance. Understanding this truth is essential, especially at a time when division, fear, and xenophobia are again shaping public discourse.

Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.: Justice Beyond Words

Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. is often remembered through selective quotes, but his work was far more challenging than many are comfortable acknowledging. Dr. King believed that justice required structural change, not just goodwill. He understood that racism, poverty, and exploitation were connected, and that civil rights without economic justice would always be incomplete.

Dr. King stood with workers because he knew dignity at work was central to dignity in life. His support of the Memphis sanitation workers was not accidental. Those workers were demanding basic respect, safety, and fair pay. Dr. King saw their struggle as a continuation of the civil rights movement. He believed organized labor had a moral responsibility to fight injustice and that unity among working people was a powerful force for change.

Nonviolence, for Dr. King, was not weakness. It was discipline. It required courage to stand unarmed in the face of violence and to remain focused on justice rather than revenge. His message challenged systems, not just attitudes. That message remains relevant today.

Malcolm X: Naming Reality and Reclaiming Dignity

Malcolm X spoke from lived experience shaped by poverty, incarceration, and constant exposure to racism. He rejected the idea that Black Americans should wait patiently while being denied basic rights. Malcolm X emphasized self-respect, political awareness, and accountability. He insisted that people had the right to defend their humanity and reject narratives that portrayed them as inferior.

His speeches resonated because they named realities that were often dismissed or denied. Malcolm X challenged both white power structures and Black leaders who avoided hard truths. Over time, his worldview expanded through travel and reflection. He came to see racial oppression as part of a global system and emphasized solidarity among oppressed people worldwide.

Malcolm X's evolution reminds us that growth matters. Learning does not weaken a movement. It strengthens it. His legacy teaches that justice begins with honesty, even when that honesty makes people uncomfortable.

Abraham Lincoln, Emancipation, and the Limits of Law

Black History Month also requires honest reflection on America's legal and political history. President Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation was a turning point in the Civil War. It declared freedom for enslaved people in Confederate states and redefined the moral purpose of the war. It also encouraged enslaved people to escape bondage and join the fight for their own liberation.

However, emancipation was not a single act of generosity. It was the result of pressure from abolitionists, resistance by enslaved people themselves, and the realities of war. The Thirteenth Amendment formally abolished slavery, but freedom did not translate into equality. Reconstruction was followed by segregation, racial terror, voter suppression, and economic exclusion that lasted for generations.

This history matters because it shows us that laws can open doors, but they do not automatically dismantle injustice. That work requires vigilance and continued struggle.

Black History Month in the Face of Modern Xenophobia

Today, we are witnessing a rise in xenophobic attacks against immigrant communities. Immigrants are being blamed for economic instability, crime, and social change. This rhetoric is familiar. It mirrors the same language once used against Black Americans, Irish immigrants, Chinese laborers, and others deemed "undesirable."

Xenophobia thrives when fear replaces facts. It turns neighbors into targets and workers into scapegoats. Immigrant families face harassment, profiling, and threats of deportation. Many live with constant anxiety, even when they are lawfully present or long-settled in this country.

These attacks often overlap with anti-Black racism and Islamophobia. People are targeted not just for where they come from, but for how they look, speak, or worship. This climate creates division among working people while shielding those truly responsible for inequality and exploitation.

History teaches us that fear is a political tool. When leaders fail to offer solutions, they offer enemies instead.

Why This Moment Demands Solidarity

For working people and unions, Black History Month carries urgent relevance. Black workers and immigrant workers have always been essential to this country's economy. They built infrastructure, staffed public services, and carried industries forward while often being denied fair wages, protections, and respect.

Division weakens collective power. Solidarity strengthens it. Dr. King warned that injustice anywhere threatens justice everywhere. Malcolm X demanded that people stop accepting mistreatment as normal. Their words speak directly to this moment.

Standing against xenophobia is not separate from honoring Black history. It is part of it. Both require rejecting narratives that dehumanize people and confronting systems that benefit from division.

Carrying the History Forward

Black History Month is not about nostalgia. It is about responsibility. Remembering history means recognizing patterns and refusing to repeat them. It means speaking out when communities are targeted and standing with those facing fear and exclusion.

As we prepare Black History Month events, we should approach them with honesty and purpose. The goal is not comfort. It is clarity. The history we honor demands action, unity, and accountability.

Black history teaches us that progress is possible, but never guaranteed. It must be defended, renewed, and passed forward. February reminds us that the struggle for justice did not end in the past. It is happening now, and each of us has a role to play.

Metro Operators Unite on Safety Concerns at Seven-Base Caucus

By Chuck Lare, Shop Steward, and Atlantic Base Safety Committee Chair, #12497 · Page 14 · Open in the PDF

Twenty-eight King County Metro operators representing all seven operator base safety committees gathered January 5th for another "Safety Boots-on-the-Ground" safety caucus, addressing critical workplace concerns ranging from protective shields to inadequate break schedules.

Operator Protective Shields Under Scrutiny

Metro Fleet Engineering's Fariba Fuller and Josh Arreola presented the current Operator Protective Shield (OPS) design, but operators raised significant concerns. The most pressing issue: the shield automatically locks each time operators leave their seats, requiring a cumbersome T-Key to unlock and restricting quick access to the emergency assistance button. Not all operators consistently have their T-Key accessible.

Additional OPS concerns include ensuring adequate space for large wheelchairs per ADA requirements, addressing the open space left when fare boxes are removed, and the inability to comply with policies requiring the shield to block the aisle during ramp deployment. Metro may consider a fully enclosed operator barrier with operator's exclusive front door use and rear wheelchair lift access for future bus purchases. Operators await responses on how these concerns will be addressed.

Communication System Improvements Needed

Operators reached consensus on requested changes to the DDU's public service announcements and messages. Key concerns include TCC frequently overlooking operators' DDU Send Messages, negating their purpose. Operators requested that pressing the emergency assistance button display "Call 911" on outside signs, and that they receive notification whenever live camera monitoring is activated.

Base Safety Committee Operations Vary Widely

A survey revealed substantial differences in how base safety committees operate. Monthly hours detailed for committee work range dramatically from just one hour at East Base to 16-18 hours at Atlantic, Central, and South bases. Guest attendees at meetings typically include representatives from SSQA, Speed and Reliability, Vehicle Maintenance, Comfort Stations, Service Quality, Scheduling, and TCC.

Committees identify unsafe locations through word of mouth, tabling, official forms, Transit Safety Advisors, and management reports. However, shared challenges include difficulty tracking issues to resolution, lack of computer access between meetings, miscommunications, delayed responses, and insufficient follow-through.

Break Time Crisis

Operators identified a critical safety issue: inadequate break time between trips prevents proper mental and physical recovery, creating unsafe conditions for both operators and the public. Current schedules combine break time with bathroom access time, forcing operators to choose between necessary breaks and basic biological needs.

Affected operators my want to collectively file workplace rights complaints with Washington State L&I's Employee Standards Program and, for Seattle routes, with the City of Seattle. These collective filings could provide relief for operators facing these untenable scheduling conditions.

Additional Safety Priorities

Operators called for enhanced safety training, particularly programs like the Red Kite Project that build resilience for handling difficult on-the-job situations. The caucus emphasized that documenting and publishing safety issues creates accountability and establishes public evidence of known hazards.

Other concerns raised include clarifying Metro's policy on auditing live camera monitoring usage, improving communication methods for unsanitary passenger situations, displaying rolling messages on interior overhead signs, connecting ramp deployment with automated announcements, and automating "last stop all passengers must exit" announcements.

Moving Forward Together

The caucus concluded with action items including planning the March quarterly "Safety Boots-on-the-Ground" meeting and encouraging collective workplace rights complaints regarding break time issues.

Operators' consensus emphasized the importance of operator engagement: follow up with your base's safety committee members elected to represent your interest in safety, and your elected union representative to the Joint Union and Management Safety Committee. We are better together and when we demand nothing about us without us; whereas we cannot necessarily fix all we face, we certainly cannot fix what we do not face.

The caucus demonstrated that when operators from all seven bases unite on safety concerns, they create a powerful voice for workplace improvements that protect both transit workers and the riding public.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 4

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
