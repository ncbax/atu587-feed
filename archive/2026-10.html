The President's Report: No Just Cause—No Peace!

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 24 · Open in the PDF

Another month has brought more discipline and discharges without just cause at King County Metro and Kitsap Transit—and another unprecedented wave of cases heading to or piling up in arbitration.

About a year ago at King County Metro, and more recently at Kitsap Transit, something changed. Both agencies moved away from corrective discipline and returned to punitive measures. They now seem more focused on limiting liability than on supporting, rehabilitating, and strengthening the frontline workforce in which they have invested so heavily—and which is so costly to recruit, train, retain, and replace.

Ten to 30 years of public service delivering transit with a clean work record now seem to carry little weight when discipline is imposed for one bad day or a split-second questionable decision later dissected frame by frame, in slow motion, from a desk and with the full benefit of hindsight.

Grievance responses routinely say that years of service and a clean record were considered, but those statements read like boilerplate designed to create the appearance that just cause was applied.

Chiefs, superintendents, TLR, and OLR routinely pass responsibility to others and say their hands are tied. Management representatives who meet with us to discuss more progressive, corrective, and appropriate discipline now claim they lack authority to reach agreements—even though the Union and management have made such agreements for decades. The Union has made clear that it will fully litigate any discipline it believes was imposed without just cause.

The good news is that, in recent weeks, management has made several last-minute arbitration settlement offers and told the Union that upper management is reviewing the issue and process in hopes of improving it and breaking the arbitration logjam. Without meaningful change, that backlog will only worsen.

We hope to return soon to progressive, corrective discipline based on all seven tests of just cause: Fair Notice, Reasonable Rule, Proper Investigation, Fair Investigation, Substantial Proof, Equal Treatment, and Proportional Discipline. Until then, we will continue filing grievances and pursuing arbitrations to protect decades-long careers that Metro has discarded as casually as a used tissue. People's livelihoods and lives are at stake. This is not a game, a debate, or a challenge to win for its own sake.

Operator Shield/Partition Update

As of 9/20/26, 80% of Metro's Excelsior coaches have the new operator partition. Installation on the remaining fleets will begin soon, and Metro still expects the entire fleet to be completed by the end of next March, if not sooner.

Metro is working with bus manufacturers to create a glass panel that fills the gap between the new barrier and the windshield. A few prototypes have been installed for initial testing. Next, Metro will install them on several coaches at each base so operators can test them and provide feedback. VM is also addressing lock issues and testing solutions for doors that close too hard because of their weight.

For all safety and design concerns, it is critical to submit an SSaM—not a BO report. A BO report is more appropriate when a partition has a mechanical problem that needs repair, rather than a design concern.

A break-language win for ASM service on the G-Line

Beginning with the spring 2027 service change, Metro will implement ASM/Fallback operation. G-Line operators assigned to this ASM service will not have fixed break times on their run cards. Instead, they will receive guaranteed breaks or layovers after each revenue trip; these are breaks, not recovery time.

ASM G-Line operators will be guaranteed at least 12 minutes or 18% of their scheduled trip time—whichever is greater—after every trip. Shifts longer than eight hours will include either two 15-minute breaks or one 30-minute break, while shifts under eight hours will include one 15-minute break. Look for the newly negotiated MOA on G-Line ASM breaks on the website or the Union's Facebook page.

We've been here before.

No one wants to reach agreement on an expired contract more than the bargaining team negotiating it. The entire bargaining unit is counting on its elected officers to deliver a contract worthy of its members' sweat and sacrifice. None of that is lost on the bargaining team.

While each contract negotiation is different, historically the average KCM contract negotiations take around a year and a half over 60 bargaining sessions. If the current rate of movement in negotiations hold, we may well not get an agreement until around the end of the year. Regardless of what you've heard, no substantial loss or gain has yet been agreed to in contract or benefits bargaining. We're holding the line while fighting for the gains that the membership expects and has instructed us to do.

Lately there has been calls for the "Special Meetings at Contract Expiration" that our bylaws call for upon the expiration of our contract. I don't believe we've held one of those since the contract expired in November1990, 36 years ago. After that special meeting we still didn't reach an agreement until January 1992, fourteen months after the contract expired, with the help of arbitration.

Your contract bargaining team knows fully what the membership expects and demands. We will have some informational membership meetings when we either have meaningful progress or an impasse to report.

Informational meetings and "Still no contract buttons" don't settle contracts. Contracts this large and complex are settled by tedious and numerous bargaining sessions. If and when negotiations grind to a halt, reach impasse, or are being bargained for in bad faith, this Union will act. For now, we plow on with the tedious grind. Remaining unified and patient throughout our long and difficult KCM contract negotiations empowers your contract negotiators to hold the line and fight for what's rightfully yours. When we fight, we win!

The Maintenance Vice President's Report: Flexible Schedules for Family Care Needs, Carpool or Use of Transit?

By Jeff Stambaugh · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

Last month I reported on the Holiday ULP settlement. In that settlement we agreed to a language change in Art 17.3.E around request for change of hours. This change gives the Base Management Team (Superintendent/Chief) the ability to allow flexible schedules for family care needs, carpool or use of public transit.

I also reported on how Bus VM management was working on updating the form for a request of change of hours. Management presented The Union with a draft; a few edits were worked out and there is a new form now being used. That is where the good news ends. It seems that VM bus Senior leadership has decided that family care needs, carpool or use of public transit to not qualify as a hardship therefore, they are not willing to accommodate requests for change of hours. Two problems with that! First, the new language does not require a hardship for a request to be granted. Second, I thought King County encouraged its employees to use public transportation including carpools. I have seen several denials for a 15-minute change to either participate in carpool or use public transportation. Two of these denials were asking for 15 earlier start and quit time to be able to commute on the Sounder train.

Metros response, "Not a serious hardship, does not qualify" when I spoke to a superintendent about this specific request the response I received, they can wait an hour and ride the next train.

Does management flex their schedules to ride the train?

Does management flex their schedules for family care needs?

While I do understand that management is salary and we are hourly it still seems very hypocritical that management flexes their schedule to fit their needs outside of work to achieve a better work life balance but will not allow its employees the same benefit.

Bus Facilities as well as rail have been relatively quiet this last month.

The Fall picks were held system wide, we are seeing more "missed" picks and frustrated members when they are placed per the CBA. Please do not miss your pick, you can submit an absentee and still call in or show up in person.

Solidarity Isn't a Slogan: What It Is and How We All Benefit From It

By Michael Mackay, Executive Board Officer #1 · Pages 8, 10 · Open in the PDF

Solidarity is a simple word for a simple idea: we are stronger together than we are alone. It means standing with a coworker even when the fight isn't yours, showing up on the picket line for members of another union, and understanding that an injury to one is an injury to all. It is not a slogan. It is a daily practice, and this month I got to watch it work.

What I Saw This Month

In Seattle, UNITE HERE Local 8 just settled the longest hotel workers' strike in Washington state history. After 85 days on the picket line, hotel workers at the Embassy Suites in Pioneer Square reached a tentative agreement that delivers real, lasting gains. Eighty-five days is not a headline number to me; it's eighty-five mornings of workers choosing each other over a paycheck, eighty-five days of coworkers, community members, and fellow unions showing up to hold the line. ATU 587 was one of the unions that Paul Neil, Bill Clifford, and I walked with in solidarity. I showed up, I stood on the line, and I watched a group of workers prove that solidarity, sustained long enough, wins.

That is what solidarity looks like from the outside. Here is what it looks like from the inside, in my own local.

Who I Stand With

ATU 587, to me, is not one workplace or one job title. I know it as Clallam Transit, Jefferson Transit, Kitsap Transit, Solid Ground Transportation, and King County Metro. I know it as the local chapter officially organized and chartered on April 8, 1912, to represent streetcar operators working for the privately owned Seattle Electric Company. I think of the mechanics and facilities workers who keep the fleet running. I'm proud to have been a bus operator for 22 years, keeping the system moving alongside them. I think of our Customer Information Specialist, Pass Sales Office Employees, Supervisors in Service Quality, Dispatch, Control Center, and Training. Every one of those roles is different, every one of them is essential, and every one of them is represented under the same union. Today, ATU 587 has more than 5,000 active members — a historic high for us.

That size and that diversity is exactly the point. A local this broad, spanning this many agencies and job classifications, only holds together if its members choose to stand with one another.

Two Questions Worth Asking Yourself

Last year, Mike Haggard, a senior organizer with the ATU International, trained and mentored me, and he put a choice in front of all of us plainly. His first question: what will you ever get from management, on your own, without a union behind you? The answer is nothing but a hard time. His second question: what will you get? The answer is whatever you're willing to take.

That second answer is the harder one to sit with, because it isn't about management at all — it's about you. Contracts, protections, and respect on the job are not handed to you out of goodwill. They are built, negotiated, and defended by workers who are organized enough to demand them and united enough to hold the line when it matters. What you're willing to accept is what you'll get. That's true whether you're negotiating a contract or deciding whether to show up for a picket line three counties away.

What You're Owed

A union rests on three pillars: wages, benefits, and seniority. Seniority binds you to every other member instead of leaving you to compete for the same job. It rewards your commitment over favoritism and gives you, whether you're a new hire or a thirty-year veteran, a clear, fair, and predictable path. Because of that structure, you don't deal with pay partiality. Your job group is paid equally across the board, not based on who you know or how long you've kept your head down. That's not an accident. It's the product of a union insisting, contract after contract, that fairness isn't negotiable.

Why Unity Matters

People at the top benefit from division. A workforce split by job title, agency, classification, or grievance is easier to manage and easier to shortchange. A workforce that sees itself as one union- mechanics standing with operators, dispatchers standing with facilities staff, one agency standing with another is a workforce that's much harder to push around. We are stronger the more we support each other, and weaker every time we let ourselves be divided.

This is the same commitment every ATU 587 member makes formally, in the Oath of Obligation taken upon joining: to promote the best interests of the union, never to cross a picket line, to keep confidences, to never knowingly let a fellow member be wronged, and to be considerate of others without discrimination of any kind. It's a promise to look out for people we may never have met, in job classifications we may never have worked, because they'd do the same for us.

The Takeaway

Solidarity isn't a slogan. It's the reason eighty-five days on a picket line in Pioneer Square ended in a contract instead of a concession. It's the reason 5,000 members across five agencies and a dozen job classifications can call themselves one union instead of a dozen separate workplaces. And it's the reason the answer to "what will you get?" is always up to us, never up to them.

Get To Know CISM

By Peter Gladhart, Metro Transit Operator, #22405 · Page 10 · Open in the PDF

Most everyone reading this has had that experience at work - that sudden, jarring, heart stopping incident, that after it has passed, left you shaking and breathless. Maybe it was an E bike that darted in front of you at the last second, or the car that came up fast on your left so it could make an abrupt right turn in front of you into a shopping center. Or maybe it was a passenger who you picked up along the way that started cursing you out. Or worse. Any of these types of incidents has the potential to overwhelm your nervous system which can trigger immediate physical reactions. High stress events can cause a fight or flight response which is your body's automatic, physical reaction to a perceived danger, stressor, or threat. When your brain senses danger it floods your body with hormones, including adrenaline, that prepares you to stay and fight or run to safety.

Once the triggering incident has passed deep breaths, sips of water and body movements are great tools to use to help calm your body down.

Good tools for de-stressing are important for a strong mental health. Critical incidents that you experience at work can build and cause stress or anxiety away from the job. Having positive routines including - exercise, good eating habits, good hydration, fun adventures with friends and family, a spiritual connection - can be positive de-stressors. Miguel Messina, the Program Manager and a counselor with King County's Employee Assistance for Supervisors program, stresses the importance of a wellness practice called Grounding. Physical grounding – standing, walking or lying on natural surfaces allows your body to connect directly to the Earths natural electric charge which provides a calming effect. Mental/emotional grounding - a strategy used to manage overwhelming stress and anxiety by using simple exercises to refocus attention on the present moment. One common method, called the 5-4-3-2-1 method is where you identify 5 things you see, 4 things you can feel, 3 things you hear, 2 things you smell and 1 thing you taste.

Great routines and positive de-stressing strategies are important, however, in rare instances these tools are not enough and additional help is needed. The CISM (Critical Incident Stress Management) team at King County Metro can help by providing confidential, peer to peer support to help you process and recover. The CISM support line is available, you can leave a message 24 hours a day and a CISM team member will call you back.

Don't Trade Away What We May Never Get Back

By Andy Price, Shop Steward, ATU Local 587 · Pages 12–13 · Open in the PDF

President Greg Woodfill's September President's Report contains a statement about medical-benefits bargaining that I believe deserves close attention from every member.

Greg wrote:

"And that, for the first time, the Union presented management last week with a proposal for what we believe the membership might accept in trade for agreeing to the slippery slope of a premium share."

He then explained that if the Union reaches an agreement with King County on a package involving monthly premium sharing, it would most likely require a separate membership vote.

Before going further, there is an important distinction members may not always have reason to think about.

We have two separate agreements involved here. Our King County Metro Collective Bargaining Agreement covers wages and most working conditions. It expired October 31, 2025, and negotiations continue.

Our medical benefits are bargained separately under a separate benefits agreement. Local 587 has historically bargained those benefits off-cycle from the main contract, and our own Union publications once described having a separate health-benefit agreement and the ability to bargain our own benefits as an advantage.

That distinction matters because we are now discussing two major protections at two different bargaining tables: premium-free medical coverage and COLA.

I appreciate the commitment to a membership vote if a premium-share agreement is reached. But there is a question that comes before the vote:

What exactly has Local 587 already proposed to King County in exchange for agreeing to premium sharing?

As of September 15, the membership has not been told.

That is particularly difficult to understand when transparency has been such an important part of the current contract negotiations. Members have been given access to bargaining proposals and have been permitted to observe CBA negotiations. That is a positive development.

But transparency should apply to medical-benefits bargaining too.

If our Union has already presented management with a proposal for what leadership believes members might accept in exchange for premium sharing, members should know what that proposal is before bargaining goes further. A vote on a completed package is not the same thing as knowing what is being proposed in our name while bargaining is still underway.

Our own history provides important guidance.

In 2013, King County was also attempting to shift more future health-care costs onto employees. Then-President Paul Bachtel wrote:

"What KC now wants is for its employees to agree to pay for all future cost increases above a negotiated level."

Local 587 rejected that approach and chose interest arbitration.

Vice President Clint DeVoss put it even more plainly:

"Basically, King County wants us to start paying more of the cost of health insurance out of our own pockets. ATU said: NO!"

Local 587 ultimately lost that arbitration. That should be stated honestly. But the Local nevertheless took the dispute through mediation and all the way through an interest-arbitration hearing rather than voluntarily accepting the County's proposed cost shift.

Greg himself calls premium sharing a "slippery slope."

I agree.

Once monthly premium sharing becomes part of our benefits structure, future bargaining starts from a different place. Instead of arguing over whether members should pay premiums, we may find ourselves bargaining over how much members will pay.

Our position should remain simple:

THE EMPLOYER SHOULD PAY 100% OF THE PREMIUM.

We should bargain to improve medical benefits and reduce member costs where possible — not create another permanent employee obligation.

The same principle applies to COLA.

At the August membership meeting, while discussing the parties' wage proposals, President Woodfill indicated that the Union was seeking a guaranteed 6% increase in each year of a three-year contract, rather than accepting Metro's COLA proposal, which was capped at 3% per year.

I strongly support demanding 6% per year.

But I do not believe that should mean giving up COLA language.

A fixed wage demand is the amount we are seeking in this bargaining round. COLA is an ongoing contractual protection against inflation.

And bargaining positions can change before a final agreement is reached. If the eventual wage settlement is less than 6% in any year, COLA becomes even more important. Even if we win 6%, COLA should remain in the contract so members continue to have protection if inflation rises beyond the guaranteed increase.

Our own Local 587 history shows that these protections are not mutually exclusive.

In August 2014, Metro proposed a three-year wage freeze. Local 587 proposed a 3% wage increase in the first year plus its existing 95% COLA language for the remaining two years. Former President Kenny McCormick also made a membership motion demanding current contract language and "a COLA with a 2% floor."

The official history of the Amalgamated Transit Union contains an entire section titled "COLA Protections Challenged." It describes "ATU-developed and promoted COLA protection" and recounts management efforts to eliminate or cap COLA during the inflationary 1970s.

The same page shows Local 788 members voting on a 1976 contract providing both a $1.28-per-hour wage increase and an improved cost-of-living formula.

That history is worth remembering.

Previous generations of ATU members did not view a guaranteed wage increase and COLA as opposites. They fought to preserve both.

In September 2014, President Bachtel reported that Local 587 members had made clear they were "not going to willingly give up their Cost of Living Allowance (COLA) language."

The message to both our Union and management should be clear:

6% AND COLA – NOT 6% OR COLA.

Premium-free medical and COLA have something important in common.

They are long-term protections.

They may be extremely difficult to recover once surrendered.

And members deserve meaningful discussion before either one is bargained away — not simply a yes-or-no vote after the bargaining is finished.

Our Union's history should not merely be something we read after negotiations are over. It should help guide us while bargaining is still underway.

NO MONTHLY PREMIUM SHARE.

6% + COLA.

DEFEND WHAT WE HAVE. BARGAIN FOR MORE.

DO NOT TRADE AWAY PERMANENT PROTECTIONS FOR SHORT-TERM GAINS.

Police Can't Arrest Their Way Out of a Housing and Mental Health Crisis

By Chuck Lare, Shop Steward and a Base Safety Committee Chair · Page 22 · Open in the PDF

Route #7, 8:40 a.m. A man is down in the doorway. Forty riders are watching while you radio TCC and handle it alone — again. Bus drivers see people in psychosis, overdosing, sleeping rough, breaking windows, scared out of their minds. It's not fine. Nobody who's actually stood in that doorway thinks it's fine.

So I understand why "just arrest them" sounds like the obvious answer. It feels fast. It feels like something is finally being done. But here's the problem: it doesn't work, and it costs more than the alternative.

Jail removes a person from a bus stop for a few hours or days. Then they come back — to the same street, the same route, the same untreated crisis. We've all watched the tents move down the block and thought: here we go again. That's not a public safety strategy. That's a very expensive game of whack-a-mole, and taxpayers are footing the bill for every round.

The number that should change your mind: Washington's Department of Corrections puts institutional incarceration at roughly $81,000 per person, per year — and that's before police response, court costs, jail health care, and the ER visits that follow. A Central Florida study found cycling a homeless person through jails and emergency rooms costs about $31,000 a year, versus roughly $10,000 for supportive housing. Denver's own randomized housing study found $6,876 in lower public costs per person, per year, after housing was factored in — fewer arrests, fewer jail stays, fewer ER runs. This isn't a mercy argument. It's a math argument.

And it's been tested at scale, not just in theory. Denver's STAR program sends trained health responders — not police — to mental health and low-level crisis calls. Stanford researchers checked for the exact "whack-a-mole" effect skeptics worry about: did crime just move somewhere else? It didn't. Targeted low-level offenses dropped 34%, with no offsetting rise elsewhere — crime even dropped during hours STAR wasn't on duty, because people who got real help stopped cycling back into crisis.

Houston ran the experiment for over a decade, regionwide. Starting in 2011, the city paired aggressive Housing First placement with continued enforcement against holdout encampments — not just kindness, but kindness plus consequences where needed. The result: homelessness down 63% since 2011, over 25,000 people housed, 90% still housed two years later. Houston is now America's fourth-largest city and has cut homelessness harder than any other major city in the country.

None of this means abandoning enforcement. If someone is violent, armed, or making credible threats, call police — full stop. That's not up for debate. But for the rider who's sick, high, confused, or sleeping at the end of the line and posing no threat, sending an armed response over and over is neither cheap nor effective. It's the response that "feels tough" while quietly failing everyone, including the taxpayer.

Seattle has a real opening right now. The mayor and county executive have announced steps to reset the regional homelessness response, with a workgroup meeting November 30. That's the moment to lock in what actually worked elsewhere: mandatory-track treatment options (not just optional ones), funding parity for behavioral-health responders instead of default police dispatch, and a direct crisis-responder line operators can call separately from 911.

ATU 587 needs to require Metro provide a simple reporting system that works for operators and inserts accountability through a simple real time live dashboard — tracking response times, repeat incidents, and operator emergency calls, so results are visible, not just promised. And we need ATU 587 to maintain a constant dialog at all Bases that facilitates two-way communications feedback to the working groups, so if the city's numbers look better but our incident reports don't, we have the evidence to prove problems are just moving, not shrinking.

Operators see this crisis up close every single shift. That's expertise the workgroups need at the table — not silence, while the details get decided without us.

Text your union representative this week: tell them you want ATU 587 to have a seat at the Breaking the Cycle and other such workgroups.

Sources

  • WA Dept. of Corrections FY2025 institutional cost data
  • Central Florida homelessness cost study (Homeless Voice)
  • Denver Housing First randomized study (NLIHC)
  • Denver STAR program evaluation (Stanford SIEPR; NBC News)
  • Houston Housing First results, 2011–present (West Houston Homelessness Partnership)
  • Mayor Wilson & Executive Zahilay, Regional Homelessness Authority announcement, July 2026.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 4

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
