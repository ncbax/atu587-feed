The President's Report: Our Voice, Our Power!

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 20 · Open in the PDF

One of the most surprising things I've learned in the last year and a half is the political power and influence of ATU, both at the International and at our Local level.

I had no idea how coveted our endorsement was, or how easily we could pick up the phone and connect with City, County, State, and National politicians whenever we had issues that needed to be addressed.

I'll give you an example: last legislative session in Olympia, Amazon sneaked in a law allowing deliveries to use our Transit lanes. All it took was for us to call our friends in Olympia, and the bill was killed, infuriating Amazon, I've heard. Amazon will be back at it next year, they already had a State Senator meet with our Union to hear out our objections.

Many members feel our Union shouldn't be involved in politics at all, while others think it isn't involved enough in the issues and causes of the day. Our Union has a decades-long tradition of endorsing and donating to Pro-Transit and Pro-Union Politicians. We don't endorse or promote Political Parties, philosophies, or causes. We stick with Pro Transit, Pro Union, because this is a very diverse Union with diverse political views/agendas. The things we all can and should rally around are the protection of our Jobs, wages, benefits, working conditions, transit funding, transit infrastructure, and transit laws.

You may not like many or any of our local or state politicians. You may not agree with endorsing or donating to Politician's whose beliefs/agendas don't align with yours. The cold hard reality though, is that the only way our union can have any influence, or protection is to play the political game. We didn't create the game or have any control over it; we don't get to choose who runs. We interview everyone who asks for our endorsement and ask the same type of questions to best gauge where they stand on Public Transit and Unions in general.

Just to be clear, COPE will absolutely endorse a conservative politician if they say the right things about Transit and Unions. We've had recent feelers from Conservatives in Olympia who want to explore working more closely with our Union on Public Transit. Conservative Republican County Council Member Dunn introduced the County Legislation to create the Transit Safety Task Force and continues to support us on vital transit issues.

If you are a 587 Member, your wages and benefits were earned on the backs of the thousands of dues-paying members who came before us. Whatever job you currently have at the County, you wouldn't be making nearly as much in wages and benefits if we weren't in a Union, nor would we have the level of public support demonstrated by votes to raise taxes for Public Transit funding.

This was an incredibly challenging election cycle for our Union. Both the County Executive and the Seattle Mayors' race pitted people we have always supported against each other. Ultimately, our COPE committee recommended endorsing Claudia & Bruce mainly for the length of time they've supported and worked with our Union and promoted public transit. But it was a difficult choice, including considering and deciding against dual endorsements.

All along, our Union felt that no matter who won the County Executive and Seattle Mayor races, we would have two leaders our Union has supported and worked well with, who would be champions for Labor and Transit. Obviously, our choices for these two offices lost, and many of our members would have preferred that we endorsed the eventual winners.

I have personally met with and congratulated Girmay twice and Katie once since the Election. They both assured me that they understood our union's endorsements, that there were no hard feelings, and that they are both ready and looking forward to working with our Union.

I called them both the moment the Union approved endorsing their challengers and checked in with them throughout the Campaign. I was invited to and attended Girmay's ceremonial swearing-in ceremony.

Our Union is YOU. If you're not happy with who we endorse or how we decide, throw your name in for consideration to join the COPE committee. Follow the News Review and attend meetings during the election cycle, as the COPE committee's endorsement recommendations are merely recommendations accepted or rejected by the membership at the monthly Union meetings.

If you currently contribute to COPE, thank you for adding to our Union's power! If you've never contributed to COPE, please consider contributing even the minimum $5 per paycheck for the protection of your job, its compensation, and your working conditions. We have many plaques on the walls of our Union Hall recognizing our Local for contributing the most to COPE for a union of our size. We took pride in that recognition; it mattered.

We could literally get back to winning these awards if every member who doesn't contribute signed up to contribute at least $5 per paycheck. And every member who currently contributes signed up to contribute at least $1 more.

The Union does not speak for members' political beliefs nor tell them who they should or must vote for. Your Union Dues are never used for political purposes; voluntary donations to COPE by our Members are the only dollars used for politics.

Our endorsements and contributions aren't deciding factors in who is or isn't elected. They just give us better access to be heard on the transit issues that matter to us. Historically, we have cultivated politicians who tend to vote pro-transit when we most need pro-transit votes.

Even if you despise politics, or despise the West Coast, King County, or Seattle politics, it absolutely is in your best interest to contribute to COPE. Politicians pick up the phone and listen when we call them directly for help. They draft and vote for pro-transit funding and initiatives.

ATU International's current slogan is "Our Voice, Our Power!" Our COPE committee, its endorsements, and its contributions are literally ATU 587's political Voice and Power. Please consider contributing or contributing a little more to the power of our voice, it matters, it makes a demonstrative difference.

ATU 587 – We Are One!

The Maintenance Vice President's Report: December Maintenance VP Report

By Jeff Stambaugh · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

Bus Facilities Maintenance

Does it ever end? Will it ever get better?

Last month I reported on the Constructors pick and TFD managements bullying and intimidation tactics. The saga continues, while TFD management finally relented on their ability to add that an employee must have a certain certification to pick a certain spot (4/10 shifts), I was having a discussion with a TFD chief about the tactics used to get employees to get certifications tell me "We are trying to incentivize employees to get certified." Mind you, all these certifications are optional and not required as part of their employment. We have agreed to a repick without certifications being required to pick spots, however management has retaliated by removing desired 4/10 positions. If that isn't the definition of bullying and intimidation, I don't know what it is. Along with all that TFD management still does not seem to have a clear grasp of what the Term outsourcing means. Back in July, TFD management decided to outsource Local 587 Union work to a vendor, grievances were filed, discussions were held, grievance hearings were held and TFD Management saw the wisdom in doing the right thing and paid the grievant's the monies due for outsourcing at first step. There was hope things were going to get better.

During the discussions, the Union was very clear that if TFD management felt they needed to outsource work, TFD needed a written agreement with the Union to do so. Unfortunately, TFD management has outsourced work again, not only is there no written agreement with the Union, there was no discussion with the Union either. As of this writing, grievances have been filed, and hearings have been scheduled. I'm optimistic we will receive a positive first step response.

Bus Vehicle Maintenance

The ongoing dispute over Metro issuing Unexcused Absences to employees agreeing to work OT or Holiday assignments and later change their mind continues. Metro has refused to engage in a resolution to this issue and instead has decided to attempt to drag it through the grievance process in hopes of strengthening their position, however through the process the Union is now in possession of emails that shows Metro's attempt to unilaterally change working conditions without following the proper process.

The Union has now filed an Unfair Labor Practice.

Prior to the filing, the Union engaged Metro at several levels trying to resolve this issue. As of this writing, Metro has been unwilling to work to a resolution. More to come as we work through the process with the Public Employees Relations Commission, commonly known as PERC.

Discipline

It has been a rough month for discipline.

Between TFD and Bus Vehicle Maintenance there have been nine (9) terminations or proposed terminations, and several other members were issued heavy handed discipline.

Most all this discipline is related to work performance issues and attendance.

Rail

Rail continues to expand and hire, resulting in adding several new maintenance positions throughout the system that has created opportunities for promotions and movement as well as confusion as how the movement is accomplished. Do we do re-pick or move-ups? That depends on several circumstances all spelled out in various sections in the contract.

The Recording Secretary's Report: Chief Shop Steward Nominations, Peninsula Nominations, and Bargaining!

By Latrelle Gibson · Page 6 · Open in the PDF

So, many of you probably ask the question "why does it take so long to get the Shop Steward Nomination list out"? Well, there's many factors at play when getting this list together. Just before pick preview, I will get a bulletin out alerting the membership of nominations being open. Nominations typically remain open through the first month of the new shakeup to allow the Supervisors to nominate during their pick. Once that has happened, I will put out Shop Steward list #1. Once members see this list, some may realize they missed the nomination window and email me requesting to be placed on the Shop Steward List. This is where list #2 comes to play, this is the revised list.

Now comes the fun part, Chief Shop Steward Nominations. Now, per our locals' bylaws, if a member wishes to be Chief Shop Steward at their base and they're the only ones running for this position, no election will be needed as they're unopposed. However, if there are multiple members running on a particular base, I will need to conduct a vote. The vote is conducted via operator mail at their respective base. The instructions are to vote for one, sign the ballot, return the ballot to the provided white secrecy envelope, and give it to the window so they can place them in the manilla envelope for me to pick up. If all goes as planned, the results are next day. But, if things don't go as planned, well, this will cause a delay in getting the Shop Steward List out to the bases.

I'm going to be working on a revision to make it clearer on how to vote, and I'll be sure to send out bulletins to our windows letting them know that I will be conducting a Chief Shop Steward vote and have instructions on what to do with the ballots and who will be picking up the ballots for the window. I will also place reminders in the News Review so everyone will get that reminder, because I truly appreciate all of you who're willing to step up for the membership and help in any way. This greatly helps us in the Union office when we have that support at the bases.

By the date of this article being published, the list will be out and if timing allows, will also be placed in this very publication for everyone's convenience.

Clallam and Jefferson Transit Shop Steward Nominations

It's that time of year again. By the time this News Review is published, there should be a bulletin posted at CTS with the upcoming Shop Steward Nominations and Election. The Shop Steward Nominations will take place at the December 8th meeting. The Election will take place in December, and the vote will be decided by a plurality vote. For Jefferson Transit, your nomination will take place at the January cycle of meetings for JTA. And as per the bylaws, the nomination announcement will be sent to JTA following the meeting, and a vote will be conducted by mail out secret ballot vote. The election results will be decided by plurality vote.

Bargaining Dates Update

I will be updating some of the bargaining session dates as some were listed with the wrong days and some dates didn't point out that bargaining would be ending early due to our meeting cycle. Please check this publication for those updates. I want to thank all of you for showing up to the open bargaining sessions, keep showing up if you can, this show of solidarity is greatly needed!

In Solidarity, RecSec Gibson

Fair Representation, Weingarten Rights, and Why Process Matters in Every Union

By Andy Price, Shop Steward local 587 · Pages 10–11 · Open in the PDF

Most union members have heard the phrase "Duty of Fair Representation," often shortened to "DFR." It's something we assume our union has "covered," but very few members ever get a clear picture of what the duty really requires — or why it exists in the first place. DFR is not some abstract legal technicality. It is the backbone of trust inside every unionized workplace.

What the Duty of Fair Representation Actually Means

The Duty of Fair Representation developed from a long line of court decisions, starting with the U.S. Supreme Court. The core idea is simple:

When a union represents a member in grievances, discipline, contract enforcement, or negotiations, it must act fairly, honestly, and without discrimination or arbitrary decision-making. DFR is not about guaranteeing a victory in every case. It is about guaranteeing a fair process for every member.

Members have the right to expect:

  • a reasonable investigation
  • contract-based explanations
  • timely responses
  • consistent procedures
  • absence of retaliation or favoritism
  • honest evaluation of the facts
  • communication about decisions.

Because the union is the exclusive representative, the law requires it to use that authority responsibly.

A Real Example (From Outside ATU or King County)

One well-known case often used in labor education is Vaca v. Sipes (U.S. Supreme Court, 1967). In that case, the union failed to properly investigate a member's grievance before dropping it. The Court held that a union violates DFR when its handling of a grievance is:

  • arbitrary (no investigation or explanation)
  • discriminatory (treating members differently for irrelevant reasons)
  • in bad faith (dishonest, retaliatory, or personal motive).

Many unions use Vaca v. Sipes in steward training programs as an example of what not to do — not because unions are malicious, but because the legal standard is strict, and consistency in procedure is essential. Using publicly decided cases like this is safe, educational, and widely practiced.

Why Consistency Matters More Than Outcomes

Across the country, unions win some grievances, lose others, and settle many.

DFR does not guarantee results — but it does guarantee fairness. A union can deny or drop a grievance if it has a rational, fact-based reason.

What it cannot do is:

  • ignore evidence
  • fail to investigate
  • base decisions on personal relationships
  • communicate nothing
  • violate its own published process
  • silence a member's representative
  • refuse to explain its rationale

Members are not required to win — but they are entitled to a fair chance.

Weingarten Rights and the Principle of Representation

Another important cornerstone of workplace fairness is Weingarten rights, established by the U.S. Supreme Court in 1975. These rights apply in all unionized workplaces and guarantee:

If an employee reasonably believes a meeting may lead to discipline, they have the right to union representation — and the right to a representative who is allowed to participate meaningfully. Weingarten rights are not merely symbolic.

Courts have repeatedly held that a union representative must be permitted to:

  • ask clarifying questions
  • consult privately with the employee
  • provide support and guidance
  • prevent intimidation or confusion
  • help clarify facts relevant to the issue

These rights exist because workers should never navigate a potentially disciplinary meeting alone or unsupported. When understood together, DFR and Weingarten rights create a balanced system where unions and members each have defined roles, responsibilities, and protections.

Why These Rights Matter to Everyone

Some members may believe these issues only matter when something goes wrong.

But that's not true.

Healthy unions maintain strong representation practices all the time, because:

  • They protect the legitimacy of grievance outcomes.
  • They strengthen the union's credibility with management.
  • They build member trust and stability.
  • They prevent preventable conflicts, misunderstandings, and legal exposure.
  • They reinforce that the union belongs to all members equally.

Even if a member never files a grievance, these rights create a foundation of fairness that protects the entire bargaining unit.

A Shared Commitment

Union officers work under enormous pressure, often juggling many cases at once. Members may not see the amount of work that goes on behind the scenes. At the same time, members deserve clarity, consistent procedures, and fair treatment. DFR and Weingarten rights give both sides a shared framework.

Strong unions thrive when all parties:

  • communicate clearly
  • follow contract language
  • apply procedures consistently
  • ensure representatives are allowed to fulfill their role
  • document decisions
  • treat every member equally

These principles strengthen solidarity, protect the rights of the individual, and contribute to the long-term health and credibility of the union.

Conclusion

The Duty of Fair Representation and Weingarten rights are not abstract rules; they are the pillars supporting fair process, equal treatment, and member confidence. When understood and honored, they help create a union environment where every member knows they will be heard, represented, and respected — no matter the issue. A strong process makes a strong union.

Yours in solidarity Andy Price Shop Steward local 587

Building Trust Through Accountability: A Conversation Worth Having

By Balaramdas Stern, Bellevue Base Operator #23327 · Page 12 · Open in the PDF

Recently, I had a candid conversation with a King County Metro operations chief that got me thinking deeply about accountability, trust, and how we show up for each other in this work. What struck me most was their genuine care—for us as operators, for the riding public, and for creating a workplace where everyone can do their best.

They shared frustrations about moments when drivers seem defensive about policy violations or push back on feedback. But underneath that frustration, I heard something else: a leader who truly wants to support us, who understands the complexity of what we do, and who feels caught in the middle trying to balance accountability with advocacy. It made me ask myself some hard questions. And I think they're worth asking together.

Where Accountability Meets Trust

Here's the thing: accountability isn't about catching people doing something wrong. It's about all of us taking ownership of our impact—even when that impact is unintended. When we roll through a stop, miss a call-out, or respond curtly to a passenger on a rough day, we might have reasons. Valid ones. But those actions still ripple outward—affecting riders who depend on us, coworkers who cover for us, and supervisors who field complaints while wanting to have our backs. The question isn't whether we make mistakes. We all do. The question is: can we stay open to hearing about them?

The Defensiveness Trap

Defensiveness is human. When someone approaches us about our performance, especially if we're already stressed, tired, or feeling undervalued, our first instinct might be to protect ourselves. We might hear criticism even when someone's offering feedback. We might assume they're building a case against us rather than trying to help us grow.

But here's what I'm learning: when we lead with defensiveness, we make it harder for people to support us. We shut down conversations before they can become collaborative. We miss opportunities to share our perspective, explain context, or find solutions together. What if, instead, we got really curious? 'Help me understand what you saw' or 'What would you have done in that situation?'

Multiple Sources, One Goal

Feedback comes from everywhere—peers, passengers, supervisors, shop stewards, even our families. Each source offers a different lens on how we're showing up. Our coworkers see our professionalism. Riders experience our customer service. Supervisors track our compliance. Our loved ones notice when work stress follows us home.

None of these voices alone tells the whole story. But together, they help us see our blind spots and build on our strengths.

The operators I most respect aren't perfect—they're the ones who can hear hard things without crumbling, who admit mistakes without shame, and who ask for support when they need it.

Bridging the Trust Gap

I get it. When a supervisor wants to "hear your side," it can feel like a setup. Sometimes past experiences make us skeptical. Sometimes the power dynamic makes us guarded. But most of the time, they genuinely want context. They want to understand what happened so they can advocate effectively—for us and for the system we all serve.

We can help bridge this gap by:

  • Sharing facts clearly without minimizing or over-explaining
  • Acknowledging impact even when our intent was good
  • Remembering that asking for support isn't weakness—it's how we all get better
  • Recognizing that middle managers care too—often more than we realize

Looking Ahead

Here's what this conversation sparked for me: what if we had access to someone truly neutral when things get difficult? Not management, not union, not HR,—just a trained professional whose only job is to help people work through conflict. They could facilitate conversations between operators, between us and management, even between supervisors and chiefs - and much more. Help us navigate misunderstandings before they escalate, stress us out, and become grievances. Give us a place to turn when we need support that doesn't fit the boxes we currently have.

It's worth exploring. Because ultimately, we're all on the same team. We all want safe service, fair treatment, and a workplace where people can thrive.

Look, accountability isn't about never messing up. It's about owning our impact, staying open when feedback is hard to hear, and remembering that the culture we experience is the culture we create—together, one interaction at a time.

What do you think? How do we build more trust and accountability in our workplace? Let's keep this conversation going.

I'd like to hear from you—anonymously and honestly. Scan the QR code below to share your thoughts, experiences, or questions. Your feedback will help shape our next conversation and ensure we're addressing what matters most to each and every one of you. (The QR code is on page 12 of the PDF.)

This article reflects one operator's reflections and is intended to spark dialogue within our workforce. All perspectives are welcome.

Are You Feeling Safe?

By Atlantic Base Shop Steward and Safety Committee Chair Chuck Lare · Page 13 · Open in the PDF

Gun violence on buses and other incidents—whether directed at operators or not—weigh heavily on those of us working routes with the greatest history of violence. What are we to do?

We need action, not rhetoric. Being told "you are an essential worker" rings hollow when operators don't feel mentally or physically safe. We also carry the additional burden of uncertainty: if trauma occurs, will we recover enough to keep driving? Will we keep our jobs?

Our Union's Priority: Protecting Operators' Jobs

Without our jobs, everything else is secondary. The best practice is preventing trauma from occurring in the first place. While there are practical limits to investing in more security personnel, our union President deserves recognition for his leadership in bringing the county-wide Regional Transit Safety Taskforce to fruition. But like any high-risk environment, there are limits to preventive protection.

Because of these limitations, advocate for robust training and adequate recovery time—giving operators the tools to protect both their health and their jobs.

Evidence-Based Training: The Red Kite Program

Effective, evidence-based training programs like the Red Kite Project have demonstrated real-world success in helping operators manage conflict and reduce burnout. Unlike other organizational change models, it utilizes a science-based approach tested in civil conflict and war zones. The Red Kite Project began its work in conflict transformation in post-war Bosnia, learning that obstacles and conflict can be transformed into something healthy, even under the most adverse conditions.

With a diverse team including mental health professionals, the Red Kite Project can address operators' needs, no matter how dire the situation. Their work around the country has shown results: fewer incidents, safer stations, and more confident riders.

The Critical Need for Recovery Time

Adequate recovery time is essential. Without it, operators don't have time to process the allostatic (cumulative) stress load from their previous trip. This makes operators more susceptible to PTSD and prevents proper recovery when incidents occur, potentially costing operators' jobs. The hypothesis could be substantiated with a University of Washington study of operators' susceptibility to PTSD from job-related allostatic loading—similar to their existing free therapy study.

Free Therapy Available Now Through 2026

To digress, many operators may qualify for the FREE therapy offered by the UW PTSD Study PATH (Positive Processes and Transition to Health). PATH is a research project investigating innovative treatment for adults (18–65 years old) with PTSD and depression, funded by the National Institute for Mental Health through 2026.

You may be eligible if:

  • You are 18–65 years old
  • You experienced a major life event within the last five years (including physical or sexual assault, loss of a loved one, end of a significant relationship, loss of job/career, war-related experiences, or COVID-19 pandemic-related losses)
  • You're experiencing trauma-related and/or depressive symptoms
  • At least 12 weeks have passed since the event.

Stress symptoms include: Recurrent thoughts of the stressor, intense fear or anxiety related to the event, nightmares, avoiding situations or people associated with the event, feeling detached from others, or being easily startled.

Contact: 206-685-3617 or visit https://pathway2help.com/

Building Alliances for Change

We need allies to champion operators' health, jobs, and public service. Metro management has been unresponsive to requesting a meeting with UW PATH's Dr. Lori Zoellner to conduct a study assessing operators' susceptibility to PTSD from allostatic job loading.

The new Executive Girmay Zahilay, from his campaigning, may offer some hope in, who will lead King County? (Seattle Times, 10/26/25) the Executive stated, "I always try to ask myself who's not in the room, who needs to be here, how can we break down silos?" Frontline workers have valuable insights that could improve county operations. We need leadership willing to bridge that gap.

I'm guardedly optimistic after receiving this response from the Executive-elect:

"Thank you so much for reaching out and for your service to this region. I grew up catching the Metro bus to get to school and after school activities (specifically the 106, 107, and the 7). I personally benefited from your work and all of the amazing operators at King County.

I take the role of leading this government with great humility and responsibility. I am in the process of getting my feet under me at the moment, and will absolutely be seeking partnership from frontline workers on both delivering quality service and solving persistent problems like homelessness.

Thank you again for reaching out and I will be in touch soon."

Take Action

Let's not leave anything to chance. Ally with those taking action for operators' health, jobs, and public service. Our safety and well-being depend on it.

Illustration: "What Metro Bus Drivers Need" — tangible action, not rhetoric: safety, job security, evidence-based training, adequate recovery time, build alliances to champion operators, available resources from PTSD study, optimism of support from new executive leadership.

The Ryerson Gang

By Cory Rigtrup, VM Board Rep · Page 16 · Open in the PDF

It's December and I have returned from union leadership exile. Last month I was sworn in to fill the vacant VM Board position. A position that went vacant when Jerry McKenna stepped down for personal reasons. Jerry is a union powerhouse, so I wish him the best and hope for his swift return to helping out fellow VM members. Enough fluff, here's my article, it's about surveillance and discipline.

There have been some Management changes in VM, some retired, some got fired, some forced to retire. Well, the vacated dominoes of invertibility happened to fall is such a way, that a "game of thrones" scenario formed. No, there are no dragons, but there is the conquest of desirous hopefuls pitting themselves against each other for just a few positions. While I believe in nature's way of competition breeding greatness, this story is not about that.

From the maintenance perspective, Ryerson base hasn't really changed over the years. Same old narrow shop doors, remote parking lot, and the base is weirdly configured to be in the middle of the yard. What has changed is the crew.

Since covid, Ryerson along with all the other bases, radically grew in Utility Service Workers. Utility Service Workers, aka USW, inside wash, even called bus cleaners, have the lowest starting wage of all 88 job classifications in the Metro bargaining agreement, except for Supported Employees.

The USW classification for the last 5 years has been a revolving door of new hires. While many have seen promotions to other positions, most have stayed put and advanced up the wage table. However, a very disproportionate large number, and I would say over half of all of VM' terminations in the last 5 years, were fired.

As the years went on, Metro absolutely failed these members, by not providing them with regular leadership. This issue was the number one issue discussed at VM labor management meetings over the years. So, in 2024, when VM posted a Leaderships recruitment for these crews of USWs, I saw light of redemption.

Fast forward till a few months ago. Some bases have used these additional Leads to train, organize, report and run these USW crews. Other bases didn't get the memo and did something different. Ryerson just failed.

Back to the "Game of thrones" scenario. Ryerson got some new management, and it was not going to be run with leadership for USW crews, but with video surveillance and discipline.

One night, management came in early and did a random check of USW work. They determined some of the work was not up to standard, and not all the employees were where they thought they should be. For whatever reason, Ryerson base never issued their USW crews radios or even provided them with a Lead to report too. When not all the employees were found in the weirdly configured base, investigations began.

They brought the whole crew in, one at a time and asked them what they did that night. Then Ryerson did the one thing everyone should be very, very considered about, they started pulling massive amounts of video. Not just video of the one or two people they had issue with, but every single person from every single coach that gang worked on that night. The video was watched for the entire graveyard shift and matched up with all the stories given. As of the date of writing this article, there have been at least 5 loudermill hearings, 3 terminations and a couple suspensions waiting for a decision.

I could go on and on and on, about the value of good leadership in a crew environment, but let's talk about cameras and our contract. While the contract allows management to basically manage to failure, the surveillance protection language is ancient and should never be touched. What it simply says in cases like these, if it's reported that there is an issue at work, management may use surveillance to verify the issue. Anything else seen, an employee cannot be disciplined for, with one major caveat. If surveillance shows a major infraction, something like stealing, then they can discipline, potentially justify termination.

There are a lot of good, hardworking people in that Ryerson graveyard gang. Their shifts end at 6:45am, even though their workstations are taken away hours earlier for sign out. Regardless of the standing order to hang loose, the "game of thrones" needed their blood. Stay tuned, more to come.

Even though I am dyslexic as hell, it's kind of fun to write these articles. I am proud to be back. Thank you.
