The President's Report: A Year of Reflection and Determination

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 20 · Open in the PDF

This year started with us gathering at the Lumen Field Event Center to honor and mourn the painful loss of our Union Brother Shawn Yim. It ended at AB/CB, with the unveiling of a remembrance bench built by our members to forever remind us of Shawn and the dangers front-line transit workers face daily.

Much of the year in between revolved around our desire and determination not to let Shawn's loss of life by the hands of brutal murderer go in vain. We hastily called a press conference at our Union Hall on the day of Shawn's murder to deliver a list of demands, which included our call for the establishment of a regional task force on Transit Safety. Metro Management met with us at our Union Hall the next day to fill us in on how they would meet all of our demands.

We gathered where Shawn was murdered for an emotional and powerful candlelight vigil.

Our members were at Shawn's murderer's arraignment, where we publicly vowed to attend every one of his trial hearings, and met with the King County Prosecutor, who promised to keep our Union updated on every aspect of the upcoming trial. We met with the outgoing King County Executive, the King County Sheriff, and her staff to receive an update on the County's plans, efforts, and commitment to establish a much larger Metro Transit Police force.

We testified at City and County Council meetings until legislation was put forward by the County Council establishing the regional task force that we had been calling for, starting 6 months before Shawn's murder. We held two regional task force meetings at the Seattle IAM Union Hall, where 130 regional leaders and police forces met to help workshop and commit to the task force.

We had 587 members at every task force subcommittee meeting, and they contributed most of the 130 recommendations that emerged.

We rushed Union officers to every major transit safety incident in the last year to give interviews demanding faster action and calling for the public to pressure their political representatives to take their share of responsibility for their citizens' safe access to public transportation.

We've participated in and signed off on every step of procuring the new safety barriers to retrofit the entire fleet. We are all frustrated at the time it's taken for this procurement, but unfortunately, it's just the reality of how long public agency procurements take. Prototypes of the winning bid will be installed any day now for operators to test and the Union to sign off on. Then around 12 weeks later, the barriers will arrive and be installed by our members at a rate of 120. The timeline that was initially envisioned for barrier installation to start before the end of 2025 has been moved up to March 2026.

Our Union demanded the closure of 12th and Jackson, and we are awaiting King County's promised action to quickly restore safety at those zones before we are forced to publicly demand their closure again.

We are transit workers, not politicians, police, or transit agency managers. It's not our job or responsibility to restore safety, order, and accountability for one's actions on and around public transit. Our job is to push and hold accountable all those whose responsibility it is. Promises of future action are just words. Our membership will not be satisfied until they see and feel meaningful and measurable change. We will not rest until we get there. Our members' and passengers' lives literally depend on it.

A harm against one is harm against all!

Our Union's power lies in its diversity and collective will to fight together and fight for ALL. We stand firmly in support of any and all segments of our membership under attack by anyone's agendas of division or hate. And are prepared to stand with and fight for them.

This philosophy extends to our fight against a growing number of managers at several properties we represent who lean toward punitive vs corrective discipline, with ever-increasing levels of discipline and or terminations that violate our contractual protections of progressive discipline, due process, and just cause. This Union will devote whatever resources or time necessary to fight back against these malicious misguided or incompetent managers who come after our members.

I hope you all enjoyed the holidays. ATU 587 – We Are One!

Bus VM

Im working on gathering the facts but if someone from management could reach out and explain why and where this is coming from because I'm confused at where the CBA or Policy allows this.

The Recording Secretary's Report: Rail Repick and Various Rec Sec Info

By Latrelle Gibson · Page 6 · Open in the PDF

For those at rail, you've probably heard that a repick is coming. Had it been MY choice, I would've told Sound Transit to wait until the Spring/Summer pick, that way runcards could've been provided and the simulated I-90 service could've been picked then. Wait, I did suggest this and also asked why couldn't rail just assign the work via the board and whatever was left, sell it at O.T, even perhaps run the simulated service at half until the pick in March. The answer was this would cost too much for Sound Transit. I was under the impression ST had some serious deep pocket coins, seeing that they receive taxes from 3 counties and all, but maybe I'm mistaken. That being said due to this, an MOA was signed to allow for this repick which is scheduled for Jan 7 – 9. This pick will take effect February 14, 2026 and will still run until March 28. The regular pick will still be the week of March 9 – 11.

The driver timecards, run cuts, and seniority pick list are available on the website to view. We will NOT be picking vacation at this repick, that will be during the regular pick in March. When operating the simulated service, please make note of the run times and be sure to report them if there is not enough time in the run for turn backs or restroom usage. Due to the rushed nature of this simulated service, there may not have been time for proper review of these run times.

RSIT Seniority List

The RSIT Seniority list is available on the Union Website to View. Please keep in mind the reason for the delay was due to the MOA that was just signed, which would've required a revision. This MOA's language is consistent with O&MSIT's language, which places those currently at Rail first in seniority order, followed by others in King County, followed by External Candidates. This Seniority list will reflect this MOA change. Thank you, Abdiwahab, Greg, and (I'm going to pat my own self on the back for suggesting using the same language as streetcar during the contract survey meetings and helping get the language drafted to propose in bargaining) for working hard on this language, now Rail is consistent with other classifications. We will still need to bargain this MOA into the new language as it is set to expire once the new TA is signed and ratified, and this language has been presented in our contract proposals.

Seniority Adjustments

I know I've written a lot about seniority and how it works. However, this topic is important to discuss as this is all we have when it comes to choosing our work shifts, work locations, vacation time, ETC. The Union CANNOT just change one's seniority. Remember, the seniority list is the employer's list, and the Union cannot alter their list, only the employer can do this. Seniority adjustments and seniority restoration are different animals. Adjustment speaks to making someone whole and restoration speaks to restoring a member to their previously held seniority date and rank.

For seniority adjustments, this would normally be the result of a settlement agreement or a forfeiture. If a member obtains an attorney, and they reach a settlement agreement with Metro, and part of this agreement is to adjust one's seniority to what it should've been, this is what the Union IS REQUIRED TO COMPLY WITH. A settlement is a legally binding agreement, and many settlements have been in the works for 4+ years before getting to this point. So, although some may be upset when an adjustment happens, know that there is nothing the Union can do about this. We have to comply with the settlement agreement.

For seniority restoration, this action is usually the result of a medical separation, transfer, or promotion. If a member is medially separated, and returns to their classification held at time of separation within 3 years, they will have their seniority restored. Medical Separation is a process, one cannot simply quit and state they were medically separated. The other instance would be one transferred or promoted to a job outside of 587 but within King County and decided to return to their previous classification within a year, they would have their seniority restored. If a member for instance promotes to SIT, and decides to return to operator, they would return to their bus operator classification with no loss in seniority, HOWEVER, if they decided to go back to SIT, they would lose all rights to the seniority they held in that classification.

Bargaining

Open bargaining will return in the New Year. This is when the "stuff" gets real! I don't know if we'll be able to always say what articles we'll be addressing, however, I will try to keep the membership updated to help members know when show up for their classification although there is nothing wrong with just simply showing up to observe. The bargaining dates will be indicated in this News Review and were listed in the December edition of the News Review. I am going to be working with our staff to get the proposals we pushed across the table uploaded to the website. This will not be something that will happen quickly, as this is new for us and we will need to figure out how to upload such large files to the website, this is the entire contract being uploaded to the website and this takes a lot of work to do. We appreciate your patience as we work to get this uploaded and see you at the next bargaining session!

In Solidarity, RecSec Gibson

Notification of Respect

By Ian Nielson, FLS Shop Steward · Page 11 · Open in the PDF

King County Metro does NOT respect it's employees. King County Metro is required by the CBA to adopt specific behaviors that are commonly associated with respect, but make no mistake, it does NOT respect you. Your Union Represented Supervisors are getting tired of the disparities behind the scenes. I will be bringing one of these to light in this article.

The CBA contains dozens of references to "notifications" and "notices" required between Metro and Union Members. Metro is required to inform Members of matters pertaining to them, and Members are required to keep Metro informed of matters in kind. On the surface, this sounds like a fair exchange.

Article 4.2 in the CBA states clearly "When an immediate supervisor wants to discuss an existing or potential disciplinary matter with an Employee, they shall notify the Employee in writing of the purpose and time limitation for having the meeting." Operators see these notices as "See me" slips from their chief, with the express written obligation to meet with their Chief within five days. Supervisors have not been getting this luxury. Over the last contract duration, numerous Supervisors have been intercepted while signing in or out for their shift, being dragged into an office with a Chief and a Shop Steward, and been subject to disciplinary hearings with no prior notification.

Additionally, the "Written Notifications" are being slid across the table at these very same meetings, and Supervisors are being forced to sign them in acknowledgment. Per the CBA "The Employee shall sign the proposal to suspend or discharge to acknowledge receipt of the same." At one of the meetings I attended as a Supervisor Shop Steward, this "Written Notification" stated that the meeting would be held at 10:00 AM, and the Supervisor was asked to sign it at the beginning of the meeting at 12:30 PM, while still having no idea what the meeting was about! Metro's stance on the subject is that the language of the CBA does not require a specific time frame for notification, and that this disrespectful practice is therefore "in compliance."

I assert that this is clearly NOT the intention of the CBA, nor is it fair or equitable to any degree. Compare the notifications in other parts of the CBA, for example:

Leave Donations require a full pay period of notification to central payroll. 10 days notice for SDAs being offered. Grievance language carries multiple notification time frames of 15 calendar days. Rehire notifications are at 10 and 15 calendar days. Metro wants to know about your Jury Duty as soon as you receive the summons. Sick Leave requires "reasonable notice," later defined as "10 calendar days," or "as soon as possible prior to the start of the Employee's work shift." Bids for Holiday work must occur seven days in advance. Metro wants to know information from you early, so it can plan ahead.

This is only a portion of the references. Strangely, we find that information flowing the other direction, is very much when Metro feels like it. Consider the following examples:

For VM, "With at least two-hour notice to an Employee, METRO may cancel an overtime assignment in its entirety" and "Metro will attempt to provide written notice," or "The employee may agree to shorter notice." For Supervisors, "When a shift is canceled, the unit Superintendent or designee will notify the UNION," or "a Relief Supervisor's extra assignment may be changed by more than four hours and with less than twelve hours notice."

Metro wants you to provide a fair and reasonable notice, as soon as is practicable, but thinks a fair notice to you is two and a half hours after the proposed start to a disciplinary meeting. Metro wants you to believe that an organization that consistently responds to changing current events throughout the greater King County area on a minute by minute basis is completely incapable of delivering a more timely notice to the dedicated employees that keep it running. "Life is political, not because the world cares about how you feel, but because the world reacts to what you do." ICE and Boarder Patrols' abduction of workers — designed as spectacles of terror — demands a rapid-response program.

Activists were able to get dos and don'ts out quickly because the union has an effective communication network within schools and across regions: area representatives communicate with school-based representatives, who communicate with members.

Chicago Teacher's Union organized rapid-response teams and distributed red cards with know-your-rights information. When the attacks came in earnest, the training expanded to include information about how to film ICE, how to report ICE, how to warn of a raid.

Our union, ATU587, must be seen prepared for when we face more direct attacks. When the National Guard arrived in D.C. the soldiers' assumed positions around Metro stations where students gather; students rely on Metro trains to get to school. The soldiers harassed and intimidated students.

The D.C. community—anchored by groups like Empower Ed and Free DC—responded by organizing an adult presence at the Metro stations and posted lookouts on street corners. They formed text-message groups to educate people about what was needed, how to support families, and how to track and respond to federal agents.

There is so much to put in place to have a response, we need both management and ATU 587 collaboration. Anywhere raids are happening people are traumatized and afraid – a federal spectacle of terror.

Currently those with authority appear to be thinking through the lens of liability, not resistance. We need ATU 587 and management material commitment to build out a plan and team to prepare resistance. Here is a proposed two-point plan:

  1. Every Base have an initiative-taking plan that includes NW Immigrant Rights Project (NWIRP.org) material roleplaying workshops addressing "what to do if ICE shows up", develop response teams and post signs explicitly indicating that ICE is not allowed.
  2. ATU 587 and management on going county-wide collaboration - education through video logs (Vlogs) and quarterly in-person presentations by frontline workers with everyday living experiences.

This would be a follow-up to what I started by proposing to our Atlantic Base Superintendent a monthly "Taste of Cultures" - storytelling by first and second-generation immigrants' living experiences. This initiative culminated in Metro funding with catered food the December 3rd's "Mosaic of Us: Many Cultures One Community" hosted by the Refugees and Immigrants Support and Empowerment (RISE) employee resource group, Atlantic Base Operations and Metro's EEO,

I facilitated the bringing together ATU 587's frontline workers who spoke of their living experiences from: Sudan, Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia, and S. Asia. These fellow members courageously shared personal stories of migration, resilience, and service. Their words illuminated journeys of loss and bold new beginnings, and the pride of serving the public they once stood among as newcomers. The room was quiet, tearful, and deeply connected.

The frontline speakers had a difficult challenge, striking a balance where they are experiencing their own trauma. Things that are so big and out of the norm are happening, and then you continue to have to come to work each day wondering if more trauma awaits. We need to be held responsible for creating a space that is structured and safe where the frontline worker knows and feels they are protected. We need support from ATU587 to make this not a one-and-done and be inclusive of the other nationalities of our ATU587 membership.

This is a challenge when the union is also in the middle of contract negotiations – but when guided by social purpose we can serve the common good and create long term progress. We as a union can continue to grow through future gatherings, storytelling spaces, and learning opportunities that meet our members where they are, across shifts, languages, cultures, communities, and work sites. This is community building in a changing immigration landscape.

Building Trust and Equity in Rail Operations

By Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs Officer, ATU Local 587/King County Labor Delegate · Pages 16–17 · Open in the PDF

Safety Culture and Building Trust

In September, I attended the Safety Culture Training in San Diego a four-day workshop designed to improve how organizations like King County Metro approach safety, communication, and inclusion. It was a very informative and engaging experience that reshaped how I think about safety and teamwork.

The training focused on creating a culture of trust, where both management and frontline employees feel respected and heard. It highlighted one of the biggest issues we face today the gap between management and employees when it comes to trust.

Too often, operators or supervisors who report safety concerns end up being treated as if they caused the problem, rather than being recognized for identifying a potential hazard. This reaction discourages open reporting, which in turn weakens safety across the system.

As someone who has worked at Metro for over a decade, I've seen how easily concerns can get lost in the layers of management from supervisors to chiefs, to managers, to directors. The more layers we have, the harder it becomes to get issues resolved promptly and fairly.

The training reminded me that a true safety culture depends on relationships built on respect and trust. Managers and supervisors should be seen as allies, not enforcers. When an operator speaks up about a safety issue, it should be viewed as a contribution to system improvement, not as a reason for punishment.

King County Metro's Safety Policy and Just Culture Principles support this approach, encouraging employees to report safety issues without fear of retaliation. But for this to work, it must be applied consistently across all levels of management.

When we invest in trust, both sides win operators feel valued and management gains valuable insight from the people who experience the work firsthand.

Training Equity and Language Barriers

Another area that needs serious attention is training equity, especially for minority and bilingual employees. Over the past year, many have shared experiences of unequal treatment during evaluations including feeling misunderstood because English is not their first language.

Under Article 2 of the ATU Local 587 Collective Bargaining Agreement (Non-Discrimination) and King County's Equal Employment Opportunity (EEO) Policy, every employee has the right to fair training, evaluation, and promotional opportunities, regardless of language, origin, or background.

Yet, we continue to hear stories of unfair grading practices and bias. For bilingual employees, it's not about ability or knowledge, it's about communication. Some trainees have been told they "Failed" tasks when, in reality, there was a misunderstanding due to language interpretation or unclear instructions.

This issue also connects to religious accommodation. An operator had been asked to provide written authorization from HR before being allowed to pray during training hours. That requirement directly conflicts with King County Metro's Religious Accommodation Policy and federal law under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which requires reasonable accommodation for religious practices.

At Metro, we have many Muslim employees who perform daily prayers. Their right to pray should not depend on an HR letter. Metro's policy already allows for religious accommodation unless it creates an undue hardship and taking five minutes for prayer does not meet that threshold.

Training environments should be inclusive, supportive, and fair. When an employee feels respected for who they are and what they believe, they perform better. ATU Local 587 will continue to advocate for equal access to training, transparent grading standards, and fair evaluation practices for all members, especially our multilingual and faith-based communities.

Scheduling and Contract Compliance

As Link light rail continues to expand, the workload and complexity of scheduling have increased dramatically. We now have multiple service layers Federal Way extensions, Angle Lake turnback's, Pinehurst single tracking duties, and others each with unique reporting times, layovers, and route transitions.

While expansion is a positive step, it has also led to frequent schedule adjustments, sometimes communicated to operators on short notice. This has caused confusion and frustration among many rail operators.

The ATU Local 587 Rail CBA, Article 20 (Picks and Assignments) clearly states that any change to a picked assignment must be communicated at least five (5) days in advance. This rule exists to protect operators' personal time and ensure predictability in their schedules.

However, several operators have reported receiving notice of schedule changes while they were on vacation, out on leave, or during their regular days off sometimes with less than five days' notice. This not only violates the CBA but also disrupts work-life balance, which is a key value under King County's Healthy Workplace Guidelines.

Metro's success depends on clear communication and respect for the labor agreement. Scheduling adjustments should always follow the five-day rule unless there is a documented operational emergency. When management honors the contract, it strengthens morale and builds the mutual respect that keeps our service running smoothly.

Moving Forward Together

As rail operations continue to grow, so must our commitment to fairness, inclusion, and safety. Every policy, every rule, and every action should reflect respect for the people who move this system every day, the operators, instructors, supervisors, and support staff.

We all want the same thing: a safe, respectful, and supportive workplace. That goal is only possible when everyone from the Director's office to the front line operates with trust and integrity.

The Safety Culture Training reminded me that a true culture of safety starts with listening, empathy, and fairness. Whether it's a safety report, a training challenge, or a scheduling issue, the first step is to treat every employee with dignity.

We are stronger when we stand together when we speak up for one another, support fair practices, and make sure every voice is heard. As ATU 587 members, that's not just our right, it's our responsibility.

Dangerous Rhetoric Has Real Consequences for Our Members

By Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs Officer, ATU Local 587/King County Labor Delegate · Page 17 · Open in the PDF

Recent remarks by President Trump attacking the Somali community and immigrants from Africa are not only offensive, but they are also dangerous. Referring to entire communities as "garbage" and labeling African nations with degrading language places minorities in a vulnerable position, especially those who work in public-facing jobs like transit.

Words from the President of the United States carry weight. They shape public behavior and set the tone for what some people believe is acceptable. When racist language comes from someone in such a powerful position, it can legitimize hatred and embolden individuals who already hold harmful views. For transit workers, this can translate into increased harassment, threats, and physical danger while on duty.

Many of our members spend their entire workday interacting with the public. Bus operators, rail operators, supervisors, and station staff do not have the option to walk away when tensions rise. When certain communities are singled out and dehumanized, our members from those communities often feel the impact first. They are visible, accessible, and exposed.

It is also important to be honest and acknowledge that these attitudes do not only exist outside our workplace. We have some members within our own union who share or excuse the same kinds of views expressed in the President's remarks. That reality makes this issue even more serious, because harm does not always come from strangers.

This reminds me of an incident at South Base back in 2017, where one of our own members casually referred to coworkers during a conversation as a "fish market," implying they were loud and smelly. Comments like that may be dismissed as jokes or casual remarks, but they carry racial and cultural meaning. They reduce people to stereotypes and create a hostile environment. That kind of language, even when spoken casually, reflects the same mindset behind national-level rhetoric we claim to oppose.

As someone from the Somali community, I know how painful and alarming this language can be. Many Somalis fled a brutal civil war and have lived through the consequences of dehumanization and fear-driven politics. When leaders or coworkers use similar language, it reopens old wounds and reinforces a sense of being unwelcome or unsafe.

The mocking and targeting of Muslim women, including comments about elected officials who wear hijab, is especially concerning. A woman wearing hijab on public transit should not have to worry about becoming a target because of statements made by national leaders or reinforced by everyday comments in the workplace. Yet that is the environment this rhetoric creates. In that spirit, I also encourage solidarity with our Muslim female coworkers. Those who choose to wear a hijab or scarf in support are showing a simple but meaningful sign of unity and respect, especially at a time when Muslim women are being unfairly targeted and made to feel unsafe.

Our workforce at King County Metro and throughout the county reflects the diversity of the communities we serve. We work alongside immigrants, refugees, asylum seekers, permanent residents, and U.S. citizens. These members are not outsiders. They are essential workers who keep the system moving every day.

Racist rhetoric does not differentiate between immigration status or citizenship. It does not stop to ask who was born here and who was not. Once a community is labeled as inferior or "other," everyone associated with it becomes vulnerable.

As a union, we have a responsibility to confront this honestly, both externally and internally. That means speaking out when public figures use language that puts our members at risk, and it also means addressing harmful behavior when it comes from within our own ranks. Respect on the job is not optional. It is a basic expectation.

This is a call for awareness, accountability, and solidarity. We must look out for one another, challenge harmful language when we hear it, and support members who feel targeted or unsafe. Our strength as a union comes from standing together, even when the conversation is uncomfortable.

Every member deserves dignity, safety, and respect, whether on the job, in the breakroom, or out in the community. That is not just a value we talk about. It is a responsibility we must live up to.

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 4

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
