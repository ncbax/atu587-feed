The President's Report: Benefits Bargaining Update

By Greg Woodfill · Pages 1, 20 · Open in the PDF

Who should pay for employees' health care insurance, the employer or the employee? Who should cover the increased costs?

Our answer to those questions, and our position in benefits bargaining, is that THE EMPLOYER should pay 100% of the cost. Especially when the frontline work that our members perform is routinely detrimental to their physical and mental health. We don't get to work from home, even during a pandemic.

Yes, we are the last King County Union that doesn't pay a monthly premium for our health benefits. Why should we be treated differently, we are asked? The answer is because we ARE different. Our Local has been more proactive in negotiating health care plan changes and choices that have kept our costs lower. Our members have kept costs down compared to other County Unions, and the County has pocketed some of those savings by paying less per employee into our health care reserves than it pays per month for most other county employees.

A high percentage of our membership is exposed to dangerous working conditions, assaults, abuse, and the resulting high rates of stress, PTSD, and other negative health impacts. Our members live with constant reminders that they are one injury, one bad customer interaction, or one mistake away from termination or medical separation, regardless of how many decades of public service they've provided the County. Many of our members work knowing that they could be the next Mark McLaughlin or Shawn Yim, brutally murdered while doing their job.

Other King County Unions don't have cameras recording their entire shifts, with managers who never did their jobs, making frame-by-frame video judgments that far too often result in discipline or termination without just cause. Judgments that have the benefit of hindsight on split-second decisions made while dealing with the millions of variables in our members workdays that can't all be trained for or practiced.

In our current Benefits Bargaining, the County is presenting its health care consultants' projections that our current reserve fund, which pays for our benefits, will go from a surplus of $10 million to a deficit of $25 million by 2028. If our reserve fund dips below $4 million, we are required to meet at the table to replenish the fund. Which means if the County's projections were correct, we would need to add a minimum of $29 million to the benefits reserve fund just to leave us at the $4 million reserve so we would need to add much more to have a cushion.

Let's talk about their consultants' projections. Their projections show the average 587-member health care cost will be $368 more per month in 2028 than the current $1,762 per employee, per month that the County currently contributes to the reserve fund. They project that our ATU 587 medical benefits will cost $107 million and that cost is projected to rise to $136 Million by 2029.

The County is saying what we all know, that health care costs could rise significantly in the next couple of years. But they expect our members to bear all of this increased cost by finally agreeing to a monthly premium share.

This is the same consultant that always produces estimates that show the sky is falling, so rest assured we have our own expert consultant at the negotiations table that we've always used, and who worked for the County's consultant in the past.

In all prior benefits bargaining, our Union has successfully negotiated cost savings on our benefits that have fought off a monthly premium share. In the current benefits bargaining, the County negotiators are posturing that their hands are tied and that they have marching orders for ATU 587 to pay a monthly premium share just like everyone else.

Without revealing all our strategies, I can report to the membership that the Union must consider the real possibility that we could reach an impasse in benefits bargaining that would result in mediation and arbitration. And that, for the first time, the Union presented management last week with a proposal for what we believe the membership might accept in trade for agreeing to the slippery slope of a premium share.

If we come to an agreement with the County on a package for a monthly Premium share. It will most likely require a membership vote, separate from a contract vote. In that scenario, it will be up to the membership whether the County has offered enough compensation to open up this slippery slope. Or, if the membership wants to continue holding the line, with the realistic potential that an arbitrator may be asked to decide whether we are forced to have a premium share or not, with no compensation.

Contract Bargaining Updates, or lack thereof

Early on in negotiations we posted some bargaining update bulletins and posted our initial proposals for all 55 articles on the website. Updates after that have been difficult to put out in print as we have been negotiating on a long list of minor disagreements and proposed language changes in each article. When members ask for a contract update, they want to know about agreements on wages, premiums, retro, and working conditions.

Wages, Retro, and any major changes in working conditions are negotiated and settled in the last weeks of negotiations, wages are often settled as the last item on the last day.

All economic or monetary asks throughout the contract are mostly settled as an overall package at the end of negotiations.

We have seven bargaining sessions scheduled for September. We may be able to pair these negotiations down to the core issues by the end of September. I'll continue answering bargaining questions at our Union meetings, as I've done since negotiations started. And we'll put out post-bargaining updates on any agreements reached on substantial issues going forward.

Please don't believe or spread rumors on what the Union has or will agree on for wages and on other serious issues, I've heard them all and none of them are true. Union Solidarity throughout negotiations is the best path for success.

The 1st Vice President's Report: Bang for a Buck

By Cory Rigtrup · Page 4 · Open in the PDF

This month's article is about representation and what your union dues get, bang for a buck.

Almost a year ago, the number of members losing their jobs radically increased. It wasn't just the typical new folks having a hard time fitting in, there were many senior members with 20 plus years losing their jobs. For six months we went down a dark path of heavy hands, too heavy handed discipline battles, in Metro Ops and Kitsap Ops. Metro Supervision has also been brutal, but that is partially self-imposed by our own members.

Something clearly had changed. In between breaths, we were hearing the same message from managers in King County as we did in Kitsap County, we are "trying to prevent another." Message received, but the answer is wrong.

We all share the same concerns, how to protect Operators from the dangers found in the public. The employer's answer was to terminate the Operator, because an interaction was less than stellar. That is why we went through six months of confrontational termination hearings. A number of these were epic, profane, emotional and sometimes with police escorts. There are scares on my soul, as I struggle to separate personal from professional.

The right answer has always been to support the Operator. If the old ways don't work, or if there are new dangers, then we need to find new ways. There is progress on that front, with new barriers and transit reform making headway, but this article is not about that. This article is about where your dues money goes, and why it's so important to support each other.

If you have been to any of our Union meetings in the last year, you likely participated in a request for funds to pursue a grievance to arbitration. In these meetings, we try to explain what the grievance is about and answer any questions best we can. Often times the member who filed the grievance is there, and lately we've been showing video evidence if we have any. It is our duty as members, to approve, or deny arbitration request. Your vote and paycheck determines where it's the end of the road for the member, or we support them till the bitter end.

August Update

Myers PERC Mediation

Brother Myers was a Kitsap Operator who was issued a suspension in pay for telling a passenger there are no courtesy rides. Brother Myers was spit on by a guy in camouflage, then suspended in pay from Kitsap. On August 5, with our lawyers and the help of Washington state PERC, an agreement was reached that restored Brother Myers' lost wages and accruals. Brother Myers has since left Kitsap Transit, and we wish him well in his next chapter in life.

Alemu Arbitration

Bother Alemu is an East base Operator, who was issued a suspension in pay for recording an incident on his coach, where he feared it would only be used for posterity purposes. A settlement was reached on August 12 resolving the grievance just weeks before the arbitration was scheduled. Mr. Alemu's lost wages and accruals have been restored. If you see brother Alemu give him a thanks for standing up for what is right!

Dailey Arbitration

Brother Dailey is currently a Rail Operator, who filed a grievance almost three years ago when he was rehired as a bus Operator. The issue was over restoring his wage rate, per policy. Due to a calamity of events, the arbitration was rescheduled for August 18th and 19th. On the eve of arbitration, a settlement agreement was reached that makes brother Dailey whole! This resolution was a long time in the making, brother Dailey's grievance kept getting pushed back to allow termination grievances to have priority.

Borges Arbitration

Brother Borges was a Rail Technical Trainer, who failed to meet standards while on probation as a Technical Trainer. Rather than sending Brother Borges back to one of his previously held Operator job classifications, either in Rail or Bus, Rail terminated his employment. Brother Borges' employment was not terminated for cause, Rail believes members have no return rights to their previous job classification if a member accepts a promotion to another 587 classification. The consequences and precedent setting of this arbitration would have massive implications for the entire membership. A non-precedent agreement was reached on the eve of arbitration, August 17th, that restored brother Borges to his previous Bus Operator classification. We welcome brother Borges back. If your paths cross, give him thanks, especially if you have any promotional ambitions!

Bang for a Buck

Every year, our Local adds up all the cost we spend defending members' grievances. We take that dollar amount and divide it by the numbers of members. That number is then divided by the two paychecks each year in which regular dues are not taken and deducted from each member. This is the cost each of us bears, support one another.

Just for persecutive, three out of four grievances mentioned above, the members had no prior discipline in their files. By all definitions, they were considered "an ideal employee." Think of this like insurance, you are paying for it in advance, 'cause you just never know.

Panel on Workers Decide: The Future of Work

Katie Durkin, WSFE, spoke on the future of AI. She spoke that this is not new for us, workers have faced having to deal with advances in technology in the past. The difference is, we weren't excluded. When we exclude workers from AI talks, this is where the issue on labor begins. There's a lot of impacts of AI on the workers and the public and having the right to bargain over this is important. The only way we can accomplish this is by making sure the workers are in the room where AI talks are on going that have a direct impact on workers. Employees of the State of Washington don't get to have a say in their workplace.

Matt Lim, IBT 117, spoke on AI being more of a digital supervisor. This technology is used in their work trucks. So, the Union wrote language around this to only work when needed, like accidents or hard braking. They also implemented in their CBA that when new versions of AI is onboarded, the language will reflect what it can or can't do. Spoke to future proofing the language to always be ahead of the implantation of AI. Spoke to really engaging and educating the members.

Jessica Hauffe, WSNA, spoke on the impacts of AI in the nursing industry. Nurses should be involved in every aspect of the creation of AI. This would ensure that nurses are involved in the process to be sure the product is working correctly, this would also help make sure the correct products are being purchased. She spoke to AI not being able to do what boots on the ground nurses do. Spoke on HB 2155 that was signed into law June 11, 2026, that updates Washington Nurse's Practice Act to make clear that only licensed human nurses can use the nurse title(s). This bill prevents AI from presenting themselves as nurses.

Workshops: Lobbying for Non-lobbyists

Jay C. Joy, OR AFL-CIO, spoke on what is lobbying. He spoke about lobby day and the importance of all workers showing up at these sessions. Spoke about a fiscal note, the amount per bi annum. It's an estimate of what the cost should be to purchase certain items required to remain operational. Who is required to register as a lobbyist? Those who're part of the AFL-CIO or other similar organizations. How to lobby? Engage with your local government, keep an eye on what your state council is doing. This information is accessible by a simple website search. The best part of lobbying within your local government is that your voices are heard by your elected officials. You can lobby for your Union here. Most times elected officials do care about what Unions have to say about certain bills, so our voices in these spaces prove to be paramount to speaking up for our working rights and things that directly impact our jobs.

Providing public comment

Set time (1 minute, 90 seconds, 2 minutes, etc.). Often this is in person or virtual, provide your name and the name of the organization you're affiliated with, and tell the story of why you care. Law makers tend to care a lot more about those who show up for public comments to speak about issues that can have a direct impact on their work.

The legislative session is around the corner. The legislative session includes coming to lobby day, which will allow you at 15min max to talk about those issues that impact their community. Planning is on its way for policy work to prep for the upcoming legislative session.

Since WSLC won expansion of the PFML, this win has been expanded to include all families, chosen family, etc., not just one's nuclear family. There are more ways to improve the program by expanding to those who're domestic abuse victims.

At the workshop, stories from those in attendance were shared on the positive impact of using PFML, A lot of politicians, even some Republicans, have been in support of the program. I was able to share with the group the positive impacts of those who are injured off the job and need to utilize the program and how this was a great help to many of our members who have had to use the program, and why it's extremely important that we continue to fight legislation to negatively alter it. This is why I will be at the Capital during the next legislative session to help fight against the proposed cuts to the program.

There have been several bills presented by Republicans to severely alter PFML. For one, public employees wouldn't have the ability to supplement their income using short term disability or long-term disability. On the plus side, the vast majority supports taxing higher wages rather than cutting benefits.

I also attended the Know your Rights and Lobbying for Non-Lobbyists workshops. At Know your Rights workshop, this workshop was geared towards providing an overview of fundamental rights in the workplace and the community. Participants will learn what rights apply to all workers, how to assert those rights, and what to do if approached by law enforcement or government agencies, including immigration enforcement. This workshop will also cover recent immigration policy updates and current enforcement practices that may impact your workforce.

Researching AI in the Workplace

At this workshop, I participated in a meet and greet, where we talked about new technologies at the workplace. I talked about the Zonar system that hasn't been bargained for yet, but operators were being trained on the system. We also talked about Disney using a computer-generated orchestra in place of real trained people who normally would play in the orchestra. There were also stories of AI being used for grading in the medical field, stories of news anchors controlling their own teleprompter, AI being used that creates biases, such as driverless vehicles being tested in affluent white communities instead of other communities.

I truly enjoy attending the WSLC, I love the ability to network with Labor from all over the State. I also find the curriculum at the convention to be truly engaging, informative, and something I'm able to use to help all members. There's always something for us to fight against, and I love being one of those willing to take our fight to our law makers!

In solidarity!

Reread 101: What Every Metro Driver Should Know

By Michael Mackay, Executive Board Officer #1 · Pages 9–10 · Open in the PDF

Getting into an accident is stressful enough without me wondering what happens next? If Metro judges my accident as Preventable and I don't agree, I have the right to challenge that call. This is a plain-language guide to the accident Reread process – what it is and how it works.

What Is a Reread?

A "reread" is a formal request to have Metro's preventability determination on an accident re-examined. It's built into the Accident Policy Matrix in Policy 003, and it exists so that no operator is stuck with a "Preventable" label without a second look.

How a Determination Happens in the First Place

Before a reread ever comes into play, here's the process that gets you there:

  1. Accident Report — You get into an accident and fill out the accident report. Reports are filled out in Origami (SSaM- Metro's Safety and Security Management system); this auto-notifies your Base/Section Supervisor.
  2. Investigation — A Transit Safety Administrator (TSA) reviews your report and any other evidence that can be found, such as … an accident report from the Service Quality Supervisor, Pictures, Witness (courtesy cards), and any video footage from the coach or surrounding coaches or area. This information is used to determine if you could have done ANYTHING differently to Prevent this accident from happening. Remember it is NOT about Fault, but Preventability!
  3. Accident Classification — An accident is classified in one of five categories. Metro follows the National Safety Council NSC Accident Preventability Guidelines.
    • a. Preventable — An incident where the driver failed to do everything they reasonably could to avoid the collision, regardless of right-of-way or adverse conditions
    • b. Non-Preventable — An incident where the driver did everything reasonable to avoid the crash and committed NO defensive driving errors themselves (such as being properly parked or legally stopped)
    • c. Unsubstantiated / Undetermined / No Judgment Required — These terms typically appear in administrative, municipal, or specialized internal review boards rather than the core NSC commercial vehicle framework. Undetermined applies when investigation narratives or reports lack sufficient evidence or detail to assign fault or preventability. No Judgment Required or Unsubstantiated applies when policy dictates zero fault-finding, or when claims lack supporting proof.
    • d. Severity Level — If you receive a "Preventable" judgment, the accident is then categorized into one of three levels of severity. Minor, Major, or Severe — each carrying its own accident-point value under the Preventable Accident Review System (PARS).
  4. Meeting w/Chief Accident Results – Your Chief will have received the compiled accident information with the TSA's judgment and will discuss it with you before anything else happens. If you believe the judgment is wrong, you have the right to request a reread. This is where the clock starts.

This is where the reread process begins….

The Reread Process, Step by Step

Step 1 — Determination - Transit Safety Administrator (TSA)

Your Chief will give you the Accident Determination. If you disagree with the TSA judgment, you can request a reread — within 7 days of notification. You will get a notification of where and when your Reread will be.

Step 2 — Accident Reread Committee

Before your hearing, you (accompanied by an Executive Board Member and a Shop Steward) have the right to sit down with your Base Chief or Safety Officer and review the entire accident packet, including the video used to make the call. At the hearing, this second-step panel includes a Chief, a Union representative, and the TSA — the TSA only votes if there's a tie. Bring any additional information that supports your case.

Step 3 — Accident Review Board (Appeal)

Disagree with the reread decision? You can appeal to the Accident Review Board, but you must personally request it within 10 days of the reread decision through the Transit Safety Section. This board is made up of two senior employees with good driving records (assigned that month) and two superintendents from Operations, Service Quality, Transit Control Center, or Safety. A safety representative chairs the board but does not vote.

Step 4 — Extraordinary Review (Tie Vote)

If the Accident Review Board can't reach a majority, the case goes to the National Safety Council in Washington, D.C. for a final determination. If the Council's review comes back non-determinative, the accident reverts to being classified under the Step 2 standard.

A note for members: If you're not sure which version applies to your case, ask your Shop Steward or Executive Board member to walk you through it before your hearing.

Ground Rules Worth Remembering

  • Postponements: Notify the Safety Section at least one hour before your scheduled hearing at (206) 684-2810. You get a maximum of two postponements per hearing — this limit applies to you, any representative, and any panel member. If you're granted a postponement, you must resubmit a new Reread/Review Request within 10 days to get rescheduled.
  • Show up. If any party misses a third scheduled appointment for any reason, the judgment goes against them.
  • You're not alone. You have the right to bring an Executive Board Member and/or a Shop Steward to review your packet and represent you at the hearing.
  • No pay outside regular hours. None of these hearing steps are paid if scheduled outside your normal work hours.
  • Check your file annually. ATU 587 recommends reviewing your accident file once a year to confirm it's accurate.
  • Full disclosure. Base Chiefs must share the full packet — video, TSA report, service quality notes, and photos — when a reread is requested.

How Accident Points Work

Once an accident is ruled preventable, it's assigned a severity level (Minor, Major, or Severe) and a corresponding point value under PARS. Those points are tallied across four rolling windows — 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, and 4-year periods — all counting back from the date of the accident being reviewed. Your chief applies the required training or discipline action based on whichever time window shows your highest point total.

On the flip side, Safe Driving Points work in your favor: for every 12 months of accident-free driving between two preventable accidents, three points come off your total — potentially wiping your record clean if you've gone long enough without an incident. The one exception: points earned before a severe accident can't be used to offset it.

Depending on where your point total lands, the response can range from a coaching-style Consultation & Ride Check, to a 1-Day Refresher, to a 3-Day Accident Retraining — and repeated or higher-point accidents can escalate to suspension, accident probation, or discharge. Because the exact point thresholds and suspension lengths involve several overlapping tables, always confirm your specific numbers with your chief or Shop Steward rather than estimating from memory.

The Bottom Line

We have real, contractual leverage if we think a preventability call is wrong — but the clock starts moving fast (7 days to request a reread, 10 days to appeal).

Know your deadlines, request your full accident packet up front, bring our Shop Steward and Executive Board rep, and you shouldn't be afraid to ask questions before we walk into that hearing room.

Questions about your own accident file or an upcoming reread? Reach out to your Base Chief, Shop Steward, or ATU Local 587 Executive Board.

Get To Know CISM

By Peter Gladhart, Metro Transit Operator, #22405 · Page 10 · Open in the PDF

Critical Injury Stress Management, or CISM, was created in the early 1970's to help firefighters and paramedics process traumatic stress after difficult calls. To this day, many high-stress professional services including law enforcement, firefighters, healthcare, the military, disaster relief workers, and public transportation workers use a CISM team to help their employees cope with traumatic situations. At King County Metro, the Critical Incident Stress Management team members come from throughout the entire system – rail, streetcar, vehicle maintenance, facilities, and bus operations – offering support that is peer-to-peer and confidential. As employees of King County, our work provides countless opportunities to see and experience difficult situations, and most of the time, the coping strategies we have developed are strong enough and positive enough that we can manage through the difficult stuff.

A particular situation I remember from when I was a new operator occurred while operating my coach along 4th Ave S. and South Lander Street. I watched a young family crossing the street in the hot afternoon sun; they were pushing strollers full of babies and pulling carts packed with possessions. They appeared homeless and seemed to have no real destination. I remember feeling very sad for that family, no judgment on how they got there, just a deep sadness that that is where they were. As I worked the rest of the day, I just could not shake that image. I was raising my own young kids at the time and watching family out in the hot sun, made me feel anxious about the responsibilities of raising a family. At the end of the day, that image and my anxious feelings stuck with me, and I took them home. I had a close family member who had been a Metro operator for over three decades and I spoke with him about it. I also used some of my own positive personal coping mechanisms to help me work through my feelings. I was able to process and move on.

Most of us who work at Metro have had experiences that affect us. Also, most of us have developed strong coping strategies that allow us to let many stressful things go. In rare instances, you may carry images, experiences, situations, or feelings from your work environment that are hard to shake. When your usual coping skills are overwhelmed, reaching out to CISM can be beneficial. All CISM members are trained to provide confidential support to assist you in processing and recovery. Call the Metro CISM Team message line anytime at 206 718-9734 to receive confidential, free, peer to peer services for crisis and critical incident support.

Bus Bridges: $8 Million for Private Contractors to Do Work We Already Do

By Andy Price, Shop Steward, ATU Local 587 · Pages 12–13 · Open in the PDF

During the World Cup, I started noticing something unusual at Sound Transit's South Bellevue Station, where I regularly lay over as a Metro operator: private motorcoaches — a lot of them.

Bus bridges are nothing new to Metro. When they're needed, operators can be assigned or called in, given the routing and reporting instructions, and put to work — much like the special assignments Metro has handled for major events for years.

Many of the coaches I was seeing were Northwest Navigator buses from Portland. I asked one of their operators if they were union, and he told me they were not.

My first step was to bring what I was seeing to the attention of our Union President. He had not previously been informed that these private operators were being brought in for the World Cup operation and indicated that he would look into it.

I also wanted to know exactly what was going on, who had contracted for the service and what it was costing. So I filed a public records request with Sound Transit.

What came back was pretty eye-opening.

Sound Transit entered into a contract with Transportation Management Services (TMS) for World Cup Bus Charter Services. The contract states that total compensation will not exceed $8.01 million.

The fully burdened rate was $241.50 per hour for each bus with operator. The contract even states that lodging and hospitality accommodations provided for drivers were covered by that rate.

Sound Transit's contingency plan called for 80 private coaches — 50 non-ADA and only 30 ADA-accessible buses.

Think about that. The majority of an 80-bus private fleet intended to provide replacement public transit service was identified in Sound Transit's own plan as non-ADA.

But the part that really makes me question the logic of this arrangement is what they did with those buses.

The plan called for TMS buses to circulate continuously along designated bus-bridge routes without passengers during their coverage periods. In other words, they were driving around empty so they would know the routes and be positioned if Link went down.

I saw some of the problems firsthand. At South Bellevue, I saw private coaches entering areas of the park-and-ride the wrong way.

Metro operators already know these streets, transit centers and park-and-rides. We already do bus bridges. We already do special-event work. We have an Extra Board and overtime procedures for putting additional operators to work when they're needed.

And Sound Transit had years to prepare.

This is supposed to be a regional transit system. Sound Transit already has established public transit partners, experienced operators and equipment right here in the region. Instead of using those years to strengthen that existing capacity, it committed millions of dollars to bringing in private coaches and operators who then had to drive around empty learning the territory.

How does that make sense?

Sound Transit's own records show how directly this private operation overlapped with work Metro already performs.

On World Cup game days, TMS was assigned Link bus-bridge coverage from start of service through end of service.

On non-game days, Metro covered 4:00 a.m. to 7:00 a.m. and 7:00 p.m. through the end of service, while TMS covered 7:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m.

Metro remained available for additional bus-bridge assistance.

So Metro was part of the plan. Metro was trusted to provide the work during certain hours and additional assistance when needed. Yet the largest block of scheduled coverage went to the private contractor.

And it wasn't just Link.

Sound Transit also planned to use TMS charter buses to replace cancelled Sounder S Line Train 1518 on June 15, 19 and 24 and carry regular commuters. Sound Transit's established alternatives in that corridor include ST Express service operated by Pierce Transit, whose operators are represented by our sister ATU Local 758.

That raises an important labor question for both Locals: when work that can be performed through Sound Transit's existing unionized public transit partners is instead planned in advance for private contractors, what protections do Section 13(c) and our collective bargaining agreements provide for that work?

There's also a question for the taxpayers.

Sound Transit receives substantial local tax revenue from people in King, Snohomish and Pierce counties. In fact, taxes make up more than half of Sound Transit's total funding.

Those taxpayers voted to build and operate a regional public transportation system. Yet Sound Transit was prepared to commit up to $8.01 million to temporary private bus capacity instead of using years of advance notice to strengthen the public transit workforce and capacity already here.

And the timing makes that question even harder to ignore.

This November, Seattle voters are being asked to approve a new 10-year, 0.3% transit sales tax, replacing the expiring Seattle Transit Measure. The City estimates it would raise about $138 million a year, with the majority going toward additional bus service.

That is a separate City of Seattle measure, not a Sound Transit tax. But taxpayers don't experience public transit budgets as an accounting exercise. They experience them every time they're asked to reach into their pockets for more money to provide better transit.

So before we continually ask taxpayers for more, maybe public agencies should also be prepared to answer a simple question about the money they already have:

Are we getting the most public transit we can for our public transit dollars?

We had the operators. We had the experience. We had established public transit partners. And we had years to prepare.

Why did Sound Transit spend millions going outside instead of investing in the public transit system and workforce that were already here?

Two Years of Questions: When Equal Opportunity Ends at the Door to the Cab

By Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs Representative ATU587 · Pages 14–15 · Open in the PDF

August marks two years since serious concerns began to emerge about what appears to be a troubling pattern affecting minority candidates seeking to become successful train operators.

This is not simply about one employee, one training class, or one disciplinary decision. It is about a pattern that deserves to be examined openly, fairly, and without fear.

For many of our members, the path to becoming a train operator begins in the classroom. There, candidates are evaluated on their knowledge, rules, procedures, and ability to learn the technical requirements of the job. What is deeply concerning is the pattern we have witnessed involving minority trainees who perform successfully in the classroom, pass written examinations, and demonstrate that they understand the material only to encounter serious problems once hands-on training and practical evaluations begin.

When a trainee succeeds in the classroom but repeatedly fails at the point where hands-on evaluation begins, we have to ask an uncomfortable question:

Are we measuring performance consistently, or are different standards being applied to different people?

That question cannot simply be dismissed as criticism of management or training personnel. It is a legitimate question about fairness, transparency, and equal opportunity.

King County and Sound Transit have publicly committed themselves to principles of equal employment opportunity and nondiscrimination. Those principles must mean more than words in a policy manual or statements on a website. Equal opportunity must exist in the classroom, in practical training, during mainline qualification, and at the final step toward graduation.

Article R.4.7.C. of the Agreement clearly provides that employees discharged or removed during their probationary period are not subject to the grievance and arbitration procedures. However, it also explicitly guarantees those employees the right to request a termination review. The request must be made within 15 days of notification of discharge, after which RAIL is required to schedule the review and provide the UNION with a written response within a reasonable time.

What is deeply troubling is that training personnel have reportedly told members who are discharged during probation that they are entitled to the termination review, but that "nothing will change" and that their discharge will not be overturned. If this is being communicated to employees, it raises serious concerns about whether the termination-review process is being treated as a meaningful contractual right or merely as a procedural formality.

A contractual right should not be undermined before an employee even has the opportunity to exercise it. Telling employees in advance that the outcome is predetermined discourages them from pursuing their rights and creates the appearance that the review process is designed to protect the decision already made rather than fairly examine the circumstances surrounding the discharge.

Even more concerning are situations where employees and helpers have raised legitimate concerns about fairness, unequal treatment, or possible bias, yet those concerns appear to have been dismissed while the underlying decisions remain unchanged. When people who are expected to stand up for fairness knowingly remain silent in the face of potential injustice and inequality, it sends a particularly damaging message to minority employees.

This is why Article R4.7.C. must be taken seriously. The termination review should be an actual opportunity for the employee and the UNION to raise concerns, examine the circumstances, and determine whether the discharge was handled fairly and consistently not a process where employees are told from the beginning that nothing can or will change.

For minority employees, the issue goes beyond a single termination. When a pattern develops in which concerns are repeatedly raised but ignored, employees can reasonably begin to question whether they are receiving equal treatment and equal protection under the Agreement. Fairness cannot simply be a principle written into a contract; it must be demonstrated through the way employees are treated when they exercise their contractual rights.

The Pattern Cannot Be Ignored

Over the past two years, minority trainees have raised concerns about being able to successfully complete the training process despite demonstrating that they possess the knowledge and skills necessary to advance.

The concern becomes particularly serious when a trainee repeatedly succeeds in academic evaluations but is ultimately deemed "unsafe or not meeting standard and denied the opportunity before On Job Training OJT."

Safety is not negotiable. Every operator must meet the same safety standards. But safety must also be evaluated objectively, consistently, and according to clearly established criteria.

If one employee is considered qualified after making certain mistakes while another employee is removed from training for comparable conduct, the union has a responsibility to ask why.

If the same performance is acceptable for one trainee but unacceptable for another, we need to know what changed.

And if a trainee is told that the issue is "performance concerns," only to later have the allegation characterized as "cheating" or another form of misconduct, the employee deserves a clear explanation and a fair opportunity to defend themselves.

The case of Sheikh Mohamed (not his real name)

The case of Sheikh Mohamed illustrates why these questions cannot simply be ignored.

Sheikh Mohamed successfully completed his examinations and progressed through mainline training. By all appearances, he had reached the final stages of becoming a qualified rail operator.

Yet instead of graduating, he was removed from the process and denied his rail qualification and certificate based initially on allegations described as "performance concerns."

After concerns were raised about the decision, the characterization of the allegations reportedly changed to "cheating," which carries a substantially different implication and can expose an employee to allegations of misconduct.

This raises fundamental questions.

  1. What exactly was the original allegation? And What evidence supported it?
  2. Why did the characterization change? And Was Sheik Mohamed given a meaningful opportunity to respond to the allegations?
  3. Were other trainees evaluated under the same standard? And were comparable cases reviewed?

And most importantly, would the same decision have been made if the employee were not a member of a minority community?

These are not unreasonable questions. They are the questions a union should be asking whenever an employee's career is placed in jeopardy.

History Teaches Us Why We Must Pay Attention

America has a long history of discrimination that was once defended as normal procedure.

People were denied housing because of the color of their skin. People were denied employment opportunities because of race. Communities were excluded from opportunities that others took for granted. Over time, many of those practices became recognized for what they were: discrimination. The lesson from that history is not that every adverse employment decision is automatically discrimination. The lesson is that patterns of unequal outcomes deserve scrutiny, particularly when the people affected repeatedly come from the same protected communities.

We cannot tell workers in 2026 that equal opportunity exists simply because a policy says it exists. We must be able to demonstrate it.

Equal Opportunity Must Be Measurable

If King County and Sound Transit are serious about their commitments to equal employment opportunity, then the agencies should welcome an objective examination of training outcomes.

The union should be asking for meaningful data, including:

  1. How many trainees entered the program during the past two years? And how many successfully graduated?
  2. How many were removed from training? And at what stage were trainees removed?
  3. How many were removed during classroom training versus practical or hands-on training?
  4. What were the documented reasons for removal? And Were minority trainees disproportionately removed during practical evaluations?
  5. Were the same standards applied consistently across all trainees? And how are allegations of unsafe/not meet standard performance documented?
  6. What appeal or review process is available to trainees? And Who independently reviews disputed training decisions?

These questions are not an attack on safety.

The Responsibility of Minority Affairs

As Minority Affairs, I have repeatedly raised concerns about the experiences of minority employees in transit. I do not raise these concerns because I want different standards for minority employees.

I raise them because minority employees deserve the same standards, the same opportunities, the same due process, and the same chance to succeed as everyone else.

That distinction is important.

Equality does not mean giving someone a shortcut. Equality means making sure nobody is placed on a harder road because of who they are.

For two years, we have watched this issue develop. We have heard concerns from employees and observed outcomes that demand further examination.

Equal opportunity is not a slogan. It is a standard that must be lived, measured, and defended.

WSLC Resolution 2026.32: Resolution to Adopt a Transit Workers' Bill of Rights

By Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs Representative ATU587 · Page 17 · Open in the PDF

Washington State Labor Council, AFL-CIO. Resolution 2026.32, submitted by Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs Representative ATU587.

WHEREAS, transit workers provide an essential public service and are responsible for the safe transportation of thousands of passengers every day; and,

WHEREAS, transit workers work under demanding conditions that require sustained concentration while navigating traffic, weather, road hazards, passenger interactions, and long hours; and,

WHEREAS, safe working conditions, respect in the workplace, and adequate facilities are fundamental rights that protect workers, passengers, and the public alike; and,

WHEREAS, ensuring these rights promotes safety, improves service reliability, strengthens workforce retention, and supports the health and well-being of transit workers; now therefore be it,

RESOLVED, that the Washington State Labor Council, AFL-CIO (WSLC) endorse the following Transit Workers' Bill of Rights and advocate for its implementation by employers, and all relevant public authorities.

Transit Workers' Bill of Rights

Every transit worker has the right to:

  1. A decent and adequate rest break during the working day, recognizing that driver fatigue is a significant safety risk. Operators must remain focused despite traffic, passenger interactions, weather conditions, and reduced visibility, and should be provided at least a one hour lunch as an opportunity to rest.
  2. A safe, roadworthy, and properly maintained vehicle that meets all applicable safety standards before and during service.
  3. Access to clean, sanitary, and properly maintained restroom and rest facilities throughout all transit routes and work locations.
  4. Report safety, security, and workplace concerns without fear of retaliation, intimidation, discipline, or discrimination from employers, transit agencies, or any other authority.
  5. Recover from serious illness without harassment or pressure to return to work when under the care of a licensed medical provider and covered by appropriate medical documentation.
  6. The Right to Use Protected Leave Without Retaliation or Loss of Opportunity. No worker should suffer adverse consequences for exercising their legal right to use protected leave. Employees shall not be subjected to retaliation, harassment, discipline, discrimination, loss of earnings, or be precluded from promotion, advancement, training, or other career opportunities because they have used protected leave or exercised any legally protected workplace right.
  7. Receive timely, relevant, and ongoing safety and operational training necessary to safely perform their duties and respond to changing workplace conditions.
  8. Have all workplace rules, policies, and disciplinary procedures provided in writing, clearly communicated, and readily accessible to all employees.
  9. Be treated with dignity and respect by employers, management, transit agencies, passengers, and the general public, free from harassment, discrimination, abuse, or violence.
  10. A fully functioning air conditioning system in the operator's cab to protect against excessive summer heat and maintain safe working conditions.
  11. A fully functioning heating system in the operator's cab to protect against cold weather and ensure safe and healthy working conditions.

RESOLVED, that the WSLC support legislation, collective bargaining efforts, workplace policies, and public education initiatives that advance and protect these rights for all transit workers throughout Washington State; and be it finally,

RESOLVED, that copies of this resolution be provided to affiliated unions, transit agencies, elected officials, and other stakeholders to encourage the adoption and implementation of the Transit Workers' Bill of Rights.

Is Metro's Workplace Harming You?

By Chuck Lare, a Shop Steward & Safety Committee Chair · Page 18 · Open in the PDF

Workplace stress contributes to at least 120,000 deaths each year and up to $190 billion in health care costs, according to research by two Stanford professors and a former Stanford doctoral student now at Harvard Business School. (https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/why-your-workplace-might-be-killing-you)

If Metro were serious about managing operator health and controlling its own health care costs, it would look hard at the environment operators work in every day. Instead, operators think twice before calling the Transit Control Center for help, never knowing if the response will be dismissive or hostile.

Wellness programs urging employees to eat better or exercise are of marginal value when Metro ignores the workplace conditions driving the stress in the first place. As the Stanford researchers put it, "your tendency to overeat, overdrink, and take drugs" is shaped by your workplace. Metro management has it backwards — treating the symptoms of stress instead of the root cause: the environment itself. One researcher put it plainly: "Companies need to get serious about creating a workplace where people feel valued, trusted, and respected, where they are engaged."

The Screen vs. The Windshield

Inside Metro's climate-controlled offices, the language of the day is "Equity, Inclusion, and Belonging." Dashboards, equity metrics, and mobility-framework mandates fill the screens of planners and policymakers. For the thousands of operators in the driver's seat, that language hits a wall of reality at the dashboard.

Decision-makers watch operations through a screen, chasing "speed-ups" to keep buses on time. Operators watch it through a windshield — living Metro's "turn-and-burn" culture: skipped breaks, threats of discipline for running late, and a fear of retaliation that suppresses reporting and keeps the metrics looking good. The result is a growing sentiment on the frontline: when it comes to Equity, Inclusion, and Belonging, operators are second-class.

For administrators, equity is data aggregation — tracked in spreadsheets, drafted in secure conference rooms. For the operator, equity is a daily, face-to-face negotiation with societal crises: regional housing shortages, untreated behavioral health emergencies, and systemic underinvestment, seen up close through the windshield. While the "screen class" enjoys hybrid schedules and administrative safety, the "windshield class" is tethered to rigid schedules and punitive management, absorbing public frustration with zero authority to change the systems they're forced to manage.

Policy vs. Practice

Metro's lack of real EIB implementation for frontline staff shows up in three places:

  • The Unsafe Schedule Trap. Administrators celebrate expanded route hours on their screens. But because of chronic labor shortages, that expansion is extracted straight from operators' physical and mental well-being through inadequate recovery and break time. On a screen, more service looks like progress. Behind the wheel, it's burnout — and unsafe conditions.
  • The Safety Chasm. For years, bureaucratic inertia — if not outright ambivalence — has slowed deployment of operator security partitions and behavioral health specialists who could address root causes instead of just moving problems around. While administrators debate structural reform on paper, operators face rising exposure to real hazards. The murder of operator Shawn Yim, and the countless operators living with PTSD, expose a painful truth: frontline safety is treated as a logistical afterthought, not an urgent equity mandate.
  • The Compliance Checklist. Metro has never provided operators with training suited to the war-zone conditions they face — nothing like the Red Kite Project. Instead, training is doled out as discipline, or comes from programs like AVADE, built by office workers for office workers, that fail to address the acute, high-stress, violent scenarios drivers face in real time. Real belonging means worker voices shape policy. Instead, operators remain passive recipients of administrative edicts.

Dismantling the Second-Class Tier

If King County Metro wants to be a genuinely anti-racist, pro-equity organization, its leadership has to close the gap between the screen and the windshield. There is no real belonging while the workers who physically move this city feel expendable to the agency that employs them.

EIB in a transit agency has to start from the ground up, with accountability measured by those living the experience — prioritizing operators' physical safety, mental health, and scheduling autonomy ahead of new administrative targets. Until what happens behind the windshield carries more weight than what shows up on the screen, Metro's EIB framework is an incomplete promise — and its most vital frontline ambassadors will keep feeling like second-class citizens.

Keep Your Address Current!! · page 15

(A request from our Local 8 Union office staff) Throughout the year Local 587 mails letters to our membership. With each mailing sent, the union receives a small percentage of letters returned due to improper address.

Local 587 maintains a database that in part includes the names and addresses of our King County Metro members. The King County Metro section of the database is updated monthly from data provided by King County Metro.

If you are a King County Metro employee and your name and address is not current with King County Metro, you may not receive Union mailings. Please keep your name and address current with King County Metro.

Month-long observances: National Latinx Heritage Month (September 15 – October 15); German American Heritage Month (September 15 – October 15); National Guide Dog Month.

  • 7 – Labor Day — The first parade occurred in New York City on September 5, 1882. The workers' unions chose the first Monday in September because it was halfway between Independence Day and Thanksgiving. At the time, people worked an average of 60 hours a week, but unions were agitating for shorter workweeks and more paid days off.

Articles/Letters to the Editor · page 3

Letters/contributions must include printed names, signatures, work ID numbers, addresses and phone numbers that can be verified during working hours. Members must be in good standing to make submissions. Letters that cannot be validated will not be published. All articles/letters are subject to editing and should be limited to 1000 words or less. Not all letters can be published due to space limitations. Cut off is 5:00 p.m. on the 15th of each month, if the 15th falls on the weekend/holiday, cut off is close of business on the following business day. Any submission from a member of Local 587 to the News Review deemed unprintable by the Recording Secretary shall be forwarded to the Executive Board for final decision to publish. Send letters and articles as original Word documents to Latrelle Gibson at: recsec@atu587.org

ATU 587 Oath of Obligation · page 5

I, .................................., in the presence of God and the members of this Union, do solemnly promise and pledge (without any reservation or evasion) to support the Constitution and General Laws of this Amalgamated Transit Union. I will keep myself in good standing by paying all dues, fines and assessments required. I will work to promote the best interests of this Union and encourage my fellow workers to become members of the same. I will not take the place of any member of this organization, or any other union worker who may be on strike or locked out. I will not reveal any of the private business to anyone not entitled to know the same.

I will not engage in activity evidencing gross disloyalty nor in conduct otherwise unbecoming a member, and will not knowingly wrong a member or see one wronged if in my power to prevent it. I will not appeal to any legal authority in matters pertaining to this Union until I have exhausted all means of redress provided by its laws. I will be considerate of others in word and action and will never discriminate against a fellow worker on account of race, religion, color, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age, disability, citizenship, or national origin. I also promise to promote the cause of trade union principles and defend freedom of thought, whether expressed by tongue or pen, with all the power at my command.

I understand there is nothing in this obligation that will in any way interfere with my political and religious rights or that will be in any way inconsistent with my duties arising from any of the relations of life.

This obligation I take upon my honor, and solemnly promise to keep the same, as long as I remain a member of this Union.
