Another month has brought more discipline and discharges without just cause at King County Metro and Kitsap Transit—and another unprecedented wave of cases heading to or piling up in arbitration.

About a year ago at King County Metro, and more recently at Kitsap Transit, something changed. Both agencies moved away from corrective discipline and returned to punitive measures. They now seem more focused on limiting liability than on supporting, rehabilitating, and strengthening the frontline workforce in which they have invested so heavily—and which is so costly to recruit, train, retain, and replace.

Ten to 30 years of public service delivering transit with a clean work record now seem to carry little weight when discipline is imposed for one bad day or a splitsecond questionable decision later dissected frame by frame, in slow motion, from a desk and with the full benefit of hindsight.

Grievance responses routinely say that years of service and a clean record were considered, but those statements read like boilerplate designed to create the appearance that just cause was applied.

Management representatives who meet with us to discuss more progressive, corrective, and appropriate discipline now claim they lack authority to reach agreements—even though the Union and management have made such agreements for decades. The Union has made clear that it will fully litigate any discipline it believes was imposed without just cause.

The good news is that, in recent weeks, management has made several last-minute arbitration settlement offers and told the Union that upper management is reviewing the issue and process in hopes of improving it and breaking the arbitration logjam. Without meaningful change, that backlog will only worsen.

We hope to return soon to progressive, corrective discipline based on all seven tests of just cause: Fair Notice, Reasonable Rule, Proper Investigation, Fair Investigation, Substantial Proof, Equal Treatment, and Proportional Discipline. Until then, we will continue filing grievances and pursuing arbitrations to protect decades-long careers that Metro has discarded as casually as a used tissue. People’s livelihoods and lives are at stake. This is not a game, a debate, or a challenge to win for its own sake.

## Operator Shield/Partition Update

As of 9/20/26, 80% of Metro’s Excelsior coaches have the new operator partition. Installation on the remaining fleets will begin soon, and Metro still expects the entire fleet to be completed by the end of next March, if not sooner.

Metro is working with bus manufacturers to create a glass panel that fills the gap between the new barrier and the windshield. A few prototypes have been installed for initial testing. Next, Metro will install them on several coaches at each base so operators can test them and provide feedback. VM is also addressing lock issues and testing solutions for doors that close too hard because of their weight.

For all safety and design concerns, it is critical to submit an SSaM—not a BO report. A BO report is more appropriate when a partition has a mechanical problem that needs repair, rather than a design concern.

## A break-language win for ASM service on the G-Line

Beginning with the spring 2027 service change, Metro will implement ASM/Fallback operation. G-Line operators assigned to this ASM service will not have fixed break times on their run cards. Instead, they will receive guaranteed breaks or layovers after each revenue trip; these are breaks, not recovery time.

ASM G-Line operators will be guaranteed at least 12 minutes or 18% of their scheduled trip time—whichever is greater—after every trip. Shifts longer than eight hours will include either two 15-minute breaks or one 30-minute break, while shifts under eight hours will include one 15-minute break. Look for the newly negotiated MOA on G-Line ASM breaks on the website or the Union’s Facebook page.

## We’ve been here before.

No one wants to reach agreement on an expired contract more than the bargaining team negotiating it. The entire bargaining unit is counting on its elected officers to deliver a contract worthy of its members’ sweat and sacrifice. None of that is lost on the bargaining team.

While each contract negotiation is different, historically the average KCM contract negotiations take around a year and a half over 60 bargaining sessions. If the current rate of movement in negotiations hold, we may well not get an agreement until around the end of the year. Regardless of what you’ve heard, no substantial loss or gain has yet been agreed to in contract or benefits bargaining. We’re holding the line while fighting for the gains that the membership expects and has instructed us to do.

Lately there has been calls for the “Special Meetings at Contract Expiration” that our bylaws call for upon the expiration of our contract. I don’t believe we’ve held one of those since the contract expired in November1990, 36 years ago. After that special meeting we still didn’t reach an agreement until January 1992, fourteen months after the contract expired, with the help of arbitration.

Your contract bargaining team knows fully what the membership expects and demands. We will have some informational membership meetings when we either have meaningful progress or an impasse to report.

Informational meetings and “Still no contract buttons” don’t settle contracts. Contracts this large and complex are settled by tedious and numerous bargaining sessions. If and when negotiations grind to a halt, reach impasse, or are being bargained for in bad faith, this Union will act. For now, we plow on with the tedious grind. Remaining unified and patient throughout our long and difficult KCM contract negotiations empowers your contract negotiators to hold the line and fight for what’s rightfully yours. When we fight, we win!
