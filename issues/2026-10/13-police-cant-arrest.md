title: "Police Can't Arrest Their Way Out of a Housing and Mental Health Crisis"
author: "Chuck Lare, Shop Steward and a Base Safety Committee Chair"

Route #7, 8:40 a.m. A man is down in the doorway. Forty riders are watching while you radio TCC and handle it alone — again. Bus drivers see people in psychosis, overdosing, sleeping rough, breaking windows, scared out of their minds. It's not fine. Nobody who's actually stood in that doorway thinks it's fine.

So I understand why "just arrest them" sounds like the obvious answer. It feels fast. It feels like something is finally being done. But here's the problem: it doesn't work, and it costs more than the alternative.

Jail removes a person from a bus stop for a few hours or days. Then they come back — to the same street, the same route, the same untreated crisis. We've all watched the tents move down the block and thought: here we go again. That's not a public safety strategy. That's a very expensive game of whack-a-mole, and taxpayers are footing the bill for every round.

The number that should change your mind: Washington's Department of Corrections puts institutional incarceration at roughly $81,000 per person, per year — and that's before police response, court costs, jail health care, and the ER visits that follow. A Central Florida study found cycling a homeless person through jails and emergency rooms costs about $31,000 a year, versus roughly $10,000 for supportive housing. Denver's own randomized housing study found $6,876 in lower public costs per person, per year, after housing was factored in — fewer arrests, fewer jail stays, fewer ER runs. This isn't a mercy argument. It's a math argument.

And it's been tested at scale, not just in theory. Denver's STAR program sends trained health responders — not police — to mental health and low-level crisis calls. Stanford researchers checked for the exact "whack-a-mole" effect skeptics worry about: did crime just move somewhere else? It didn't. Targeted low-level offenses dropped 34%, with no offsetting rise elsewhere — crime even dropped during hours STAR wasn't on duty, because people who got real help stopped cycling back into crisis.

Houston ran the experiment for over a decade, regionwide. Starting in 2011, the city paired aggressive Housing First placement with continued enforcement against holdout encampments — not just kindness, but kindness plus consequences where needed. The result: homelessness down 63% since 2011, over 25,000 people housed, 90% still housed two years later. Houston is now America's fourth-largest city and has cut homelessness harder than any other major city in the country.

None of this means abandoning enforcement. If someone is violent, armed, or making credible threats, call police — full stop. That's not up for debate. But for the rider who's sick, high, confused, or sleeping at the end of the line and posing no threat, sending an armed response over and over is neither cheap nor effective. It's the response that "feels tough" while quietly failing everyone, including the taxpayer.

Seattle has a real opening right now. The mayor and county executive have announced steps to reset the regional homelessness response, with a workgroup meeting November 30. That's the moment to lock in what actually worked elsewhere: mandatory-track treatment options (not just optional ones), funding parity for behavioral-health responders instead of default police dispatch, and a direct crisis-responder line operators can call separately from 911.

ATU 587 needs to require Metro provide a simple reporting system that works for operators and inserts accountability through a simple real time live dashboard — tracking response times, repeat incidents, and operator emergency calls, so results are visible, not just promised. And we need ATU 587 to maintain a constant dialog at all Bases that facilitates two-way communications feedback to the working groups, so if the city's numbers look better but our incident reports don't, we have the evidence to prove problems are just moving, not shrinking.

Operators see this crisis up close every single shift. That's expertise the workgroups need at the table — not silence, while the details get decided without us.

Text your union representative this week: tell them you want ATU 587 to have a seat at the Breaking the Cycle and other such workgroups.
