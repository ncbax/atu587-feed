Solidarity is a simple word for a simple idea: we are stronger together than we are alone. It means standing with a coworker even when the fight isn't yours, showing up on the picket line for members of another union, and understanding that an injury to one is an injury to all. It is not a slogan. It is a daily practice, and this month I got to watch it work.

## What I Saw This Month

In Seattle, UNITE HERE Local 8 just settled the longest hotel workers' strike in Washington state history. After 85 days on the picket line, hotel workers at the Embassy Suites in Pioneer Square reached a tentative agreement that delivers real, lasting gains. Eighty-five days is not a headline number to me; it's eighty-five mornings of workers choosing each other over a paycheck, eighty-five days of coworkers, community members, and fellow unions showing up to hold the line. ATU 587 was one of the unions that Paul Neil, Bill Clifford, and I walked with in solidarity. I showed up, I stood on the line, and I watched a group of workers prove that solidarity, sustained long enough, wins.

That is what solidarity looks like from the outside. Here is what it looks like from the inside, in my own local.

## Who I Stand With

ATU 587, to me, is not one workplace or one job title. I know it as Clallam Transit, Jefferson Transit, Kitsap Transit, Solid Ground Transportation, and King County Metro. I know it as the local chapter officially organized and chartered on April 8, 1912, to represent streetcar operators working for the privately owned Seattle Electric Company. I think of the mechanics and facilities workers who keep the fleet running. I'm proud to have been a bus operator for 22 years, keeping the system moving alongside them. I think of our Customer Information Specialist, Pass Sales Office Employees, Supervisors in Service Quality, Dispatch, Control Center, and Training. Every one of those roles is different, every one of them is essential, and every one of them is represented under the same union. Today, ATU 587 has more than 5,000 active members — a historic high for us.

That size and that diversity is exactly the point. A local this broad, spanning this many agencies and job classifications, only holds together if its members choose to stand with one another.

## Two Questions Worth Asking Yourself

Last year, Mike Haggard, a senior organizer with the ATU International, trained and mentored me, and he put a choice in front of all of us plainly. His first question: what will you ever get from management, on your own, without a union behind you? The answer is nothing but a hard time. His second question: what will you get? The answer is whatever you're willing to take.

That second answer is the harder one to sit with, because it isn't about management at all — it's about you. Contracts, protections, and respect on the job are not handed to you out of goodwill. They are built, negotiated, and defended by workers who are organized enough to demand them and united enough to hold the line when it matters. What you're willing to accept is what you'll get. That's true whether you're negotiating a contract or deciding whether to show up for a picket line three counties away.

## What You're Owed

A union rests on three pillars: wages, benefits, and seniority. Seniority binds you to every other member instead of leaving you to compete for the same job. It rewards your commitment over favoritism and gives you, whether you're a new hire or a thirty-year veteran, a clear, fair, and predictable path. Because of that structure, you don't deal with pay partiality. Your job group is paid equally across the board, not based on who you know or how long you've kept your head down. That's not an accident. It's the product of a union insisting, contract after contract, that fairness isn't negotiable.

## Why Unity Matters

People at the top benefit from division. A workforce split by job title, agency, classification, or grievance is easier to manage and easier to shortchange. A workforce that sees itself as one union- mechanics standing with operators, dispatchers standing with facilities staff, one agency standing with another is a workforce that's much harder to push around. We are stronger the more we support each other, and weaker every time we let ourselves be divided.

This is the same commitment every ATU 587 member makes formally, in the Oath of Obligation taken upon joining: to promote the best interests of the union, never to cross a picket line, to keep confidences, to never knowingly let a fellow member be wronged, and to be considerate of others without discrimination of any kind. It's a promise to look out for people we may never have met, in job classifications we may never have worked, because they'd do the same for us.

## The Takeaway

Solidarity isn't a slogan. It's the reason eighty-five days on a picket line in Pioneer Square ended in a contract instead of a concession. It's the reason 5,000 members across five agencies and a dozen job classifications can call themselves one union instead of a dozen separate workplaces. And it's the reason the answer to "what will you get?" is always up to us, never up to them.
