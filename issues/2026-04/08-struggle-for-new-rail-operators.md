author: Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs, ATU 587/King County Labor Delegate

Rail has been growing quickly, and the demand for manpower continues to increase. As service expands and more trains are added to the system, the need for qualified Light Rail Operators grows with it. In many cases, the shortage has reached a point where supervisors are stepping in to operate trains themselves just to maintain service levels. That alone shows how much pressure the system is under.

To address the shortage, rail has accelerated hiring and is currently running two training classes per day to bring in new operators as quickly as possible. On paper, that sounds like progress. But the reality on the ground is more complicated. The pace of hiring has increased, yet the training environment has become far more difficult for students who are trying to learn the job.

The mainline is already heavily congested. With trains running on roughly a four-minute headway, there is very little room for error and very little opportunity for trainees to practice in a controlled environment. New LRO students are trying to learn a complex job while operating in one of the busiest conditions the system has ever seen. Every movement on the line must stay on schedule, and controllers are focused on keeping service moving. That pressure naturally carries over to the trainers and the students.

For trainees, this can create a stressful learning environment. Instead of having the space and time to develop confidence behind the controls, many students feel rushed. Trainers are balancing two responsibilities at the same time: teaching a new operator while also making sure the train stays on schedule and does not disrupt the flow of service. When the system is running at maximum capacity, training can easily take a back seat to keeping the line moving.

The impact of these conditions can be seen in the graduation numbers. Success rates vary widely from class to class. In some classes of 11 to 15 students, only four or five make it through to graduation. In other classes with similar numbers, 11 or even 12 students complete the program successfully. When outcomes swing that dramatically, it raises legitimate questions about consistency in training, evaluation standards, and the environment students are learning in. It also raises the question of whether students are being given a fair and equal opportunity to succeed.

Another concern is the reduction in on-the-job training hours. In the past, trainees received about 80 hours of hands-on operating experience before completing the program. Today that number has been reduced to 64 hours. That means new operators are getting less real-world practice at a time when the system itself has become more demanding and more congested. Those lost hours matter. Operating a train safely and confidently takes repetition, experience, and time behind the controls.

Learning the system involves much more than just moving a train from one station to another. Students must learn signal systems, station procedures, communication protocols, passenger safety, incident response, and how to operate in unusual or emergency situations. These are skills that develop through practice, not just classroom instruction. When hands-on training time is reduced, students may graduate with less confidence and less experience than operators in the past.

At the same time, trainers are under pressure to move classes through the system quickly in order to fill staffing gaps. That pressure can create uneven expectations and evaluations. Some trainers may allow students more time to develop their skills, while others may apply stricter standards under tighter timelines. The result is inconsistency from class to class.

If rail service continues to grow, training must grow with it. Expanding service without strengthening the training structure will only create more pressure on new operators, trainers, and the system as a whole. Adequate hands-on training time, consistent evaluation standards, and a training environment that allows students to learn without excessive operational pressure are essential.

Rail operators carry enormous responsibility. They move thousands of passengers every day and operate within a system where safety and precision are critical. Preparing them properly is not optional. It is an investment in safety, reliability, and the long-term success of the rail system. When new operators are given the time, support, and environment they need to succeed, the entire system benefits.

## Representation Must Keep Pace with a Changing Transit System

Congratulations to Rail and the smaller properties for having the option to elect a Vice President Three dedicated to your needs, while Vice President One focuses fully on Transit Operators. That structure creates balance and gives different parts of our membership the representation they need. In many ways, this is a win for everyone.

Over the last few weeks there has been a lot of discussion about the proposal to establish a Vice President position focused on operations, particularly rail. Members have raised questions about cost, voting eligibility, and whether the position is necessary. These are fair questions, and every member deserves clear information before casting their vote.

But before we get lost in the details, it helps to step back and look at the bigger picture. Our union has grown and changed, and our leadership structure must evolve with it.

ATU Local 587 represents workers across many different properties and systems. Our membership includes King County Metro bus operators, Link Light Rail operators, Streetcar workers, and members from Kitsap Transit, Jefferson Transit, Clallam Transit, and other agencies across the region. Each of these systems operates under different contracts, rules, and workplace conditions.

One area in particular has grown rapidly over the past decade: rail. Link Light Rail continues to expand across the region. Lines from Lynnwood, to Eastside, and Federal Way. With each expansion comes more operators, controllers, maintenance staff, and supervisors. Rail is no longer a small part of our union. It is a major and growing division within our membership.

Rail operations also come with technical challenges that are very different from bus operations. Rail members work under a separate collective bargaining agreement and operate in an environment with its own safety systems and procedures.

These are not abstract concerns. They are real situations that affect real members. As rail continues to grow, so does the number of grievances, investigations, and disciplinary cases involving rail operators and other rail employees. Expecting the same leadership structure that existed years ago to keep up with a rapidly expanding rail system is becoming increasingly difficult.

This is why many members believed the time has come to establish a Vice President position dedicated to rail operations. The goal is not to divide the union or create unnecessary positions. The goal is to ensure that members working in a complex and growing system have representation from leadership that understands their work environment, while allowing other union officers to focus on the needs of the rest of the membership. Strong unions adjust when the industry changes. Our transit system is changing, and our union must be able to keep up.

Some members have raised concerns about the cost of adding another position. Those concerns are understandable. But representation always requires resources, and the cost of inadequate representation can be far greater. When a member faces discipline, contract violations, or unfair treatment, having leadership that understands the job can make the difference between protecting a career and losing it.

Others have pointed out that the change would not take effect until 2027 and therefore does not solve immediate problems. But structural decisions are always made with the future in mind. The rail system will continue expanding for years to come. Preparing now ensures that the union is ready to represent the workforce that is still growing.

At the same time, this conversation should not turn into a conflict between different parts of our membership. Bus operators, rail operators, streetcar employees, and members at other properties all face different challenges. Our strength as a union comes from recognizing those differences while standing together.
