Ihave spent enough time inside this system to know what it is supposed to be: precise, accountable, and fair. That is the foundation accident preventability was built on, grounded in National Safety Council standards and reinforced through decades of policy meant to correct behavior, not punish it. But what I have been seeing lately does not always match that promise. At times, it feels like the narrative is no longer guided by facts, but by something else entirely, something closer to fiction than reality.

Nearly twenty years ago, when I was operating out of North Base, I was accused of striking a truck while merging onto I 5 in the early morning hours. By midday, the decision had already been made preventable. Case closed.

But it was not closed. With guidance from a seasoned shop steward, Neal Safron, I returned to the scene with a Polaroid camera and a measuring tape. I was not there to argue. I was there to document. What I found told a different story. The damage on the bus sat nearly three feet higher than where the truck’s mirror could have made contact. Even after factoring in tire height variations for that vehicle model, the conclusion became unavoidable: the narrative did not hold up.

That ruling was overturned. That experience stayed with me because it proved something essential. The system works when facts are allowed to lead. It also showed how quickly a conclusion can be written before the facts are fully understood.

In my current role overseeing accident rereads and arbitrations for the Union, I am seeing a troubling pattern. Since stepping into this position, I was informed that we have experienced an 84 percent turnover rate. That level of turnover does not happen in isolation. It raises a necessary question: are we applying policy in a way that aligns with its intent? Too often, the answer appears to be no.

I see small errors turning into major consequences. Preventable designations are issued without full context. Point totals escalate in ways that feel mechanical rather than deliberate. With the matrix, fifteen points become twenty four, and suddenly a career is on the line.

I have seen tires at the end of their lifecycle unzip at worn seams and be labeled preventable. I have seen coaches, physically unable to move, still deemed preventable when another vehicle struck them. I have seen inconsistencies in accident probation, where lower seniority employees are granted second chances while higher seniority operators with decades of service are not.

And then there is the math. You are told this system is objective, built on clear thresholds and consistent application. But when a Safety Quality report explicitly states, “no visible injury,” and two points for visible injury are still added, the numbers begin to tell a different story. When a 15,000 dollar vehicle damage claim is treated as if it crosses a 28,000 dollar threshold, triggering additional points, the calculation no longer reflects reality.

Somehow, two plus two becomes eight.

At that point, it is no longer just a miscalculation. It is a narrative being imposed on the facts rather than drawn from them.

In Stranger Than Fiction, the main character begins to hear a voice narrating his life, dictating outcomes before they happen. He initially assumes the system around him is fixed and inevitable. As the story unfolds, it becomes clear that the narrative itself can be challenged, and that it is not beyond correction.

That is where you are now. You are told the system is straightforward: resolve issues at the lowest level, keep safety and discipline separate, keep safety and base chiefs separate, and focus on correction rather than punishment. On paper, that looks simple. In practice, that simplicity often breaks down. Outcomes drift. Decisions lose connection to context. The line between what happened and how it is recorded starts to blur.

There are moments when the system corrects itself. I served as the partisan arbitrator in the Ben DeMonte arbitration last year, where termination was pursued despite the absence of a fatality or external claim. The arbitrator rejected that approach and reinforced a key principle: the most severe discipline must be reserved for the most severe circumstances. The result was reinstatement with back pay.

That decision made something clear. The system is not beyond repair, but it does not correct itself automatically.

We now have to decide what kind of system we are operating in. We can continue to rely on a framework that is sound on paper but inconsistently applied, or we can insist that its principles are followed in practice.

Ican’t speak for every operator at King County Metro; that would be presumptuous, but I can speak for myself. And if others feel the same after reading this, then maybe these words will finally give voice to a gratitude many of us have carried quietly for far too long. Because before any bus rolls out of a base, before a single passenger steps aboard, before an operator even clocks in, there is an entire workforce behind the scenes holding this system together with skill, sweat, and pride. Maintenance, Facilities, every crew who cleans, repairs, inspects, welds, replaces, rebuilds, and responds, you are the backbone of this operation, the reason any of us can do our jobs at all.

People see operators every day. They hear our voices, watch us navigate traffic, and sometimes blame us for everything from late trips to the weather. But they rarely see you — the ones who keep these buses breathing. You’re out there in the cold, in the rain, in the feel disconnected from reality.

But when similar cases produce different results, when calculations become flexible, and when context is overlooked, confidence in the system erodes. People stop believing that decisions are anchored in facts. They start feeling like characters in someone else’s story, waiting for a conclusion that has already been written.

Like any story, this one is still being written. If we are not paying attention, it can drift into something unrecognizable, structured, documented, and official, yet just a little too strange to be true.

But it can also be corrected. That requires attention to detail, a willingness to question outcomes, and a commitment to ensuring that decisions reflect facts, not assumptions. It requires us to insist that accident preventability remain what it was meant to be: precise, accountable, and fair.

Because in transit, as in any system built on trust, accuracy is not optional. Fairness is not negotiable. And the line between reality and interpretation must remain clear.
