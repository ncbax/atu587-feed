---
title: "Good Investigations Identify Causes—Shortcuts Identify Culprits"
author: "Andy Price, Union Member"
pages: 9
---
Missed stops are not new. What appears to be new is the growing tendency to treat every missed-stop complaint as though the only question that matters is whether somebody can later be identified on a video.

That should concern every operator.

Many operators have noticed an increasing reliance on surveillance footage and a growing tendency to reduce investigations to a single question:

"Was someone standing there?"

If the answer is yes, the conclusion increasingly seems to be that the operator must have done something wrong.

But transit is not that simple.

A person visible on a paused video image reviewed later in an office is not necessarily visible to an operator approaching in real time while monitoring traffic, pedestrians, cyclists, mirrors, signals, and roadway hazards.

You cannot stop for what you cannot reasonably see.

Many Eastside operators know exactly what we're talking about. Some bus zones are hidden behind hedges. Some stop poles are placed far back on private-property setbacks. Some are poorly illuminated. Some are so rarely used that the only reason operators know they exist is because years of experience have taught them where they are.

If a stop can disappear behind a hedge, then the problem is bigger than the operator.

Operators are increasingly finding themselves disciplined for passing up passengers who made little or no effort to indicate they wanted service. Standing motionless in dark clothing, several feet back from the curb, looking at a phone, and giving no visible indication of intent may be enough for a surveillance camera to identify someone afterward. That does not necessarily mean the person was reasonably visible to an operator approaching in darkness.

Some passengers quite literally stand like statues in the dark and then, after the bus has gone by, become clearly visible on a paused video image viewed later in an office.

Management points to the video and says, "See, they were there."

But that is exactly the problem.

Article 3.8 was negotiated because surveillance footage was never intended to become the basis for routine minor discipline. Policy 15 exists because customer complaints are supposed to be investigated, not reduced to a freeze frame and a predetermined conclusion.

The increasing dependence on video may actually be narrowing management's vision rather than improving it.

Technology should assist investigations, not replace them.

Metro already has a customer complaint policy. Policy 15 exists for a reason. Complaints are supposed to be investigated. Customers are supposed to be contacted. Circumstances are supposed to be considered. The goal should be to understand why something happened and how to prevent it from happening again.

Instead, many operators are concerned that investigations increasingly begin and end with video review.

Ironically, this growing dependence on surveillance can undermine protections intended for both customers and operators. In the process of showing videos to operators, the identities of complaining customers can effectively become known, even though the complaint process was designed to protect those individuals.

Nobody benefits when policies designed to protect everyone are gradually ignored.

Across the transit industry, agencies recognize that visibility is a shared responsibility.

Island Transit instructs riders to stand where they can be seen, signal approaching buses, and even use flashlights during darkness or fog. Kitsap Transit encourages riders to wave, so operators know they intend to board.

Around the world, passengers are expected to make their intentions known. Anyone who has used transit systems in places like London understands this. Simply standing near a bus stop without making your intentions obvious can easily result in being passed by. That isn't poor service. It's common sense.

King County Metro already recognizes this principle in some locations. Passenger-activated flashing lights already exist at certain stops to alert approaching operators that someone is waiting. If these systems improve safety and reliability at some locations, why not expand them?

Why not trim vegetation more aggressively?

Why not relocate hidden stops?

Why not move stop poles to locations visible from the operator's seat?

Why not review locations generating repeated complaints?

Why not focus on prevention instead of discipline?

Assigning blame is easy.

Solving problems is harder.

Operators accept responsibility for doing our jobs professionally. We always have.

Management should accept responsibility for the system it creates.

Missed stops are not simply operator problems.

They are system problems.

And system problems require system solutions.

Operators are not asking for special treatment.

We are asking management to follow the contract, follow its own policies, and recognize that missed stops are rarely caused by one factor alone.

Good investigations identify causes.

Shortcuts identify culprits.

Those are not the same thing.
