---
title: "Letter to the Editor: A \"Break-Language Win\" — But What Did We Give Up?"
author: "Andy Price, Bellevue Base Shop Steward"
pages: 12, 13
---
Dear Editor,

In the October News Review, President Greg Woodfill announced what he described as "A break-language win for ASM service on the G-Line."

He reported that operators would receive guaranteed breaks after every revenue trip, with a minimum of 12 minutes or 18% of scheduled trip time, whichever is greater.

That sounds encouraging. But after examining the actual Memorandum of Agreement (MOA), I have some serious questions about what we've gained, what we've given up, and why Metro wanted these changes.

And there's another question: Why couldn't I ask about this agreement during questions on the President's Report when the agreement was expressly discussed in his published report?

## What the Agreement Actually Says

Our existing collective bargaining agreement, Section 15.3(I), requires guaranteed breaks and layovers to be printed on operators' run cards.

That's an important protection. It gives operators a written record against which their guaranteed rest periods can be checked.

The new G-Line MOA changes that arrangement for ASM operations. Instead of documenting guaranteed breaks on run cards in the existing manner, the agreement provides that they will be referenced in the Operations Manual.

Why remove an existing contractual documentation requirement without clearly explaining what equally enforceable protection replaces it?

At the October 3 membership meeting, Woodfill acknowledged that Metro approached the Union seeking changes to accommodate ASM operations. He explained that departure and break times under ASM are not fixed.

That explains Metro's desire for flexibility. It doesn't fully answer what operators receive in exchange for giving up an established documentation requirement.

And the agreement contains provisions deserving even closer scrutiny.

Operators arriving at an ASM terminal are required to immediately notify the on-duty supervisor when beginning a guaranteed 15-minute break.

Does that mean locating the supervisor? What happens if the supervisor is unavailable? Does the break begin upon arrival or only after notification?

The language doesn't clearly answer those questions.

But perhaps the most concerning provision states:

> "In an extreme emergency, Metro may use discretion to ensure functional service for customers while maintaining at least one hour of break time per eight hours."

Read that carefully.

The agreement establishes guaranteed breaks after every revenue trip, yet also provides management with discretion during an undefined "extreme emergency."

Who determines what constitutes such an emergency? Can ordinary service disruptions qualify? Can individual guaranteed breaks be postponed or shortened?

And what exactly counts toward that one hour of break time? Does it include recovery time or only actual guaranteed rest periods during which operators are relieved of duty?

An aggregate hour of break time over eight hours is not necessarily equivalent to guaranteed rest following each revenue trip.

The agreement doesn't expressly grant unlimited management discretion. But it does establish an exception whose limits and application deserve explanation.

A guaranteed break should not become discretionary simply because management encounters operational difficulties.

## Negotiating Changes During an Expired Contract

Our principal collective bargaining agreement expired October 31, 2025.

Yet this MOA expressly states that the parties intend to incorporate the new Section 15.3(W) into the successor agreement.

We're therefore negotiating changes to established rest-break provisions while our principal contract remains unresolved.

If this agreement represents a significant improvement, it should withstand careful questioning.

## Why Couldn't I Ask During the President's Report?

At the October 3 membership meeting, Woodfill completed his oral report and opened the floor for questions.

I attempted to ask about the G-Line MOA, which he had expressly discussed in his officially published President's Report.

Recording Secretary Latrel Gibson intervened, asserting that the written President's Report and Woodfill's oral presentation were separate. Because Woodfill hadn't mentioned the MOA orally, I was told to wait until New Business.

I had the News Review in my hand and pointed to the heading "The President's Report."

I asked where our bylaws established that restriction.

No governing provision was identified.

My initial motion to proceed to New Business died for lack of a second. Eventually, I was permitted to ask both questions.

Woodfill offered explanations, but important questions concerning documentation and the relationship between existing and new contractual protections remained unanswered.

## This Concerns Every Member

The President's Report has appeared prominently in the News Review for as long as I can remember. Frequently, the written report contains considerably more information than the President repeats orally.

Our membership also conducts business through several meetings during each monthly cycle. Oral presentations can vary, and another officer may preside when the President is absent.

Should members' opportunities to question the President's published report depend on what happens to be repeated aloud at the particular meeting they attend?

Article XI, Section 1 of Local 587's bylaws establishes Reports of Officers as part of the regular order of business and directs members to raise questions under the proper heading. Section 17 adopts Robert's Rules of Order for matters not otherwise covered.

I've found no provision stating that matters officially published in the President's Report are excluded from questions merely because the President didn't repeat them orally.

After the meeting, I asked Woodfill to identify the governing rule. I received no response identifying it.

I subsequently sought clarification through a formal appeal to the International. President Costa declined to consider the appeal on procedural grounds, noting that I eventually asked my questions under New Business.

But that doesn't resolve the central question.

May members question the President about matters expressly contained in his published President's Report when his report is opened for questions, even if those matters weren't repeated orally?

And returning to the G-Line agreement:

If this is a win for operators, why are existing documentation requirements being changed, why is supervisor notification being introduced, and why does the agreement give Metro emergency discretion over guaranteed breaks?

An agreement described as a victory should withstand questions about its actual language.

If Metro requested changes to established contractual protections, members deserve to know what changed, why it changed, and how their rights will be enforced.

And when the President publishes a report announcing those changes, members deserve a clear, consistent opportunity to ask questions about them.
