Every operator knows the feeling. Traffic backs up, the bus loads slowly, a wheelchair boards, and by the time you reach the outer terminal your recovery time is gone. Your break exists on paper, but the clock says go, "turn-and-burn." So you pull out and keep driving.

That is not a personal failing. It is a hazard built into the schedule.

## The Hazard Chain

1. **Schedule design:** recovery time is too short.
2. **Late running:** congestion, passenger loads, incidents, and construction eat the buffer.
3. **Missed breaks:** you arrive at the terminal with no time left, under pressure to leave.
4. **Fatigue builds:** sustained attention and workload without recovery wear you down.
5. **Impaired judgment:** reaction time, attention and decision-making suffer.
6. **Crash risk:** collisions, pedestrian strikes and passenger injuries become more likely.

## Metro Knows

Metro cannot claim surprise.

- CBA Article 15.3.L requires Metro to review routes showing "a pattern of insufficient break time" after operators file OSFRs (Green Sheets).
- The Transit Operator Manual tells operators to send DDU messages when taking guaranteed breaks, which acknowledges that breaks get missed.
- The labor agreement created a joint Committee to Address Employee Fatigue. Fatigue is a system-level safety issue, not an individual comfort complaint.
- FTA Safety Bulletin 26-01 put every transit agency on notice. It counted 133 fatigue-related safety events since 2014, 73 of them from 2022 to 2025.

The science is just as clear. NIOSH says fatigue slows reaction time, reduces concentration, and impairs judgment, and recommends brief rest breaks at least every two hours. FTA research describes transit driving as "active fatigue": dense traffic, a timed schedule, fares, and dispatch communication all at once. L&I policy recognizes that hurried restroom or food stops are not genuine rest, especially when the work demands constant vigilance.

The toll is also personal. A NIOSH-supported study of medical claims found depression in 16.2% of local passenger transit workers, the highest of 55 industries studied and about 55% above the 10.5% overall rate. Chronic time pressure, little recovery, and restricted restroom access wear on people. The fix is changing the work environment, not telling operators to cope better.

## Why Your Evidence is Needed

Metro may say this is only a contract issue. It isn't. Washington's safety law (WISHA, RCW 49.17.060) requires every employer, Metro included, to provide a workplace free from recognized hazards likely to cause serious injury or death. That duty exists regardless of the CBA. L&I/DOSH has authority to investigate whether Metro's scheduling and dispatch practices create a recognized fatigue hazard, and to require correction.

But investigators and Metro's own schedulers respond to documentation, not stories. We need a record showing where, when, and how often the schedule fails.

## What You Can Do Starting Your Next Shift

- File an OSFR (Green Sheet) every time a run does not give you the guaranteed break. The CBA says Metro will review them. Make them.
- Record the facts every time you arrive late at a terminal: date, route, block/run, terminal, scheduled and actual arrival, scheduled and actual departure, and minutes of break or layover lost.
- Note the cause: congestion, event traffic, construction, passenger incident, dispatch instruction.
- Note any pressure to leave early, and DDU messages tied to breaks.
- Know your entitlement: under Article 15.3.J, less than 7 minutes of layover in the previous two hours entitles you to a 13-minute break. Take it, and document what happens.
- Send copies to Chuck@lareassoc.com / text 206-465-1435 so patterns by route, run dates and time of day patterns of insufficient break time can be identified.

Please don't exaggerate or guess. Accurate, consistent records are the most powerful tool we have.

## Where This Goes

With the evidence by route, run, date, and time(s): Metro per CBA Article 15.3.L is required to address routes that have a pattern of insufficient break time. If routes are not fixed, and there is sufficient material evidence that an L&I Worker Rights Complaint and a WISHA safety hazard complaint may be in order. These would request an onsite inspection of Metro's scheduling, dispatch and break-management practices, including run cards, AVI data, OSFRs and dispatch logs. If investigators confirm a recognized hazard, L&I can require abatement/corrective action.

Operators are asked to drive safely, on time, through the worst traffic in the region, without the recovery the schedule promises. A bad schedule is a safety hazard for you, your passengers and everyone on the road.
