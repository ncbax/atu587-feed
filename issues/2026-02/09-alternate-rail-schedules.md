---
title: "Alternate Rail Schedules and Their Impact on Frontline Staff"
author: Barbara Pastores and Muhammad Ehsan (ATU 587 Schedulers)
pages: 7
---
For Operators, a schedule is not an abstract idea. It is the foundation of the workday. It determines when they work, when they rest, how they are paid, and how they plan life beyond the cab. To understand why recent disruptions have been so difficult, it is important to understand how Link Light Rail schedules are created, changed, and put into practice; and what can be done to improve the process with fairness, clarity, and compassion.

At its core, train run times are developed through engineering standards, field testing, safety requirements, and historical performance data. Run times define how long it should take a train to travel from one point to another under normal conditions. Sound Transit develops running times and shares them with King County Metro Scheduling staff as part of each service change package.

Using the service change package as a guide, Schedulers build vehicle schedules taking into consideration headways, terminal recovery time, and systemwide operating limits. Together, these elements form the underlying schedule, which becomes the backbone for Operator duties. Duties are then created to align with the current Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA), which sets rules for maximum spread, break frequences and durations, and pay protections. For planned service changes (Shakeups), this system provides predictability and fairness. However, things become far more complicated when the system is constantly changing during a service change.

Planned maintenance, infrastructure upgrades, and system expansion are necessary investments in a reliable future. However, each of these projects can require changes to service patterns. When any segment of rail network is affected, the underlying schedule shifts, and Operator duties shift with it.

These changes can escalate quickly. Single track operations extend run times. Track closures introduce bus bridges. A station closure may force a new fallback location with limited facilities. What may appear on paper as a two-hour closure late in the day can completely reshape an Operator's entire shift.

For Operators, these disruptions are not just operational. They are personal. Sudden duty changes can interfere with childcare plans, second jobs, and rest cycles. Repeated, short-notice adjustments create uncertainty and fatigue, especially when they occur back-to-back. Operators are not resisting change. They are absorbing it, often quietly and often at personal cost.

The real question is not whether disruptions will happen. They will. The question is how they are managed. There are clear steps that can lead to a better path forward.

First, project planning timelines that require schedule changes need clearer structure and expectations. The current Intergovernmental Agreement (IGA) and CBA do not include sufficient language addressing schedule changes during a service change. If scheduling receives change requests well in advance, modifications can be completed earlier, and impacts can be communicated sooner. Clear and timely communication allows Operators to plan, adjust, and make informed decisions.

Clear and timely communication also helps First Line staff prepare for the day of disruption. This can create opportunities for voluntary sign-ups, swaps, or other flexible solutions that reduce stress and confusion.

Second, there must be clearer definitions of what truly qualifies as an emergency. Not every disruption qualifies as an emergency. Clearly defining emergency work builds transparency and trust.

Third, emergency work must be fairly compensated. When genuine emergencies require short notice changes, Operators should be paid accordingly. Recognition in pay matters just as much as recognition in words.

Finally, the strength of the workforce lies in its union. When expectations, protections, and processes are clearly written into agreements, they are implemented. When they are not, work still gets done, often at the expense of frontline Operators and First-Line Supervisors. Putting solutions in writing is not about rigidity. It is about respect. It ensures consistency, accountability, and fairness, even during disruptions.

None of this should overshadow one essential truth. Operators and First Line Supervisors have performed extraordinarily well under extraordinarily difficult conditions. They have adapted to constant change, navigated unfamiliar service patterns, and delivered reliable service despite ongoing disruptions. Their professionalism has kept the system moving. They deserve not only gratitude, but systems and agreements that truly honor their effort.
