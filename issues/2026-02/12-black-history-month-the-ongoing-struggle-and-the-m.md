February is Black History Month, a time set aside to reflect on the history, struggles, and contributions of Black Americans. Each year, communities prepare events to honor this history, but Black History Month is not meant to be symbolic or ceremonial. It exists because the full story of this country has too often been ignored, distorted, or minimized. This month is a reminder that Black history is not a side chapter. It is foundational to the United States.

From slavery to segregation, from civil rights to labor movements, Black Americans have consistently pushed this nation toward its stated ideals, often at great personal cost. Progress has never come easily, and it has never come without resistance. Understanding this truth is essential, especially at a time when division, fear, and xenophobia are again shaping public discourse.

## Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.: Justice Beyond Words

Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. is often remembered through selective quotes, but his work was far more challenging than many are comfortable acknowledging. Dr. King believed that justice required structural change, not just goodwill. He understood that racism, poverty, and exploitation were connected, and that civil rights without economic justice would always be incomplete.

Dr. King stood with workers because he knew dignity at work was central to dignity in life. His support of the Memphis sanitation workers was not accidental. Those workers were demanding basic respect, safety, and fair pay. Dr. King saw their struggle as a continuation of the civil rights movement. He believed organized labor had a moral responsibility to fight injustice and that unity among working people was a powerful force for change.

Nonviolence, for Dr. King, was not weakness. It was discipline. It required courage to stand unarmed in the face of violence and to remain focused on justice rather than revenge. His message challenged systems, not just attitudes. That message remains relevant today.

## Malcolm X: Naming Reality and Reclaiming Dignity

Malcolm X spoke from lived experience shaped by poverty, incarceration, and constant exposure to racism. He rejected the idea that Black Americans should wait patiently while being denied basic rights. Malcolm X emphasized selfrespect, political awareness, and accountability. He insisted that people had the right to defend their humanity and reject narratives that portrayed them as inferior.

His speeches resonated because they named realities that were often dismissed or denied. Malcolm X challenged both white power structures and Black leaders who avoided hard truths. Over time, his worldview expanded through travel and reflection. He came to see racial oppression as part of a global system and emphasized solidarity among oppressed people worldwide.

Malcolm X’s evolution reminds us that growth matters. Learning does not weaken a movement. It strengthens it. His legacy teaches that justice begins with honesty, even when that honesty makes people uncomfortable.

## Abraham Lincoln, Emancipation, and the Limits of Law

Black History Month also requires honest reflection on America’s legal and political history. President Abraham Lincoln’s Emancipation Proclamation was a turning point in the Civil War. It declared freedom for enslaved people in Confederate states and redefined the moral purpose of the war. It also encouraged enslaved people to escape bondage and join the fight for their own liberation.

However, emancipation was not a single act of generosity. It was the result of pressure from abolitionists, resistance by enslaved people themselves, and the realities of war. The Thirteenth Amendment formally abolished slavery, but freedom did not translate into equality. Reconstruction was followed by segregation, racial terror, voter suppression, and economic exclusion that lasted for generations.

This history matters because it shows us that laws can open doors, but they do not automatically dismantle injustice. That work requires vigilance and continued struggle.

## Black History Month in the Face of Modern Xenophobia

Today, we are witnessing a rise in xenophobic attacks against immigrant communities. Immigrants are being blamed for economic instability, crime, and social change. This rhetoric is familiar. It mirrors the same language once used against Black Americans, Irish immigrants, Chinese laborers, and others deemed “undesirable.”

Xenophobia thrives when fear replaces facts. It turns neighbors into targets and workers into scapegoats. Immigrant families face harassment, profiling, and threats of deportation. Many live with constant anxiety, even when they are lawfully present or long-settled in this country.

These attacks often overlap with anti-Black racism and Islamophobia. People are targeted not just for where they come from, but for how they look, speak, or worship. This climate creates division among working people while shielding those truly responsible for inequality and exploitation.

History teaches us that fear is a political tool. When leaders

Afail to offer solutions, they offer enemies instead.

## Why This Moment Demands Solidarity

For working people and unions, Black History Month carries urgent relevance. Black workers and immigrant workers have always been essential to this country’s economy. They built infrastructure, staffed public services, and carried industries forward while often being denied fair wages, protections, and respect.

Division weakens collective power. Solidarity strengthens it. Dr. King warned that injustice anywhere threatens justice everywhere. Malcolm X demanded that people stop accepting mistreatment as normal. Their words speak directly to this moment.

Standing against xenophobia is not separate from honoring Black history. It is part of it. Both require rejecting narratives that dehumanize people and confronting systems that benefit from division.

## Carrying the History Forward

Black History Month is not about nostalgia. It is about responsibility. Remembering history means recognizing patterns and refusing to repeat them. It means speaking out when communities are targeted and standing with those facing fear and exclusion.

As we prepare Black History Month events, we should approach them with honesty and purpose. The goal is not comfort. It is clarity. The history we honor demands action, unity, and accountability.

Black history teaches us that progress is possible, but never guaranteed. It must be defended, renewed, and passed forward. February reminds us that the struggle for justice did not end in the past. It is happening now, and each of us has a role to play.
