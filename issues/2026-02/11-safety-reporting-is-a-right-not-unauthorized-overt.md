Safety in our workplace does not happen by accident. It happens because workers speak up, document hazards, and take the time to report conditions that could injure someone or worse. That process is not only encouraged — it is protected.

Under Washington State workplace safety law and our collective bargaining agreements, employees have the right to identify and report unsafe conditions without fear of retaliation. Joint health and safety committees exist for this very reason: to ensure hazards are found, documented, and corrected before they result in injuries. That protection matters, because safety reporting is real work.

## Safety reports take time — and that matters

Anyone who has completed a safety report knows it is not a casual exercise. It requires logging into a system, documenting conditions accurately, explaining why an issue matters, and sometimes identifying patterns across equipment or vehicles. This takes focus and time, and policy recognizes that reality.

For that reason, members are permitted to be compensated for the time it takes to complete legitimate safety documentation. This is not an abuse of the system — it is the system. A reporting structure that demands accuracy but refuses to acknowledge the time required to achieve it is fun-

## Why labels don’t change the facts

Calling safety-reporting time “unauthorized overtime” does not change what actually occurred: a worker identified safety deficiencies and documented them through an approved process. The law does not look favorably on attempts to discourage safety reporting through wordplay or after-thefact recharacterizations.

The real question is not how the time is labeled, but what the effect is.

If workers begin to believe that taking safety reporting seriously might expose them to discipline, fewer reports will be written. Hazards will go undocumented. Problems will spread quietly instead of being fixed early. That is not speculation — it is exactly why safety laws exist.

## The chilling effect is real

A workplace where people hesitate before reporting safety issues is a workplace heading in the wrong direction. Fear is the enemy of safety.

When even one worker is challenged, scrutinized, or disciplined for submitting a safety report in good faith, the message travels quickly. Others notice. Conversations happen. People begin to ask themselves whether it’s “worth it” to speak up.

That chilling effect doesn’t just harm individual workers — it undermines the entire safety culture.

## Your rights extend beyond the report

Members should know that their rights do not end once a report is submitted.

You have the right to: • Report unsafe conditions without retaliation

• Be compensated for authorized safety-reporting time

• Ask questions about safety policies

• Seek union representation if management challenges your actions

• File a grievance if safetyrelated rights are violated

Shop stewards play a critical role in this process. If management raises concerns about a safety report, that is precisely when members should involve their steward — not after the fact, and not alone.

## Safety reporting benefits everyone

Safety reporting is not about pointing fingers. It is about prevention. Every documented hazard is an opportunity to correct a problem before it causes harm. Every report strengthens the record and improves accountability.

Management often emphasizes the importance of collecting safety data. That data does not appear magically. It comes from workers who take the time to document what they see. Undermining those efforts — intentionally or not — defeats the very purpose of the system.

## Getting safety culture right

A healthy safety culture requires consistency. Workers must be confident that reporting hazards will be met with corrective action, not suspicion. Management must recognize that encouraging safety reporting means accepting the time and effort it requires.

Safety reporting is not insubordination.

It is not misconduct. And it is not “unauthorized overtime.”

It is a protected activity — and a responsibility we all share.
