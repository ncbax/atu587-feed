---
title: "Dangerous Rhetoric Has Real Consequences for Our Members"
author: "Hassannoor Aden, Minority Affairs Officer, ATU Local 587/King County Labor Delegate"
pages: 17
---
Recent remarks by President Trump attacking the Somali community and immigrants from Africa are not only offensive, but they are also dangerous. Referring to entire communities as “garbage” and labeling African nations with degrading language places minorities in a vulnerable position, especially those who work in public-facing jobs like transit.

Words from the President of the United States carry weight. They shape public behavior and set the tone for what some people believe is acceptable. When racist language comes from someone in such a powerful position, it can legitimize hatred and embolden individuals who already hold harmful views. For transit workers, this can translate into increased harassment, threats, and physical danger while on duty.

Many of our members spend their entire workday interacting with the public. Bus operators, rail operators, supervisors, and station staff do not have the option to walk away when tensions rise. When certain communities are singled out and dehumanized, our members from those communities often feel the impact first. They are visible, accessible, and exposed.

It is also important to be honest and acknowledge that these attitudes do not only exist outside our workplace. We have some members within our own union who share or excuse the same kinds of views expressed in the President’s remarks. That reality makes this issue even more serious, because harm does not always come from strangers.

This reminds me of an incident at South Base back in 2017, where one of our own members casually referred to coworkers during a conversation as a “fish market,” implying they were loud and smelly. Comments like that may be dismissed as jokes or casual remarks, but they carry racial and cultural meaning. They reduce people to stereotypes and create a hostile environment. That kind of language, even when spoken casually, reflects the same mindset behind national-level rhetoric we claim to oppose.

As someone from the Somali community, I know how painful and alarming this language can be. Many Somalis fled a brutal civil war and have lived through the consequences of dehumanization and feardriven politics. When leaders or coworkers use similar language, it reopens old wounds and reinforces a sense of being unwelcome or unsafe.

Our workforce at King County Metro and throughout the county reflects the diversity of the communities we serve. We work alongside immigrants, refugees, asylum seekers, permanent residents, and U.S. citizens. These members are not outsiders. They are essential workers who keep the system moving every day.

Racist rhetoric does not differentiate between immigration status or citizenship. It does not stop to ask who was born here and who was not. Once a community is labeled as inferior or “other,” everyone associated with it becomes vulnerable.

As a union, we have a responsibility to confront this honestly, both externally and internally. That means speaking out when public figures use language that puts our members at risk, and it also means addressing harmful behavior when it comes from within our own ranks. Respect on the job is not optional. It is a basic expectation.
