## Safety Culture and Building Trust

In September, I attended the **Safety Culture Training** in San Diego a four-day workshop designed to improve how organizations like King County Metro approach safety, communication, and inclusion. It was a very informative and engaging experience that reshaped how I think about safety and teamwork.

The training focused on **creating a culture of trust**, where both management and frontline employees feel respected and heard. It highlighted one of the biggest issues we face today the **gap between management and employees** when it comes to trust.

Too often, operators or supervisors who report safety concerns end up being treated as if they caused the problem, rather than being recognized for identifying a potential hazard. This reaction discourages open reporting, which in turn weakens safety across the system.

As someone who has worked at Metro for over a decade, I've seen how easily concerns can get lost in the layers of management from supervisors to chiefs, to managers, to directors. The more layers we have, the harder it becomes to get issues resolved promptly and fairly.

The training reminded me that a true **safety culture** depends on relationships built on respect and trust. Managers and supervisors should be seen as allies, not enforcers. When an operator speaks up about a safety issue, it should be viewed as a contribution to system improvement, not as a reason for punishment.

King County Metro's **Safety Policy** and **Just Culture Principles** support this approach, encouraging employees to report safety issues without fear of retaliation. But for this to work, it must be applied consistently across all levels of management.

When we invest in trust, both sides win operators feel valued and management gains valuable insight from the people who experience the work firsthand.

## Training Equity and Language Barriers

Another area that needs serious attention is **training equity**, especially for minority and bilingual employees. Over the past year, many have shared experiences of **unequal treatment during evaluations** including feeling misunderstood because English is not their first language.

Under **Article 2 of the ATU Local 587 Collective Bargaining Agreement (Non-Discrimination)** and **King County's Equal Employment Opportunity (EEO) Policy**, every employee has the right to fair training, evaluation, and promotional opportunities, regardless of language, origin, or background.

Yet, we continue to hear stories of unfair grading practices and bias. For bilingual employees, it's not about ability or knowledge, it's about communication. Some trainees have been told they "Failed" tasks when, in reality, there was a misunderstanding due to language interpretation or unclear instructions.

This issue also connects to religious accommodation. An operator had been asked to provide written authorization from HR before being allowed to pray during training hours. That requirement directly conflicts with **King County Metro's Religious Accommodation Policy** and federal law under **Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964**, which requires reasonable accommodation for religious practices.

At Metro, we have many Muslim employees who perform daily prayers. Their right to pray should not depend on an HR letter. Metro's policy already allows for religious accommodation unless it creates an undue hardship and taking five minutes for prayer does not meet that threshold.

Training environments should be **inclusive, supportive, and fair.** When an employee feels respected for who they are and what they believe, they perform better. ATU Local 587 will continue to advocate for equal access to training, transparent grading standards, and fair evaluation practices for all members, especially our multilingual and faith-based communities.

## Scheduling and Contract Compliance

As Link light rail continues to expand, the workload and complexity of scheduling have increased dramatically. We now have multiple service layers Federal Way extensions, Angle Lake turnback's, Pinehurst single tracking duties, and others each with unique reporting times, layovers, and route transitions.

While expansion is a positive step, it has also led to **frequent schedule adjustments**, sometimes communicated to operators on short notice. This has caused confusion and frustration among many rail operators.

The **ATU Local 587 Rail CBA, Article 20 (Picks and Assignments)** clearly states that any change to a picked assignment must be communicated at least **five (5) days in advance**. This rule exists to protect operators' personal time and ensure predictability in their schedules.

However, several operators have reported receiving notice of schedule changes while they were on vacation, out on leave, or during their regular days off sometimes with less than five days' notice. This not only violates the CBA but also disrupts work-life balance, which is a key value under **King County's Healthy Workplace Guidelines**.

Metro's success depends on clear communication and respect for the labor agreement. Scheduling adjustments should always follow the five-day rule unless there is a documented operational emergency. When management honors the contract, it strengthens morale and builds the mutual respect that keeps our service running smoothly.

## Moving Forward Together

As rail operations continue to grow, so must our commitment to fairness, inclusion, and safety. Every policy, every rule, and every action should reflect respect for the people who move this system every day, the operators, instructors, supervisors, and support staff.

We all want the same thing: a safe, respectful, and supportive workplace. That goal is only possible when everyone from the Director's office to the front line operates with trust and integrity.

The **Safety Culture Training** reminded me that a true culture of safety starts with listening, empathy, and fairness. Whether it's a safety report, a training challenge, or a scheduling issue, the first step is to treat every employee with dignity.

We are stronger when we stand together when we speak up for one another, support fair practices, and make sure every voice is heard. As ATU 587 members, that's not just our right, it's our responsibility.
