We work in a place where people are always reading...the route, the radio, people’s tone of voice, the look on someone’s face, and the silence that sometimes follows.

Sometimes this is where problems arise: less in what happened, and more in the stories we create in our minds about “what happened.”

When we don’t know why someone did something, our brains naturally try to fill in the blanks. This space, between what someone intended and what was received, has been called The Interpersonal Gap.

Usually, when we let our guesses and unverified assumptions run the show, we end up in what organizational psychologist Gervase Bushe calls interpersonal mush: the foggy, sticky, fear-fed mess where trust starts to break down and fester. The interpersonal mush erodes the kind of trust that is vital to an agency grounded in sustained partnership on behalf of all those we serve...including each other.

Here is what struck me when I first learned about this: the mush is not a malfunction, it's the normal default. It forms automatically every time human beings interact, because none of us can see inside another person’s mind. The question is never whether mush will form. It always will. The question is whether we have a way to notice when we're doing this, and clear it.

For a long time, this was a blind spot for me. I wonder how many of us are in the same boat.

Instructor lean toward a Supervisor and whisper while looking in their direction. The trainee’s heart sinks, and their mind goes immediately to the worst case scenario: “They’re talking about me and what I did wrong. I’m sure to get fired.”

What the Instructor is actually thinking: “They held it together through a difficult start. We should give them a later report time tomorrow.”

Same situation. Two completely different ideas about what’s happening. I have been on both sides of this one.

Now picture a vehicle maintenance Lead walking through the bay multiple times in an hour. No greeting. No stop. Just that slow, thinking face we all know so well. The mechanic asks herself: “I’m sure they’re watching me because they don’t trust my work.”

The Lead is actually buried in a parts delay, a schedule problem, and a vendor issue, trying to solve three fires with limited time and resources.

Same walk-by. Different story being fabricated.

Or consider a Superintendent who sends a vague email to a Chief at their base: “See me at 2:00 PM.” That is all it takes. The rest of the afternoon becomes a full internal trial. “I’m being called in for bad news. Maybe they’ve already decide d.”

The Superintendent wants to talk about a mentoring opportunity. They think you’d be good at it. No one does their best work while imagining the worst case scenario. One added sentence...“It’s a positive conversation, wanted to give you the heads up”...and the whole day changes. That is a leader who understands human nature and chooses to get ahead of the story before it starts. What if more of us did that for each other?

Here is the question that can be hard to sit with: how many times this month have you been the person someone else was building a story about, and you never knew it?

What if the worst version of you that lives in someone else’s head was created on a day you don’t even remember, and the story they made up about you isn’t true?

That is what makes the Interpersonal Gap relevant in our work and home lives. It doesn’t just happen to us, it happens by us. We are all doing this, every day, without realizing it. Every time we rush past someone without a greeting, make an expression we’re unaware of, send a vague message, or sit in silence when interacting with others, someone else’s story machine starts spinning.

A skeptic might say: “Sometimes the story IS right. Sometimes they ARE talking about you.”

This may very well be true. However, even then, the conversation is how you find out. This possibility of resolution isn’t available to anyone when we don’t sincerely inquire.

The clearing conversation is simpler than you think. We can name what we saw. Name the story we started telling ourselves. Then, ask a clear question: “I noticed you and the Supervisor talking while looking my way. The story I started telling myself was that I was in trouble. Is this true or is it something else?”

One sentence, and the chaos in our minds lifts.

Our roles in transit are integral to communication with others. We rely on kind, respectful communications that fortify trust and sustain partnership. A missed signal, a rough tone, a vague message...these things spread faster than good news, especially if nobody checks out the stories they’re making up about others, and then lives from that story as if it were the truth.

So here is an invitation: try it once this week (or more if you like!). Pick the smallest moment...someone’s look, a flippant comment, their tone... and instead of being absolutely sure of your story and keeping it to yourself, or trying to make sense of it with others, ask those involved. See if your assumption is correct. I’ve found it takes courage to do that. It may even surprise you.

So here is what I find myself wondering:

What would happen if we brought that same character to the stories we tell ourselves about each other? What would it look like if we chose to ask instead of assume? And what might we discover about ourselves and each other that the silence was never going to reveal?
