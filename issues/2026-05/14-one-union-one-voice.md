Who Are We? Unity Across the black communities. In recent years, as a minority affairs executive Board ATU587 I have seen something troubling: growing division within communities that have long shared common struggles. These divisions are especially visible within Black communities, where questions of identity, history, and belonging are being debated in ways that can either strengthen us or pull us apart.

As a first-generation immigrant, I have personally experienced moments where my place within Black spaces was questioned. I have been told that because my family was not part of the history of slavery in the United States, I could not fully belong in Black caucuses or conversations. That perspective, while rooted in real historical pain, risks overlooking a broader truth.

Our histories may be different, but they are deeply connected. In the United States, the Black experience has been shaped by slavery, Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and the long fight for civil rights. This struggle produced powerful leaders like Martin Luther King Jr., who called for unity through nonviolence and justice, and Malcolm X, who emphasized dignity, self-determination, and global awareness of Black struggles.

At the same time, across Africa and the Caribbean, nations were fighting their own battles against colonial rule. Countries like Ghana, led by Kwame Nkrumah, gained independence in 1957, while others followed throughout the 1960s. Leaders such as Julius Nyerere guided their people through liberation movements that challenged exploitation and demanded self-governance.

These movements were not isolated. They were connected by a shared desire for freedom, dignity, and self-determination. Martin Luther King Jr. himself attended Ghana's independence celebration, recognizing that the fight for justice in America and the fight for independence in Africa were part of the same global struggle.

This idea is rooted in Pan-Africanism, a philosophy advanced by thinkers like W. E. B. Du Bois, who believed that people of African descent, no matter where they are in the world, share a linked destiny.

So why the division today?

Part of it comes from the need to protect history. For many African Americans, identity is tied to a specific and painful legacy that must never be erased or diluted. That concern is valid.

At the same time, immigrants from Africa and the Caribbean bring their own histories of colonization, displacement, and struggle. Being excluded from Black spaces can feel like a continuation of the very marginalization we are all trying to overcome.

When we focus only on what separates us, we weaken our collective voice. When we recognize what connects us, we strengthen it.

The leaders we often look to for guidance understood this clearly: Martin Luther King Jr. spoke of the "Beloved Community," a vision where justice and equality are achieved through unity across differences. Malcolm X, especially later in his life, expanded his view to see the struggle for Black liberation as part of a global human rights movement. Shirley Chisholm built coalitions across race, gender, and background because she understood that real change requires solidarity.

None of them argued for division. They argued for expanding the circle while still honoring the unique histories within it.

Unity does not mean ignoring differences. It means respecting them while recognizing that our futures are tied together.

In the workplace, in our unions, and in our communities, unity has practical power. It strengthens our ability to advocate for fair treatment, equal opportunity, and protection from discrimination. It ensures that no group is isolated or left behind. It builds the kind of collective strength that institutions cannot ignore.

Division, on the other hand, weakens our influence and allows inequality to persist.

So the question "Who are we?" is not meant to divide us. It is meant to remind us.

We are descendants of resilience. We are products of different histories, but shared struggles.

We are stronger together than we are apart.

The path forward is not about choosing one identity over another. It is about building bridges between them. Because in the end, unity is not just an idea. It is a strategy for justice.

## Probationary Discharges and the Impact on Minority Workers

Article 4, Section 7 of our agreement states that employees who are discharged during their probationary period are not subject to the grievance and arbitration procedure. However, the language does provide one safeguard: upon request, an employee has the right to a termination review. This review must be requested within 15 days of notification, and Rail is required to schedule the review and respond to the Union in writing within a reasonable timeframe.

On paper, this may appear to strike a balance between management authority and employee rights. In practice, however, this language has had a disproportionate and concerning impact on minority employees, particularly within the training department.

As a representative in Minority Affairs, I have witnessed firsthand how this provision operates. It places probationary employees in a vulnerable position, where decisions about their future often rest on subjective evaluations rather than clear, consistent standards. Many members are released without being given a meaningful opportunity to improve, demonstrate their abilities, or fully learn the job.

There is a growing concern that this process has been inconsistently applied across training classes. Outcomes often vary depending on trainers, instructional approaches, and expectations. This inconsistency raises serious questions about fairness and equity, especially when patterns emerge showing that minority employees are disproportionately affected.

The situation also creates tension within Amalgamated Transit Union Local 587. The union represents both trainers and trainees, which can make accountability difficult and leave Minority Affairs caught in the middle. Despite this, it is our responsibility to speak up when we see patterns that undermine fairness and equal opportunity.

For many in our membership, particularly those of African descent and other underrepresented groups, the current process does not feel neutral. There is a growing perception that bias, whether intentional or not, is influencing outcomes. When individuals are judged as "not capable" without being given a fair chance to develop, it raises serious concerns about the integrity of the system.

The termination review process, while important, is limited in its effectiveness. It does not carry the same weight as a grievance or arbitration process, and in many cases, decisions are upheld without meaningful reconsideration. This leaves employees without a true path to challenge their discharge.

It is clear that this language needs to be revisited. We must ask whether it still serves the purpose of fairness, or whether it has become a tool that allows too much unchecked discretion. A system that gives management full control without strong accountability measures risks undermining trust across the workforce.

## Engaging in the Political Process and The Role of ATU-COPE

I had the privilege of serving on the Committee on Political Education (COPE), the political action arm of the Amalgamated Transit Union. This committee plays a critical role in connecting transit workers to the political decisions that shape our daily lives.

COPE is more than just a committee. It is the collective voice of transit workers, ATU-COPE ensures that working people have a seat at the table at every level of government.

By law, union dues cannot be used to fund political campaigns. Instead, ATU-COPE is powered by voluntary contributions from members who believe in advancing pro-labor and pro-transit leadership. These contributions help support candidates who understand and respect the needs of frontline workers. But the work does not stop after Election Day. ATU-COPE continues to engage with elected officials to make sure our members' interests are represented in policy decisions.

Many of our members hold strong and diverse views about politics. Some are skeptical of politicians altogether, while others fall on different sides of the political spectrum. Regardless of where we stand individually, one thing is clear: political decisions directly affect our jobs, our safety, and our communities. Choosing to disengage does not remove the impact, it only removes our voice.

We have seen firsthand how political races, such as the recent King County government executive election, can create division within our union. Endorsements are not always easy, and they can bring strong opinions. However, the purpose of COPE is not to divide, but to ensure that our collective interests are considered when policies are written and implemented.

During my time on the committee, I had the responsibility, as part of Minority Affairs, to ask critical questions of candidates seeking endorsement. These questions focused on issues that matter deeply to our members and our communities.

These conversations are not symbolic. They are essential. They help us understand how those seeking power intend to use it, and whether their values align with fairness, justice, and the well-being of working people.

ATU-COPE makes endorsements as needed each year, always with the goal of protecting and advancing the interests of our members. Participation in this process is one of the strongest tools we have as union members.

Being part of COPE reinforced something important: whether we engage or not, politics will always shape our workplace and our future. Through COPE, we choose to engage, to ask questions, and to make sure our voices are heard.
