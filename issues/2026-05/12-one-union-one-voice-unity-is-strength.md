Who Are We? Unity Across the black communities. In recent years, as a minority affairs executive Board ATU587 I have seen something troubling: growing division within communities that have long shared common struggles. These divisions are especially visible within Black communities, where questions of identity, history, and belonging are being debated in ways that can either strengthen us or pull us apart.

As a first-generation immigrant, I have personally experienced moments where my place within Black spaces was questioned. I have been told that because my family was not part of the history of slavery in the United States, I could not fully belong in Black caucuses or conversations. That perspective, while rooted in real historical pain, risks overlooking a broader truth.

Our histories may be different, but they are deeply connected. In the United States, the Black experience has been shaped by slavery, Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and the long fight for civil rights. This struggle produced powerful leaders like Martin Luther King Jr., who called for unity through nonviolence and justice, and Malcolm X, who emphasized dignity, self-determination, and global awareness of Black struggles.

These movements were not isolated. They were connected by a shared desire for freedom, dignity, and self-determination. Martin Luther King Jr. himself attended Ghana’s independence celebration, recognizing that the fight for justice in America and the fight for independence in Africa were part of the same global struggle.

This idea is rooted in Pan- Africanism, a philosophy advanced by thinkers like W. E. B. Du Bois, who believed that people of African descent, no matter where they are in the world, share a linked destiny.

So why the division today? Part of it comes from the need to protect history. For many African Americans, identity is tied to a specific and painful legacy that must never be erased or diluted. That concern is valid. At the same time, immigrants from Africa and the Caribbean bring their own histories of colonization, displacement, and struggle. Being excluded from Black spaces can feel like a continuation of the very marginalization we are all trying to overcome.

When we focus only on what separates us, we weaken our collective voice. When we recognize what connects us, we strengthen it.

Engaging in the Political Process and The Role of ATU-COPE

I had the privilege of serving on the Committee on Political Education (COPE), the political action arm of the Amalgamated Transit Union. This committee plays a critical role in connecting transit workers to the political decisions that shape our daily lives.

COPE is more than just a committee. It is the collective voice of transit workers, ATU- COPE ensures that working people have a seat at the table at every level of government.

By law, union dues cannot be used to fund political campaigns. Instead, ATU- COPE is powered by voluntary contributions from members who believe in advancing pro-labor and pro-transit leadership. These contributions help support candidates who understand and respect the needs of frontline workers. But the work does not stop after Election Day. ATU-COPE continues to engage with elected officials to make sure our members’ interests are represented in policy decisions.

Many of our members hold strong and diverse views about politics. Some are skeptical of politicians altogether, while others fall on different sides of the political spectrum. Regardless of where we stand individually, one thing is clear: political decisions directly affect our jobs, our safety, and our communities. Choosing to disengage does not remove the impact, it only removes our voice.

We have seen firsthand how political races, such as the recent King County government executive election, can create division within our union. Endorsements are not always easy, and they can bring strong opinions. However, the purpose of COPE is not to divide, but to ensure that our collective interests are considered when policies are written and implemented.

During my time on the committee, I had the responsibility, as part of Minority

Affairs, to ask critical questions of candidates seeking endorsement. These questions focused on issues that matter deeply to our members and our communities.

These conversations are not symbolic. They are essential. They help us understand how those seeking power intend to use it, and whether their values align with fairness, justice, and the well-being of working people.

ATU-COPE makes endorsements as needed each year, always with the goal of protecting and advancing the interests of our members. Participation in this process is one of the strongest tools we have as union members.

Being part of COPE reinforced something important: whether we engage or not, politics will always shape our workplace and our future. Through COPE, we choose to engage, to ask questions, and to make sure our voices are heard.
