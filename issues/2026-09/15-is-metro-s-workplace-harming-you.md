Workplace stress contributes to at least 120,000 deaths each year and up to $190 billion in health care costs, according to research by two Stanford professors and a former Stanford doctoral student now at Harvard Business School. (https:// www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/ why-your-workplace-might-bekilling-you)

If Metro were serious about managing operator health and controlling its own health care costs, it would look hard at the environment operators work in every day. Instead, operators think twice before calling the Transit Control Center for help, never knowing if the response will be dismissive or hostile.

Wellness programs urging employees to eat better or exercise are of marginal value when Metro ignores the workplace conditions driving the stress in the first place. As the Stanford researchers put it, “your tendency to overeat, overdrink, and take drugs”is shaped by your workplace. Metro management has it backwards — treating the symptoms of stress instead of the root cause: the environment itself. One researcher put it plainly: “Companies need to get serious about creating a workplace where people feel valued, trusted, and respected, where they are engaged.”

## The Screen vs. The Windshield

Inside Metro’s climatecontrolled offices, the language of the day is “Equity, Inclusion, and Belonging.” Dashboards, equity metrics, and mobilityframework mandates fill the screens of planners and policymakers. For the thousands of operators in the driver’s seat, that language hits a wall of reality at the dashboard.

Decision-makers watch operations through a screen, chasing “speed-ups” to keep buses on time. Operators watch it through a windshield — living Metro’s “turn-and-burn” culture: skipped breaks, threats of discipline for running late, and a fear of retaliation that suppresses reporting and keeps the metrics looking good. The result is a growing sentiment on the frontline: when it comes to Equity, Inclusion, and Belonging, operators are second-class.

For administrators, equity is data aggregation — tracked in spreadsheets, drafted in secure conference rooms. For the operator, equity is a daily, face-to-face negotiation with societal crises: regional housing shortages, untreated behavioral health emergencies, and systemic underinvestment, seen up close through the windshield. While the “screen class” enjoys hybrid schedules and administrative safety, the “windshield class” is tethered to rigid schedules and punitive management, absorbing public frustration with zero authority to change the systems they're forced to manage.

## Policy vs. Practice

Metro's lack of real EIB implementation for frontline staff shows up in three places:

• The Unsafe Schedule Trap. Administrators celebrate expanded route hours on their screens. But because of chronic labor shortages, that expansion is extracted straight from operators’ physical and mental well-being through inadequate recovery and break time. On a screen, more service looks like progress. Behind the wheel, it’s burnout — and unsafe conditions.

• The Safety Chasm. For years, bureaucratic inertia — if not outright ambivalence — has slowed deployment of operator security partitions and behavioral health specialists who could address root causes instead of just moving problems around. While administrators debate structural reform on paper, operators face rising exposure to real hazards. The murder of operator Shawn Yim, and the countless operators living with PTSD, expose a painful truth: frontline safety is treated as a logistical afterthought, not an urgent equity mandate.

• The Compliance Checklist. Metro has never provided operators with training suited to the war-zone conditions they face — nothing like the Red Kite Project. Instead, training is doled out as discipline, or comes from programs like AVADE, built by office workers for office workers, that fail to address the acute, high-stress, violent scenarios drivers face in real time. Real belonging means worker voices shape policy. Instead, operators remain passive recipients of administrative edicts.

## Dismantling the Second-Class Tier

If King County Metro wants to be a genuinely anti-racist, pro-equity organization, its leadership has to close the gap between the screen and the windshield. There is no real belonging while the workers who physically move this city feel expendable to the agency that employs them.

EIB in a transit agency has to start from the ground up, with accountability measured by those living the experience — prioritizing operators’ physical safety, mental health, and scheduling autonomy ahead of new administrative targets. Until what happens behind the windshield carries more weight than what shows up on the screen, Metro’s EIB framework is an incomplete promise — and its most vital frontline ambassadors will keep feeling like second-class citizens.
