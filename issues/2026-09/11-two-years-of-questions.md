August marks two years since serious concerns began to emerge about what appears to be a troubling pattern affecting minority candidates seeking to become successful train operators.

This is not simply about one employee, one training class, or one disciplinary decision. It is about a pattern that deserves to be examined openly, fairly, and without fear.

For many of our members, the path to becoming a train operator begins in the classroom. There, candidates are evaluated on their knowledge, rules, procedures, and ability to learn the technical requirements of the job. What is deeply concerning is the pattern we have witnessed involving minority trainees who perform successfully in the classroom, pass written examinations, and demonstrate that they understand the material only to encounter serious problems once hands-on training and practical evaluations begin.

When a trainee succeeds in the classroom but repeatedly fails at the point where hands-on evaluation begins, we have to ask an uncomfortable question:

***Are we measuring performance consistently, or are different standards being applied to different people?***

That question cannot simply be dismissed as criticism of management or training personnel. It is a legitimate question about fairness, transparency, and equal opportunity.

King County and Sound Transit have publicly committed themselves to principles of equal employment opportunity and nondiscrimination. Those principles must mean more than words in a policy manual or statements on a website. Equal opportunity must exist in the classroom, in practical training, during mainline qualification, and at the final step toward graduation.

Article R.4.7.C. of the Agreement clearly provides that employees discharged or removed during their probationary period are not subject to the grievance and arbitration procedures. However, it also explicitly guarantees those employees the right to request a termination review. The request must be made within 15 days of notification of discharge, after which RAIL is required to schedule the review and provide the UNION with a written response within a reasonable time.

What is deeply troubling is that training personnel have reportedly told members who are discharged during probation that they are entitled to the termination review, but that "nothing will change" and that their discharge will not be overturned. If this is being communicated to employees, it raises serious concerns about whether the termination-review process is being treated as a meaningful contractual right or merely as a procedural formality.

A contractual right should not be undermined before an employee even has the opportunity to exercise it. Telling employees in advance that the outcome is predetermined discourages them from pursuing their rights and creates the appearance that the review process is designed to protect the decision already made rather than fairly examine the circumstances surrounding the discharge.

Even more concerning are situations where employees and helpers have raised legitimate concerns about fairness, unequal treatment, or possible bias, yet those concerns appear to have been dismissed while the underlying decisions remain unchanged. When people who are expected to stand up for fairness knowingly remain silent in the face of potential injustice and inequality, it sends a particularly damaging message to minority employees.

This is why Article R4.7.C. must be taken seriously. The termination review should be an actual opportunity for the employee and the UNION to raise concerns, examine the circumstances, and determine whether the discharge was handled fairly and consistently not a process where employees are told from the beginning that nothing can or will change.

For minority employees, the issue goes beyond a single termination. When a pattern develops in which concerns are repeatedly raised but ignored, employees can reasonably begin to question whether they are receiving equal treatment and equal protection under the Agreement. Fairness cannot simply be a principle written into a contract; it must be demonstrated through the way employees are treated when they exercise their contractual rights.

## The Pattern Cannot Be Ignored

Over the past two years, minority trainees have raised concerns about being able to successfully complete the training process despite demonstrating that they possess the knowledge and skills necessary to advance.

The concern becomes particularly serious when a trainee repeatedly succeeds in academic evaluations but is ultimately deemed "unsafe or not meeting standard and denied the opportunity before On Job Training OJT."

Safety is not negotiable. Every operator must meet the same safety standards. But **safety must also be evaluated objectively, consistently, and according to clearly established criteria.**

If one employee is considered qualified after making certain mistakes while another employee is removed from training for comparable conduct, the union has a responsibility to ask why.

If the same performance is acceptable for one trainee but unacceptable for another, we need to know what changed.

And if a trainee is told that the issue is "performance concerns," only to later have the allegation characterized as "cheating" or another form of misconduct, the employee deserves a clear explanation and a fair opportunity to defend themselves.

## The case of Sheikh Mohamed (not his real name)

The case of Sheikh Mohamed illustrates why these questions cannot simply be ignored.

Sheikh Mohamed successfully completed his examinations and progressed through mainline training. By all appearances, he had reached the final stages of becoming a qualified rail operator.

Yet instead of graduating, he was removed from the process and denied his rail qualification and certificate based initially on allegations described as "performance concerns."

After concerns were raised about the decision, the characterization of the allegations reportedly changed to "cheating," which carries a substantially different implication and can expose an employee to allegations of misconduct.

This raises fundamental questions.

1. What exactly was the original allegation? And What evidence supported it?
2. Why did the characterization change? And Was Sheik Mohamed given a meaningful opportunity to respond to the allegations?
3. Were other trainees evaluated under the same standard? And were comparable cases reviewed?

And most importantly, **would the same decision have been made if the employee were not a member of a minority community?**

These are not unreasonable questions. They are the questions a union should be asking whenever an employee's career is placed in jeopardy.

## History Teaches Us Why We Must Pay Attention

America has a long history of discrimination that was once defended as normal procedure.

People were denied housing because of the color of their skin. People were denied employment opportunities because of race. Communities were excluded from opportunities that others took for granted. Over time, many of those practices became recognized for what they were: discrimination. The lesson from that history is not that every adverse employment decision is automatically discrimination. The lesson is that patterns of unequal outcomes deserve scrutiny, particularly when the people affected repeatedly come from the same protected communities.

We cannot tell workers in 2026 that equal opportunity exists simply because a policy says it exists. We must be able to demonstrate it.

## Equal Opportunity Must Be Measurable

If King County and Sound Transit are serious about their commitments to equal employment opportunity, then the agencies should welcome an objective examination of training outcomes.

The union should be asking for meaningful data, including:

1. How many trainees entered the program during the past two years? And how many successfully graduated?
2. How many were removed from training? And at what stage were trainees removed?
3. How many were removed during classroom training versus practical or hands-on training?
4. What were the documented reasons for removal? And Were minority trainees disproportionately removed during practical evaluations?
5. Were the same standards applied consistently across all trainees? And how are allegations of unsafe/not meet standard performance documented?
6. What appeal or review process is available to trainees? And Who independently reviews disputed training decisions?

These questions are not an attack on safety.

## The Responsibility of Minority Affairs

As Minority Affairs, I have repeatedly raised concerns about the experiences of minority employees in transit. I do not raise these concerns because I want different standards for minority employees.

I raise them because **minority employees deserve the same standards, the same opportunities, the same due process, and the same chance to succeed as everyone else**.

That distinction is important.

Equality does not mean giving someone a shortcut. Equality means making sure nobody is placed on a harder road because of who they are.

For two years, we have watched this issue develop. We have heard concerns from employees and observed outcomes that demand further examination.

**Equal opportunity is not a slogan. It is a standard that must be lived, measured, and defended.**
