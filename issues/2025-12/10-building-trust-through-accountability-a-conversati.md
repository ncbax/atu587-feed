Recently, I had a candid conversation with a King County Metro operations chief that got me thinking deeply about accountability, trust, and how we show up for each other in this work. What struck me most was their genuine care—for us as operators, for the riding public, and for creating a workplace where everyone can do their best.

They shared frustrations about moments when drivers seem defensive about policy violations or push back on feedback. But underneath that frustration, I heard something else: a leader who truly wants to support us, who understands the complexity of what we do, and who feels caught in the middle trying to balance accountability with advocacy. It made me ask myself some hard questions. And I think they're worth asking together.

## Where Accountability Meets Trust

Here's the thing: accountability isn't about catching people doing something wrong. It's about all of us taking ownership of our impact—even when that impact is unintended. When we roll through a stop, miss a call-out, or respond curtly to a passenger on a rough day, we might have reasons. Valid ones. But those actions still ripple outward—affecting riders who depend on us, coworkers who cover for us, and supervisors who field complaints while wanting to have our backs. The question isn't whether we make mistakes. We all do. The question is: can we stay open to hearing about them?

## The Defensiveness Trap

Defensiveness is human. When someone approaches us about our performance, especially if we're already stressed, tired, or feeling undervalued, our first instinct might be to protect ourselves. We might hear criticism even when someone's offering feedback. We might assume they're building a case against us rather than trying to help us grow.

But here's what I'm learning: when we lead with defensiveness, we make it harder for people to support us. We shut down conversations before they can become collaborative. We miss opportunities to share our perspective, explain context, or find solutions together. What if, instead, we got really curious? 'Help me understand what you saw' or 'What would you have done in that situation?'

## One Goal

Feedback comes from everywhere—peers, passengers, supervisors, shop stewards, even our families. Each source offers a different lens on how we're showing up. Our coworkers see our professionalism. Riders experience our customer service. Supervisors track our compliance. Our loved ones notice when work stress follows us home.

None of these voices alone tells the whole story. But together, they help us see our blind spots and build on our strengths.

The operators I most respect aren't perfect—they're the ones who can hear hard things without crumbling, who admit mistakes without shame, and who ask for support when they need it.

## Bridging the Trust Gap

I get it. When a supervisor wants to "hear your side," it can feel like a setup. Sometimes past experiences make us skeptical. Sometimes the power dynamic makes us guarded. But most of the time, they genuinely want context. They want to understand what happened so they can advocate effectively—for us and for the system we all serve.

We can help bridge this gap by:

• Sharing facts clearly without minimizing or over-explaining

• Acknowledging impact even when our intent was good

• Remembering that asking for support isn't weakness—it's how we all get better

• Recognizing that middle managers care too—often more than we realize

## Looking Ahead

Here's what this conversation sparked for me: what if we had access to someone truly neutral when things get difficult? Not management, not union, not HR,—just a trained professional whose only job is to help people work through conflict. They could facilitate conversations between operators, between us and management, even between supervisors and chiefs - and much more. Help us navigate misunderstandings before they escalate, stress us out, and become grievances. Give us a place to turn when we need support that doesn't fit the boxes we currently have. It's worth exploring. Because ultimately, we're all on the same team. We all want safe service, fair treatment, and a workplace where people can thrive.

Look, accountability isn't about never messing up. It's about owning our impact, staying open when feedback is hard to hear, and remembering that the culture we experience is the culture we create—together, one interaction at a time.
